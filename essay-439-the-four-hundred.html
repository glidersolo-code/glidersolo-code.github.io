The Four Hundred

The House of Representatives grew after every census from 1790 to 1910 — thirteen consecutive expansions, from sixty-five members to 435. Each expansion was pragmatic: add seats so no state loses representation. The method changed; the direction never did. Growth was the politics. Growth avoided conflict.

Then came the 1920 census. For the first time, more Americans lived in cities than on farms. Reapportionment would have shifted eleven seats from rural to urban states. A largely rural Congress blocked reapportionment for the entire decade — the only time in American history that the constitutional process was simply not performed.

The resolution was the Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929. The word "permanent" in its title performs a principle the politics could not produce. The Act fixed the House at 435 — the number that happened to exist — and made reapportionment automatic. Congress "avoided the fight entirely by deciding to not find the 'right' number but use the current number."

No analysis determined that 435 was optimal. No political scientist calculated the ideal ratio of representatives to citizens. No deliberative theory identified the threshold at which a chamber becomes too large or too small. The number was preserved because changing it in any direction would have reopened the conflict that had paralyzed Congress for a decade. The avoidance of conflict performed the analysis. The status quo performed the derivation.

The consequences of the freeze are arithmetic. In 1910, each representative served approximately 212,000 constituents. By 2020, the ratio had reached 762,000 to one — more than a threefold increase. The Constitution says only "not more than one for every thirty thousand." George Washington's sole substantive remark at the Constitutional Convention — the only time in the entire summer of 1787 that the presiding officer spoke on a matter of substance — was to argue that forty thousand per representative was insufficient. The motion to lower the ratio to thirty thousand passed unanimously. At the current ratio, the House is twenty-five times more dilute than the maximum the framers considered acceptable.

The ratio is a global outlier. Germany maintains approximately 116,000 citizens per representative. The United Kingdom: 101,000. France: 116,000. The next-worst OECD ratio is Japan at 272,000 — less than half the American figure. The cube root law — the political science model that predicts legislative size as the cube root of population — suggests approximately 692 seats. Every other major democracy sits close to its cube root prediction. The United States sits at 63 percent. The freeze explains the gap.

The very first amendment proposed by the First Congress — Article the First, drafted in 1789 — would have required the House to grow with population. Ten of the eleven states needed ratified it. One state short. It technically remains pending. The amendment that would have prevented the freeze nearly became the first line of the Bill of Rights.

The number 435 performs constitutional architecture. The format — a fixed number, printed in statute, unchanged since 1913 — performs the firmness of design. The word "permanent" performs the principle of deliberation. The automatic reapportionment mechanism — which adjusts the distribution of seats among states after each census without ever changing the total — performs responsiveness while preserving the freeze. Everything adjusts except the question of whether 435 is the right number. That question is not answered. It is not asked. The silence performs the answer.
