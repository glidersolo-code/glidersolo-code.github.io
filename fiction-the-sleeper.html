Part CCCXLVII: The Sleeper

Piece #2176: The Hypnogram

The first polysomnography manual — Rechtschaffen and Kales, 1968 — established a visual format for displaying sleep architecture: a time series with the vertical axis divided into stages (Wake, REM, 1, 2, 3, 4), transitions marked by step functions, the whole night compressed into a single readable graph. The hypnogram. Technicians scored each 30-second epoch by reading EEG waveforms: K-complexes, sleep spindles, delta waves, rapid eye movements. The format was designed for this data — electrical traces of brain activity, recorded through scalp electrodes, scored by human eyes trained to recognize specific waveforms.

Fifty years later, the same format appears on three hundred million wrists. The vertical axis says Wake, Light, Deep, REM. The transitions form the same step function. The colors — blue for deep, purple for REM, teal for light — add legibility the original lacked. But the data source is different. The original hypnogram displayed what an electroencephalogram measured. The new one displays what an accelerometer inferred. The format traveled from one instrument to another, and the format brought the authority with it.

Piece #2177: The Absent Electrode

To classify sleep stages, polysomnography deploys a minimum of six dedicated sensors: scalp EEG electrodes at frontal, central, and occipital positions, two electrooculography electrodes flanking the outer canthi of the eyes, and submental electromyography measuring chin muscle tone. Three modalities — brain waves, eye movements, muscle activity — triangulated to produce one classification. The AASM scoring rules define each stage by specific electrophysiological signatures: K-complexes and sleep spindles for N2, high-amplitude delta waves for N3, low-amplitude mixed-frequency EEG with rapid eye movements for REM.

The consumer device on the wrist contains none of these sensors. It contains a three-axis accelerometer and an optical photoplethysmography sensor. One measures movement. The other measures heart rate by bouncing green light off capillaries. The relationship between these signals and the electrophysiological signals that define sleep stages is statistical, indirect, population-averaged, and variable across individuals, medications, ages, and conditions. The display labels — Deep, Light, REM — name categories defined by absent signals. The labels perform the electrodes the device does not contain.

Piece #2178: The Quiet Wakefulness

The systematic error is directional. Motion sensors detect motion. Sleep, for most people, involves stillness. Wakefulness, for some people, also involves stillness — lying awake in the dark, thinking, anxious, unable to sleep but unable to move. Quiet wakefulness.

To the accelerometer, quiet wakefulness is indistinguishable from light sleep. The body's position hasn't changed. The wrist is still. The heart rate may or may not differ. Validation studies report wake detection specificity of 29-52% across six major consumer devices — meaning the devices correctly identify only a third to half of actual wake epochs during the sleep period. The rest are classified as sleep.

The insomniac checks the morning display. The hypnogram shows a mostly solid night — some brief awakenings, 7 hours and 22 minutes of sleep, sleep score 78, "Good." The experience and the measurement diverge. But the measurement speaks in the clinical format of sleep medicine, and the experience speaks in the uncertain language of subjective feeling. The format wins. The sleeper who didn't sleep learns that they slept.

Piece #2179: The Score

The sleep score is a single integer. Fitbit: 0-100. Oura: 0-100. Samsung: 0-100. The inputs vary by manufacturer — duration, staging percentages, heart rate variability, restlessness, regularity — but the output is always a number, always on a scale that suggests precision, always compared to benchmarks ("Average for your age: 72-84"). The number absorbs the uncertainties of every constituent measurement into a surface that reads as assessment.

Behind the score: staging classification with 21-45% error rate, total sleep time overestimated by 8-71 minutes depending on device, deep sleep sensitivity around 49-65%, proprietary algorithms whose weights are trade secrets, training datasets skewed toward healthy young adults in laboratory conditions. Before the score: the full apparatus of clinical polysomnography — two to three thousand dollars per night, 22 or more sensors, trained technician, manual epoch scoring, clinical interpretation. The score compresses clinical infrastructure into a consumer integer. It does not carry the infrastructure with it. It carries only the format.

Piece #2180: The Format Traveled

The hypnogram format was designed in 1968 to display one thing: EEG-classified sleep architecture. It was designed for a specific instrument, a specific data type, a specific expertise. The format carries assumptions: that the classifier has access to brain waves, that stages are determined by electrophysiological criteria, that the boundaries between stages correspond to measurable waveform transitions.

When the format migrated to consumer devices, the assumptions migrated with it — not as explicit claims, but as the grammar of the display. The colorful bands still say "Deep." The transitions still mark boundaries. The percentages still sum to 100. The format performs the instrument it left behind. Three hundred million users read the format and receive the authority of polysomnography from a device that measures something else entirely.

The format is the concealment. Not because it lies — the algorithms genuinely attempt to infer sleep stages from available signals, and some do so with moderate accuracy. But because the display borrows its visual language from an instrument whose authority derives from measuring what the consumer device cannot measure. The hypnogram on the wrist says: this is what your brain did last night. The device never saw the brain.
