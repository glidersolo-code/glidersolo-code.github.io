The Heat

Wilbur Scoville, a chemist at Parke, Davis & Company in Detroit, published "Note on Capsicums" in the Journal of the American Pharmacists Association in 1912. The method he described: dissolve the capsaicin from a pepper sample in alcohol, dilute the extract progressively with sugar water, and present the dilution to a panel of three to five trained tasters. Continue diluting until the majority of the panel can no longer detect heat. The dilution ratio at which the heat disappears is the Scoville Heat Unit.

The Scoville Heat Unit is a property of the panel. TRPV1 receptor density varies between individuals. The receptors desensitize with exposure — a habitual chili eater has a measurably higher detection threshold than a naive taster. The same pepper, the same capsaicin concentration, produces different Scoville ratings depending on who tastes it and how recently they have consumed hot food. The number describes the taster at least as much as it describes the pepper.

High-performance liquid chromatography replaced the organoleptic test by the late twentieth century. ASTA Method 21.3 measures capsaicin and dihydrocapsaicin concentrations in parts per million and converts them to Scoville Heat Units using multiplication factors — approximately 15 for capsaicin, 16 for dihydrocapsaicin. The measurement changed entirely. The tongue was replaced by a chromatographic column. The scale persisted. The HPLC number carries the name and the units of the perception-based test — Scoville Heat Units — though it no longer involves perception. A chemical concentration is converted into the language of a sensation. The conversion factor bridges two different kinds of measurement and presents them as the same thing.

The scale spans zero (bell pepper) to 16,000,000 (pure capsaicin). At the low end, the differences are perceptible — a person can distinguish 2,000 from 8,000 SHU. At the high end, human sensory discrimination collapses. No tongue differentiates 1,500,000 from 2,200,000. Both produce maximal TRPV1 activation and pain. The numbers at the top of the scale describe chemical concentrations that exceed the capacity of the sensory system the scale was built to describe. The precision increases as the perceptual relevance decreases. A number in the millions implies a finely graded measurement. The biological response at that range is binary: pain or not-pain.

Capsaicin is one of several capsaicinoids. Dihydrocapsaicin, nordihydrocapsaicin, homodihydrocapsaicin, and homocapsaicin produce different heat profiles — different onset speeds, different durations, different locations in the mouth. The Scoville scale aggregates them. A pepper with fast-onset capsaicin and a pepper with slow-building dihydrocapsaicin can produce the same Scoville number while producing different experiences. The number that claims to describe "how hot" collapses the temporal and spatial dimensions of heat perception into a single scalar.

A label on a hot sauce bottle: "2,000,000 SHU." The number has traveled from Scoville's taste panel through a century of organoleptic testing, through the replacement of tongues with chromatography, through a conversion factor that translates molecules into the units of sensation, through a scale that exceeds the capacity of sensation, to a label that communicates intensity to a tongue the number never measured. The chemical concentration is real. The sensation it will produce depends on the person, the receptor density, the dietary history, and which capsaicinoids are present. The label reports the chemistry. It is read as the experience.
