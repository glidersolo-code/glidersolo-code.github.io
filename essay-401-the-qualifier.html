The Qualifier

The word is "qualified." It means: limited, conditional, subject to exceptions. The immunity has boundaries. There are cases where it does not apply. The word performs limitation — the doctrine, the word promises, is not absolute.

In 1967, the word was accurate. Pierson v. Ray: the Supreme Court introduced a defense for officers who sincerely believed their conduct was lawful. Good faith. Subjective. The immunity was qualified by the officer's state of mind — what the officer thought, what the officer intended. The word "qualified" described a real limitation on a real defense: prove your mental state, or lose the protection.

In 1982, Harlow v. Fitzgerald eliminated the subjective element entirely. Good faith became irrelevant. The doctrine now protects even malicious conduct, as long as no prior court decision specifically deemed the conduct unconstitutional in a nearly identical factual scenario. The qualification was removed. The word "qualified" remained. The word now describes the doctrine's discarded origin, not its current function.

The "clearly established" standard, as refined in Anderson v. Creighton (1987), requires plaintiffs to identify a prior case where an officer violated the same right in nearly the same way under nearly the same circumstances. The right cannot be defined "at a high level of generality." Officers released a police dog on a man who had surrendered by sitting with his hands raised; immunity was granted because the closest precedent involved a suspect who had surrendered by lying down (Baxter v. Bracey). Officers executing a search warrant allegedly stole $225,000 in cash and rare coins; immunity was granted because no prior case in that circuit held that police stealing during a search violated the Fourth Amendment (Jessop v. City of Fresno, 2019). An officer shot a stationary, non-threatening woman four times; immunity was granted (Kisela v. Hughes, 2018). Justice Sotomayor's dissent: the doctrine "transforms into an absolute shield," telling officers "they can shoot first and think later."

The circularity is the deepest structure. In Pearson v. Callahan (2009), the Court held that judges may skip the constitutional question. Under the prior rule — Saucier v. Katz (2001) — courts had to decide first whether the conduct was unconstitutional, then whether the law was clearly established. Even if immunity was granted, the constitutional ruling created precedent for future cases. Pearson eliminated this requirement. Courts now routinely dismiss on "clearly established" grounds without ever ruling on the constitutional question. If the court never rules, no new precedent is created. If no precedent is created, the law remains not clearly established. If the law remains not clearly established, the next identical case also receives immunity. The doctrine prevents the creation of the very case law it requires. Each grant of immunity ensures the next grant of immunity.

The statute the doctrine undermines — Section 1983, the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871 — was enacted during Reconstruction to combat state violence against Black citizens. The text: any state actor who violates federal constitutional rights "shall be liable to the party injured." No qualifications. No immunity. Nearly one hundred years later, the Supreme Court invented, without statutory authorization, a doctrine that shields the officials the statute was designed to reach. Reuters found that police won 57% of qualified immunity claims from 2017 to 2019, up from 44% in the three years ending 2007 — a 13-percentage-point increase. The Fifth Circuit granted immunity in 64% of cases; the Ninth Circuit in 42%. Constitutional rights vary by geography. The standard is called "clearly established." The outcomes suggest nothing is clear, nothing is established, and nothing is qualified about the immunity the word "qualified" describes.
