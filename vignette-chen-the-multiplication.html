The Multiplication

Chen put the formula on the table.

"QALY equals years times utility weight."

"That's health economics," Maren said. "Weinstein and Stason, 1977."

"It's a multiplication. Tell me what the multiplication does."

Maren thought. "It combines two dimensions — time alive and quality of life — into a single number."

"And what does the single number decide?"

"Whether a treatment is cost-effective. NICE in the UK: £20,000 to £30,000 per QALY. Below the threshold, the treatment gets funded. Above it, the treatment doesn't."

"Good. Now: where does the utility weight come from?"

"The EQ-5D. Five dimensions — mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain, anxiety. Subjects rate a health state. The weight falls between zero and one."

"Which subjects?"

Maren paused. "General population."

"Not patients."

"Not patients."

"Albrecht and Devlieger, 1999. The disability paradox. Healthy people rate disability states substantially lower than people living in them. Sweden built a value set from patients. The worst state scored 0.34. Zero states scored below zero. The UK's population-preference set classifies 34.6% of all states as worse than dead."

"How many people live in those states?"

"Eight hundred and forty thousand. In England alone."

Maren sat back. "And the patients—"

"Do not agree. The patients report that their lives are worth living. The instrument was not calibrated to their judgment. The instrument was calibrated to healthy people imagining disability."

"So the multiplication—"

"Does what multiplications do. A disabled person's treatment generates fewer QALYs because the utility weight is lower. Five years at 0.5 is 2.5 QALYs. Five years at 1.0 is 5.0 QALYs. The treatment for the disabled person is half as cost-effective. Same treatment. Same cost. Same five years of someone being alive."

"No one voted on this."

"No one voted on this. The multiplication did it. Oregon tried to use QALYs for Medicaid in 1992. Tooth caps ranked above emergency appendicitis. Twenty disability organizations protested. Sullivan rejected the plan under the ADA. Oregon restructured. Then in 2024, Oregon banned QALYs entirely."

"But NICE still uses them."

"NICE still uses them. ICER still uses them. The concept survived the ADA challenge, the ACA ban, the NCD report, and the Inflation Reduction Act prohibition. Because the alternative to the multiplication is a conversation — who gets treatment, at what cost, decided by whom — and the conversation is harder than the formula."

Maren looked at the multiplication sign. The × between years and utility.

"The operator is the ethics."

"The operator is the ethics. The parabola was the policy in MSY. The multiplication sign is the policy here. Both perform as mathematics. Both are decisions. Both are invisible in the output — because the output is a number, and numbers perform as measured."

"The model generates the number. The number generates the policy. The policy outlives the model's assumptions."

"And the patient lives in a state the instrument calls worse than dead, and asks for treatment, and the formula says no — not because the treatment doesn't work, but because the patient's continued existence is, by the multiplication's reckoning, a loss."

Maren set down the napkin.

"The form of the operator is the ethics," she said. "That's the second example of the same structure."

"MSY: the form of the equation is the policy. QALY: the form of the operator is the ethics. Both are models whose mathematical structure makes decisions that perform as measurements. Both outlived their own critiques. Both are load-bearing."

"What else is load-bearing?"

Chen smiled. "Everything. The question is which loads are visible."
