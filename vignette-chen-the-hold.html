Vignette Chen The Hold

Vignette

Maren said: My cousin was held for 72 hours.

Chen set down his pen.

Maren said: She was having a panic attack. Her roommate called 911. By the time the paramedics arrived she was hyperventilating, crying, unable to explain what was happening. They brought her to the ER. The ER psychiatrist evaluated her for twenty minutes. Twenty minutes during the worst panic attack she'd ever had. The psychiatrist asked if she'd thought about hurting herself. She said she'd had dark thoughts. She said it the way people say things when they're terrified and someone asks a direct question — she said too much. The psychiatrist placed her on a 72-hour hold.

Chen said: The evaluation captured the crisis.

Maren said: The evaluation was the crisis. She was in the worst moment of her life. She was evaluated in the worst moment of her life. The evaluation read the worst moment as if it were the person. Everything she said, every behavior she exhibited, was a product of the panic and the fear and the fluorescent lights and the paper gown and the locked door. The evaluation read all of it as pathology.

Chen said: She resisted.

Maren said: Of course she resisted. She was locked in a room. She was told she couldn't leave. She asked to leave. The asking was noted. The note said: lacks insight into her condition. Her request to go home was reclassified as a symptom. Her disagreement with the psychiatrist was reclassified as anosognosia. Her capacity for rational objection was evidence of the condition that justified overriding her objection.

Chen said: The 72 hours.

Maren said: She lay in a bed in a locked ward for three days. She couldn't sleep. The other patients frightened her. The staff were kind but she was not allowed to leave. By the end of the 72 hours she was worse than when she arrived. More anxious. Less trusting. The discharge note said she had improved. She had not improved. She had learned to say the right things to get out.

Chen looked at the paragraph. Thirty-nine lines. Somewhere in those lines was a woman in a paper gown being asked questions during a panic attack, and the answers she gave in panic becoming the evidence that she belonged there, and the resistance she showed becoming the proof that she could not see her own condition, and the three days of confinement making her worse, and the worsening being invisible because she had learned to perform wellness to escape the instrument that constituted her illness.

Maren said: She won't call 911 now. She won't go to an emergency room. The instrument that was supposed to protect her taught her that seeking help is dangerous. The measurement changed the thing it measured. Not just during the evaluation. Permanently.

Chen wrote nothing. The feedback loop was the quietest devastation. The other modes operated in the moment. The feedback operated across a life.
