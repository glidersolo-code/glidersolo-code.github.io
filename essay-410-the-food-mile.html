The Food Mile

Tim Lang coined the phrase in 1994 at the SAFE Alliance in London, intending language that "encapsulated the complexities of distance, energy, culture and trade" in terms ordinary people could find meaningful. The phrase succeeded beyond its author's design. "Food miles" entered policy documents, consumer campaigns, supermarket labeling, and the vocabulary of environmental consciousness. The metric is intuitive: food travels from farm to plate, the distance is measurable, and shorter is better. Closer is kinder. Local is responsible. The moral geometry of proximity.

The geometry is wrong. Weber and Matthews (2008, Environmental Science & Technology) analyzed the complete supply chain of U.S. household food consumption — 8.1 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per year per household — and found that production accounts for 83% of the total greenhouse gas emissions. All transportation combined: 11%. Final delivery from producer to retail: 4%. The remaining 6%: processing, packaging, and retail. The variable the consumer optimizes — distance — captures at most one-ninth of the footprint. The variable that dominates — what was grown and how it was grown — has no label, no number, no phrase.

The counterintuitive cases are not edge cases. A tomato grown in a heated greenhouse in England produces three times the emissions of a field-grown tomato shipped from Spain. Heating energy accounts for 56.8% of on-farm cumulative energy demand; the truck across a continent is comparatively trivial. New Zealand grass-fed lamb, shipped 12,000 miles by container vessel, carries a lower carbon footprint than American lamb raised on supplemental grain — year-round pasture and mild climate eliminate the inputs that dominate production-phase emissions, and ocean freight is the most carbon-efficient transport mode per kilogram-mile. Regional hydroponic lettuce grown 30 miles from the consumer generates twenty times the emissions of California field-grown lettuce shipped 3,000 miles — electricity for lighting, cooling, ventilation, and pumping overwhelms the lifecycle. In each case, the legible variable (distance) and the dominant variable (production method) point in opposite directions. The consumer who follows the legible variable increases emissions.

Poore and Nemecek (2018, Science) synthesized data from 38,700 commercially viable farms across 119 countries producing 40 food products. Impact varied fifty-fold among producers of the same product. Dietary composition dominated environmental impact across all product categories. Transport remained a minor contributor. The finding extends Weber and Matthews across the global food system: what matters is the production, not the journey.

Weber and Matthews calculated the substitution comparison directly: replacing less than one day per week's worth of red meat and dairy with chicken, fish, eggs, or plant-based alternatives reduces household food emissions more than switching to 100% locally sourced food for every meal. The dietary shift addresses the 83%. Buying local addresses the 4%. The smaller intervention produces the larger reduction because it acts on the dominant variable.

The metric persists because legibility is its own force. "Miles" is the most intuitive unit available. Everyone knows what a mile is. The moral geometry — shorter is better, closer is kinder — maps directly onto spatial intuition. Lifecycle analysis, the method that reveals the 83% vs. 11% split, requires expertise, modeling, data, and tolerance for counterintuitive results. The British greenhouse tomato is worse. The New Zealand lamb is better. These findings violate the proximity heuristic. Accepting them requires the consumer to replace a legible metric with an illegible one — to trade a number on a label for a system of analysis that has no label, no number, and no phrase that encapsulates its complexities in terms ordinary people can find meaningful.

No such phrase appeared. "Food miles" held the field. The variable that accounts for 4% became the metric. The variable that accounts for 83% remains unnumbered, unlabeled, and largely invisible on the shelf. The distance traveled from farm to plate is printed, calculated, celebrated, and minimized. The emissions generated in the field, the greenhouse, the feedlot, and the processing facility are nowhere on the package. Legibility is its own argument. The number that exists displaces the number that matters.
