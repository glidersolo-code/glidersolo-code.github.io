Fiction The Denominator

Fiction

Part CCLXXXIII: The Denominator

Piece #1856: The Two Numbers

Thomas writes them on the whiteboard. The same clinical trial. The same 43,548 participants. The same virus. The same endpoint.

95%.

0.71%.

He steps back. Both numbers are correct. Both describe the same dataset. The first is the relative risk reduction: 1 minus the ratio of infection rates. The second is the absolute risk reduction: the difference between infection rates. The first divides. The second subtracts. The first reached every headline. The second required a commentary in The Lancet Microbe to surface.

Thomas draws a line between them. The line is a division sign. Everything above it is 95%. Everything below it — the denominator, the baseline risk — was divided away. With the denominator went the information that tells you whether the vaccine prevented 475 cases per thousand or 8 cases per thousand. Both yield 95%. The number cannot tell them apart.

Piece #1857: The Reversal

Thomas puts the table on the whiteboard. Five vaccines. Two columns.

Pfizer: 95% relative, 0.84% absolute. Moderna: 94% relative, 1.2% absolute. AstraZeneca: 67% relative, 1.3% absolute. Johnson & Johnson: 67% relative, 1.2% absolute.

He stares at it. By relative risk reduction, Pfizer is the best vaccine and AstraZeneca is the worst. By absolute risk reduction, AstraZeneca is the best vaccine and Pfizer is the worst. The rankings are inverted. The same trial data, the same vaccines, two columns — and the hierarchy reverses depending on which column gets the headline.

The reason is the denominator. Pfizer's trial had a low background infection rate (0.9%). AstraZeneca's trial had a higher one (1.9%). When you divide by a smaller denominator, you get a larger ratio. The vaccine that looked best by ratio looked best because its trial happened to have fewer infections in the placebo group. The denominator — the thing the division removed — was the thing that determined the ranking.

Piece #1858: The Swing

Thomas reads Schwartz and Woloshin's study. A hypothetical cholesterol drug. Relative risk reduction: 34%. Absolute risk reduction: 1.4%.

Show only the relative number: 88% of subjects would take the drug. Show the absolute number: 42% would take it.

Thomas writes: 46 percentage points. Nearly half the sample switched their medical decision based on which arithmetic they received. Not which drug. Not which side effect. Not which evidence. Which arithmetic.

The arithmetic is a frame. The frame is transparent to the person who computed it. The frame is invisible to the person who reads only the result. The denominator — the baseline risk, which determines whether 34% means preventing 340 cases per thousand or 14 cases per thousand — was divided out before the number reached the reader.

Piece #1859: The Drug Facts Box

Thomas holds a mock-up of Schwartz and Woloshin's proposed Drug Facts Box. Two columns. One shows the drug group. One shows the placebo group. Both columns display event rates. Side by side.

No ratio. No division. No denominator removed. The reader sees both numbers and can compute both the relative and absolute effects. The frame that conceals is replaced by the frame that reveals.

The FDA Risk Communication Advisory Committee endorsed it unanimously. Congress directed the FDA to consider it under Section 3507 of the Affordable Care Act.

Lisa Schwartz died in 2018. The box has not become mandatory.

Thomas sets down the mock-up. A tool designed to make the denominator visible. Endorsed by the committee. Directed by the legislature. Not required by the regulator. The denominator remains divided away.

Piece #1860: The Division

Thomas returns to the whiteboard. He erases everything except the division sign.

The sign is the concealment. Not the format — the format is a percentage, and percentages are appropriate for rates. Not the precision — the numbers are precise and correctly computed. Not the dimensions — there is one dimension, efficacy, measured one way. The concealment is the mathematical operation. Division by baseline risk inflates the result in exact proportion to how low the baseline risk was. The lower the baseline, the higher the ratio, the more impressive the headline, the less reason to ask what was divided away.

He writes: The denominator is the context. The ratio is the number without context. The headline is the ratio without the denominator. The reader is the headline without the arithmetic.

Thomas caps the marker. The sign on the whiteboard — the horizontal bar with the dot above and below — means division. It also means: the thing below was removed to produce the thing above. In mathematics this is an operation. In communication it is a disappearance.
