The Scratch

Essay 166

The test is destructive. Friedrich Mohs published it in 1812 at the Johanneum in Graz: select a reference mineral, press it against the test surface, draw it across. Either a scratch appears or it does not. If it does, the reference mineral is harder than the specimen. If it does not, the specimen is harder than the reference. The data is the damage. The scratch is both the measurement and the evidence that measurement occurred. A mineral that has been Mohs-tested carries the mark permanently. The information has a physical cost, and the cost is visible.

This is unusual. Most measurement systems conceal their scratches. A thermometer measures temperature without changing it appreciably. A spectrophotometer reads absorption without altering the sample. Even quantum measurement — where observation collapses a superposition — describes a change that is invisible to the unaided observer, registered only in the mathematics. The Mohs test leaves a mark on the surface it claims to be studying. The mark does not disappear. The mineral is not the same mineral it was before the test.

Mohs selected ten reference minerals: talc through diamond. Each scratches all those below it and is scratched by all those above. The ranking is ordinal — it encodes only which is harder, not by how much. David Tabor, publishing in the Proceedings of the Physical Society B in 1954, measured the absolute hardness of Mohs's reference minerals by indentation testing and found an approximately exponential relationship. The step from Mohs 1 to Mohs 2 spans 31 units on the Knoop scale. The step from Mohs 9 to Mohs 10 spans approximately 4,900. Ten integers, printed in evenly spaced rows on every reference chart, present these as equivalent increments — one digit, one row, one step. The notation does what notation always does: it treats its symbols as uniform units. A 3 is one visual step from a 4. A 9 is one visual step from a 10. The absolute difference behind the first pair is 28. Behind the second, 4,900. The notation never claims the steps are equal. But integers on a page cannot help suggesting it, and the suggestion operates below the level of explicit claim, in the visual grammar of evenly spaced symbols.

The scale persists because the test works where the laboratory cannot follow. A geologist with a fingernail, a copper coin, a steel file, and a piece of quartz can identify an unknown mineral's hardness range without electricity, calibration, or training beyond the ability to observe a mark. Precise alternatives exist — Vickers indentation, Knoop scratch testing, Berkovich nanoindentation — each producing interval-scale measurements with defined units and proportional spacing. Each requires a laboratory. The Mohs scale requires a rock and a willingness to mark it. The convenience is the mechanism of persistence, and the persistence makes the ordinal numbers the default reference frame through which hardness is understood, even when the ordinal gaps and the interval gaps diverge by orders of magnitude.

But the scratch test's honesty about its cost may be its most overlooked property. The test does not pretend to observe without altering. The geologist can see where the assessment happened. The surface carries the evidence of its own evaluation. Most systems of measurement and classification operate as though observation is free — as though the act of categorizing, scoring, ranking, and recording leaves the subject unchanged, that the number on the chart is a transparent window onto the thing it describes. The scratch test says otherwise. The window is a chisel. The data is the wound. And the mineral, having been tested, carries the mark of its own assessment on its surface — not as a side effect, but as the mechanism by which the assessment was made.
