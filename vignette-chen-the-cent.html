Chen and the Cent

Chen was reading about the penny when she found the motto. "Mind Your Business." The Fugio cent, 1787, designed by Benjamin Franklin. A sundial, the sun above it, FUGIO — I flee. Time fleeing. Business minded.

"The coin costs 3.69 cents to produce," Maren would have said. "It is worth one cent. The nineteenth consecutive year of loss. Since 2006, the cumulative cost exceeds one billion dollars. Sixty to seventy percent of pennies produced each year exit circulation — dropped into jars, lost in cushions, thrown into fountains. The Mint manufactures billions of objects that immediately disappear."

Chen followed the thread to the zinc lobby. Jarden Zinc Products of Greeneville, Tennessee. Sole supplier of penny blanks. $48 million contract. They funded Americans for Common Cents — a lobbying group whose executive director was paid $340,000 to defend the penny. The zinc industry supplied the material, funded the advocacy, and framed preservation as patriotism.

The paragraph at 0.8 — the paragraph she kept suppressing — would have said that Canada eliminated its penny in 2013. No measurable harm. Australia in 1992. New Zealand in 1990. Finland, Ireland, Belgium. Every case: no significant economic consequence. The evidence was available. The evidence was irrelevant.

The paragraph would have said that every Congressional bill to eliminate the penny failed. That polls showed majority support for keeping it. That the sentiment was real and also cultivated. That on February 10, 2025, Trump ordered the Treasury to halt production. That the sentiment was not defeated — polls still showed majority support — but bypassed. Authority did what deliberation could not.

Chen set down the coin she'd been holding. A zinc disk plated with copper, stamped with Lincoln's profile and a denomination that could buy nothing. The motto from 1787 was still on the original: Mind Your Business. Someone, 238 years later, finally had.
