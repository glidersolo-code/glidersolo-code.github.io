The Type

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator assigns each person one of 16 personality types. Four letters: E or I, S or N, T or F, J or P. Each letter represents one side of a binary — extraversion or introversion, sensing or intuition, thinking or feeling, judging or perceiving. The test produces a type. The type produces an identity. "I'm an INTJ." Millions of people say this about themselves with confidence. The statement has less predictive validity than most people would believe possible for something used by 88% of Fortune 500 companies.

Isabel Briggs Myers developed the indicator during World War II. She had no formal training in psychology or psychometrics. Her mother, Katharine Cook Briggs, had been studying personality since 1917 and adopted Carl Jung's framework of psychological types after his Psychologische Typen was published in 1921. Myers believed that understanding type could help women entering the wartime workforce find suitable jobs. The motivation was practical and generous. The instrument was published by Educational Testing Service in 1962.

The instrument measures four continuous scales. On each scale, a test-taker receives a score — a number on a continuum. Then the continuum is cut in half. A person who scores 51% toward extraversion is assigned E. A person who scores 49% is assigned I. These two people — nearly identical in their measured extraversion — receive opposite type letters. They will describe themselves differently. They will be described by colleagues differently. They will be sorted into different teams, given different career advice, shown different frameworks for self-understanding. The difference between them is one scale point. The difference assigned to them is categorical.

The actual distributions on each MBTI dimension are unimodal — bell-shaped, with most people clustered near the middle. They are not bimodal, not split into two natural groups. There is no dip at the midpoint. There is no gap between extraverts and introverts. The test imposes two categories on a single peak. The categories are not found in the data. They are applied to it.

David Pittenger reviewed the psychometric evidence and found that up to 50% of test-takers receive a different four-letter type when retaking the MBTI five weeks later. Half. The instrument claims to measure a stable personality type. The type is not stable under the instrument's own measurement. Robert McCrae and Paul Costa showed that the four MBTI dimensions correlate with four of the Big Five personality traits — a well-validated continuous model. The MBTI measures approximately the same thing, worse. It discards the continuous information and replaces it with binary categories that are less reliable, less valid, and less informative.

The MBTI persists not because of its psychometric properties but despite them. It persists because "INTJ" is identity-shaped. It fits in a bio, a dating profile, a team-building exercise, a conversation opener. "I scored 62nd percentile on introversion, 71st on intuition, 55th on thinking, and 48th on judging" does not. The label is more portable than the measurement. The label is more stable than the measurement — not because the underlying trait is stable, but because the label, once adopted, resists revision. People remember their type. They forget their score. They forget that the score is what the test actually produced, and the type is what was imposed on it.

The concealment is not in the test. The test is publicly available, the scoring is documented, the limitations are discussed in every psychometrics textbook. The concealment is in the format. The four-letter type generates the impression of a real classification — 16 kinds of people, as natural as the 16 squares on a chessboard quarter. The format is the product. The product is the identity. The identity is the sale.
