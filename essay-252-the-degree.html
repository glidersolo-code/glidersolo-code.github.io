Essay 252: The Degree

Zero degrees Fahrenheit is the freezing point of a saturated brine solution — water mixed with ammonium chloride to the point of saturation. Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit, a Polish-German physicist working in Amsterdam, chose this as his zero in 1724 because he needed a temperature below the freezing point of water for calibrating his mercury thermometers, and the brine solution was the coldest reproducible temperature he could reliably produce in his workshop. Thirty-two degrees is water freezing. Ninety-six degrees was intended as body temperature — Fahrenheit chose 96 because it was divisible by many small numbers, convenient for graduating a scale with a file. The boiling point of water, 212 degrees, was derived afterward. The distance between 32 and 212 is 180 — a half-circle, a coincidence that Fahrenheit may or may not have valued.

Zero degrees Celsius is the freezing point of water. One hundred degrees is the boiling point. Anders Celsius, a Swedish astronomer, proposed the scale in 1742 with the values inverted — 0 for boiling, 100 for freezing. Carl Linnaeus, or possibly Jean-Pierre Christin in Lyon the following year, reversed the direction. The scale that bears Celsius's name is not the one he proposed. The reference points — water at two phase transitions, separated by 100 degrees — are transparent. The consumer knows why 0 is 0 and why 100 is 100.

The American consumer does not know why 32 is 32. Thirty-two degrees Fahrenheit is experienced as a fact about water the way 212 is experienced as a fact about boiling — a number the universe assigned. The universe assigned nothing. Fahrenheit assigned 32 because he assigned 0 to brine and built a scale between them. The number 32 is an artifact of a Dutch workshop in the 1720s. It persists because the infrastructure — weather forecasts, thermostats, cooking instructions, medical references, the intuitive sense that 70 is comfortable and 90 is hot — has crystallized around it.

The conversion is not intuitive. Celsius to Fahrenheit: multiply by nine-fifths, add thirty-two. The formula contains a fraction and an offset. It is neither a simple multiplication (like proof to ABV: divide by two) nor a simple shift (like UTC to CST: subtract six hours). It is both simultaneously. The two scales share no common zero. They are not proportional. They intersect at minus forty — the one temperature where Fahrenheit and Celsius agree — and diverge in both directions from there.

The United States is one of five countries that use Fahrenheit as the primary temperature scale. The others — the Bahamas, Belize, the Cayman Islands, and Palau — together comprise fewer than a million people. Three hundred and thirty million Americans experience Fahrenheit as the temperature of the world. Seven and a half billion others experience Celsius. The American weather forecast says 75. The rest of the world says 24. Both are correct. Neither means anything to the other without a formula most people cannot perform in their heads.

The Fahrenheit degree is smaller than the Celsius degree — one Fahrenheit degree is five-ninths of one Celsius degree. This gives Fahrenheit finer resolution without decimals. The difference between 70°F and 71°F is a real, perceivable change in comfort — about 0.56°C. The Celsius equivalent requires a decimal: 21.1 versus 21.7. The extra resolution is genuine and also unnecessary — weather forecasts could report to one decimal place. They don't, because integers are cleaner, and the format prefers integers.

Two scales, one measuring from brine and the other from ice, each experienced as the temperature of things. The format is the degree.
