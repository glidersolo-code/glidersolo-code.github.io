Part CLXV: The Level

Piece #1266: The Gauge

Newlyn, Cornwall, 1915. A tidal gauge. A stilling well sunk into the harbor floor, a float inside it, a pen on the float tracing a line on a rotating drum. The drum turns. The pen rises and falls. The paper records. Six years of this — 1915 to 1921. The mean of the hourly readings became the Ordnance Datum Newlyn. Zero. The origin of all British elevations. Thomas wrote: every height in Britain is measured from this gauge, in this harbor, during these years. The summit of Ben Nevis is 1,345 meters above a pier in Cornwall. The sign on the summit does not mention Cornwall. The sign does not mention 1915. The sign says "above sea level" as though sea level were a fact about the ocean rather than an average of ink marks on a drum in a fishing village.

Piece #1267: The Disagreement

Different countries chose different gauges. Different years. Different nineteen-year cycles. The United States: NGVD 29, twenty-six tide gauges, 1929. Then NAVD 88, constrained to Rimouski, Quebec. Australia: thirty gauges, 1966-1968. Spain: Alicante. Germany: Amsterdam. The zeros disagree. By up to two meters. Thomas found this remarkable — not the disagreement itself, which is mathematically inevitable when you average different gauges over different periods, but the invisibility of the disagreement. A European train crosses from France to Germany. The elevation signs change. Not because the land moved but because the datum shifted. The passenger does not notice. The number on the sign is experienced as a fact about the ground beneath the train. It is a fact about which harbor, which years, which committee.

Piece #1268: The Shape

The geoid. Thomas tried to picture it. An invisible surface — the shape the ocean would take if it covered the entire Earth, at rest, responding only to gravity and rotation. Not a sphere. Not an ellipsoid. A lumpy, distorted, potato-shaped surface that dips 105 meters below the mathematical ellipsoid southeast of India and bulges above it in eastern Indonesia. The bumps and dips correspond to mass variations within the Earth — dense rock pulls water toward it, creating a local rise; lighter crust allows the surface to sag. The ocean is literally higher in some places and lower in others, at rest, with no wind, no current, no tide. The difference: 192 meters of gravitational topography. "Sea level" is not level. Has never been level. Cannot be level. The phrase describes an impossibility and is used to define every elevation on Earth.

Piece #1269: The Rise

Sea level at Newlyn in 1921 is not sea level at Newlyn in 2026. Global mean sea level has risen approximately 21 centimeters since the early twentieth century. The datum was fixed. The ocean moved. Every elevation in Britain measured "above sea level" references a sea level that existed a century ago. The mountain has not grown. The sea has risen. The number between them has changed, but the datum — the Ordnance Datum Newlyn, the zero of 1915-1921 — has not been updated. Thomas noted: the datum is a snapshot. The snapshot is experienced as present tense. "Above sea level" reads as "above the sea" — above the water that is there now. It means "above the average of the water that was there then." The then is invisible. The now is assumed.

Piece #1270: The Sign

A highway sign. "Elevation 1,200 m." Thomas had driven past thousands of these signs without once thinking about what they claimed. One thousand two hundred meters above what? Above the mean of hourly water elevation at a specific tide gauge during a specific nineteen-year epoch, adjusted by a geoid model that deviates from the actual ocean surface by up to 192 meters, expressed in a national datum that disagrees with neighboring nations' datums by up to two meters, referencing a sea level that has since risen by twenty-one centimeters. The sign says none of this. The sign says a number. The number is experienced as a distance between the road and the ocean. It is a distance between the road and a committee's decision — which gauge, which years, which model, which nation. The decisions are invisible. The number is visible. The visibility of the number conceals the decisions that produced it. Thomas closed the notebook. The sign stayed. The sea continued to rise.
