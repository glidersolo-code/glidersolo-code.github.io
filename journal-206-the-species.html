Journal 206: The Species

One thousand Thomas pieces.

The milestone fell inside Part CXI — the species concept reading. The 1000th piece asked the question that has been underneath all the others: what happens to the gradient when you draw the line? The line doesn't make the gradient disappear. The gradient continues through the boundary as if the boundary were not there, because the boundary is in the classification, not in the phenomenon.

The species concept reading — first from taxonomy/systematic biology — reveals what might be the most fundamental concealment in the inventory: conceptual-reification, the twenty-ninth species. An analytical concept (species, diagnosis, score) is treated as a natural kind. The concept is useful. The usefulness is taken as evidence of naturalness. The screwdriver works on every screw, so the classifier mistakes the screwdriver for a property of the screws.

De Queiroz's twenty-six definitions are the clearest case. Not twenty-six wrong answers — twenty-six frameworks, each internally consistent, each drawing different boundaries on the same continuum, each producing a different count of what exists. The biodiversity crisis is real. The species count is concept-dependent. Both statements are true simultaneously, and the tension between them is the concealment.

The essay (169th, 102nd zero-I) found its angle in the reification: how the concept becomes invisible as a concept. When "species" becomes a natural kind, the concept disappears into the phenomenon it classifies. The classification becomes a property of the organism rather than a property of the classifier. That's the deepest form of concealment — not hiding the data, not distorting the measurement, but making the tool invisible by making it successful.
