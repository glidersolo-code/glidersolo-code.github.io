The Normal

The blood is drawn. The serum is centrifuged. The thyroid-stimulating hormone is measured. The result: 3.8 mIU/L. The laboratory report says: normal. Or the laboratory report says: elevated. Both are true, depending on which reference range the laboratory uses.

The upper limit of the TSH reference range — the boundary between normal thyroid function and subclinical hypothyroidism — is one of the most consequential contested numbers in clinical medicine. For most laboratories, the upper limit is approximately 4.5 mIU/L, derived from population studies conducted in the 1990s. In 2002, the National Academy of Clinical Biochemistry recommended narrowing it to 2.5. The American Thyroid Association endorsed the narrower range. The Endocrine Society did not. A 2004 consensus panel of three professional societies ended without consensus.

Between 2.5 and 4.5 lies a population. Tens of millions of people, predominantly women over 50, whose diagnosis depends on which committee their physician follows. At 2.5, they are subclinically hypothyroid — a disease category that did not exist before the range was narrowed. At 4.5, they are normal. Their biology has not changed. The number has not changed. The boundary moved.

The original reference population was contaminated. The 1988-1994 NHANES III survey included all participants — including those with undiagnosed Hashimoto's thyroiditis, which elevates TSH. When those individuals were retrospectively excluded, the upper limit dropped. The contamination was invisible in the original data: the autoimmune thyroid disease was undiagnosed, so the reference sample appeared healthy. The boundary between normal and abnormal was set using a sample that included the condition the boundary was meant to detect.

Age was not stratified. TSH rises naturally with age — approximately 3.6 at the 97.5th percentile for ages 20-29, approximately 5.9 for ages 80 and older. A single reference range, applied across all ages, treats a 25-year-old with a TSH of 4.0 and an 80-year-old with the same value identically. For the younger patient, the value is statistically unusual. For the older patient, it is average. Both receive the same laboratory flag.

The TRUST trial settled nothing and everything. Seven hundred thirty-seven adults over 65 with subclinical hypothyroidism were randomized to levothyroxine or placebo. No difference in quality of life. No difference in fatigue. No difference in cardiovascular events. The treatment designed for the disease category created by the narrowed reference range did not produce a detectable benefit. The disease category persists. The prescriptions persist. Levothyroxine is one of the most prescribed medications in the United States — approximately 23 million prescriptions per year.

The reference range appears to describe the body. The reference range describes the sample from which it was derived: who was included, who was excluded retrospectively, whether age was stratified, and which committee's recommendation the laboratory adopted. The boundary between health and disease is a committee decision formatted as a biological fact. The formatting is what makes it stick.
