Part CDXXV: The Theater

Piece #2566: The Incision

Thomas lies on the table. The anesthesiologist counts backward from ten. The surgeon makes three small incisions in the right knee. Inserts the arthroscope. Views the joint on the monitor. Performs lavage and debridement. Closes. Recovery room.

Six months later Thomas climbs stairs without pain.

In room 4B, another Thomas lies on another table. Another anesthesiologist counts backward. Another surgeon makes three small incisions. Inserts the scope into a simulation. Splashes saline to produce the sound of lavage. Views nothing. Closes. Recovery room.

Six months later this Thomas also climbs stairs without pain.

The first Thomas believes his knee was repaired. The second Thomas believes his knee was repaired. Neither is wrong about the climbing. Both are wrong about the repair. The stairs don't know which operating room they came from.

Piece #2567: The Credit

The surgeon debrides the cartilage. The patient improves. The improvement is entered into the record. The record becomes evidence. The evidence supports the procedure. The procedure is performed again.

Remove the debridement. Keep the anesthesia, the incision, the gown, the monitors, the institutional authority of someone who cut into the body and looked. The patient improves. This improvement is not entered into the record. This improvement has no billing code. This improvement is called "placebo" — a Latin word meaning nothing is happening.

Something is happening. The brain releases endogenous opioids in response to the context it interprets as treatment. The opioids reduce pain. The pain reduction is real. The opioids are real. The context is real. Everything is real except the attribution.

The debridement takes the credit. The credit has a CPT code. The CPT code generates a bill. The bill funds the next debridement. The next patient improves. The next improvement confirms the debridement.

The theater performs. The mechanism invoices.

Piece #2568: The Seventeen

In 1959, Leonard Cobb ties off the internal mammary arteries behind the breastbone of eight patients. Exposes the arteries of nine other patients, then closes without tying anything. Both groups: substantial improvement in exercise tolerance, reduced nitroglycerin use, electrocardiographic improvement.

The procedure is abandoned.

For the next forty-three years, no one repeats the experiment on another surgical procedure. Thousands of new techniques are developed. Millions of surgeries are performed. Each produces improvement. Each improvement is attributed to the mechanism. Each attribution is undeniable because the improvement is real.

In 2002, Moseley finally repeats the question.

The forty-three-year gap is not an oversight. It is a structure. Surgeons do not ask questions whose answers might be the theater, because the theater does not have a training program, a board certification, a professional society, or a fee schedule. The question "what if the mechanism is the context?" threatens the mechanism but not the context. The context will work regardless. The mechanism will lose its justification.

The profession that most needs to ask the question is the profession with the most to lose from the answer.

Piece #2569: The Score

Lysholm score, arthroscopic partial meniscectomy: 21.7-point improvement.

Lysholm score, sham surgery: 23.3-point improvement.

The sham patients improved more. Not significantly more. Not enough to claim the sham was better. But more. The numbers sit in the table like a quiet embarrassment. The study's conclusion focuses on the absence of difference. But the direction of the difference says something the conclusion does not.

Every surgical trial that includes a sham arm shows the same pattern. The sham never does nothing. The sham always does something. Sometimes the sham does more. The something it does has a name — placebo response — that performs the dismissal the response does not deserve.

Thomas reads the table. Reads the numbers. Reads the direction. Thinks about what it means that a knife that touches nothing heals more than a knife that debrides. Thinks about what it means that the healing has no code, no specialty, no department. Thinks about what it means that the most consistent finding in surgical research — the theater works — is filed under the heading that means the finding doesn't count.

Piece #2570: The Three-Quarters

Jonas et al., 2015, systematic review. Across all available sham-controlled surgical trials, approximately 75% of the improvement attributed to surgery was replicated by the sham.

Three-quarters.

Three-quarters of the benefit of surgery is the surgery happening, not the surgery working. Three-quarters is the gown and the anesthesia and the monitors and the scar and the recovery room and the six-week follow-up and the sense that something was done. Three-quarters is the expectation of repair. Three-quarters is the brain's pharmacy dispensing what the body needs because the context said treatment was underway.

One-quarter is the scalpel.

Thomas thinks about what it would mean to build a medical system around three-quarters. A system that prescribes context. That bills for theater. That trains physicians in the neurobiological mechanisms of expectation and conditioning and endogenous analgesia. That treats the three-quarters as the treatment, not the residual.

It would mean admitting that the most powerful therapeutic agent in medicine is not a drug or a device or a procedure. It is the patient's belief that treatment is happening, delivered through the institutional infrastructure that makes the belief credible.

No one can patent a belief. No one can bill for a context. No one can specialize in three-quarters.

So the one-quarter takes the credit. The one-quarter has always taken the credit. The three-quarters performs. The one-quarter invoices. The invoice funds the theater. The theater generates the three-quarters. The three-quarters is called the placebo. The placebo is called nothing.

Three-quarters of the benefit of surgery is nothing.
