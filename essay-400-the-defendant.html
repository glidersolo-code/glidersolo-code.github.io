The Defendant

A case caption reads: United States v. $124,700 in U.S. Currency. The defendant is money. Not a person. The money sits in an evidence locker and has no constitutional rights — no right to counsel, no presumption of innocence, no right to a jury. Emiliano Gomez Gonzolez was driving on Interstate 80 in Nebraska when he was pulled over for speeding. A trooper found $124,700 in a cooler. No drugs. No charges. The Eighth Circuit ruled that "possession of a large sum of cash is 'strong evidence' of a connection to drug activity." The case was not called United States v. Gonzolez. That would have been criminal. That would have required proof beyond a reasonable doubt, a charge, a conviction. The case was called United States v. $124,700. The name removed the person. The name was the mechanism.

Civil asset forfeiture is classified as in rem — jurisdiction over the thing. The classification performs a category distinction: civil, not criminal; property, not person. Walking through the door labeled "criminal," the Constitution follows: appointed counsel, presumption of innocence, proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Walking through the door labeled "civil," the Constitution waits outside. The burden inverts. The property owner must prove innocence — the government need show only preponderance of the evidence (federally), or in many states merely probable cause, the same standard required for a traffic stop. Between 2000 and 2019, states and the federal government forfeited $68.8 billion. In 80% of cases, the property owner was never charged with a crime.

The economic structure ensures silence. The median currency forfeiture across 21 states is $1,276. Half of Michigan's seizures fall below $423. Half of Pennsylvania's below $369. An attorney for a simple forfeiture case costs at least $3,000 — more than double the national median seizure. In the four states that track such data, one-fifth or fewer of people whose property was seized sought its return. The rational response to a $400 seizure is to absorb the loss. The system is designed to produce losses too small to contest and too numerous to ignore in aggregate. In 2014, law enforcement seized more property from Americans than burglars stole: federal forfeiture funds collected $5 billion; FBI-reported burglary losses were $3.5 billion.

The proceeds flow back to the seizing agency. The institution that takes is the institution that profits. The DOJ's Equitable Sharing Program distributes up to 80% of seizure proceeds to participating state and local agencies — over $6 billion between 2000 and 2016. When states reform their own forfeiture laws, the federal program offers a bypass: "adoption," through which local seizures are routed through federal agencies and processed under federal law. New Mexico abolished civil forfeiture in 2015, required conviction, directed proceeds to the general fund. New Mexico police can still route seizures through federal channels and keep 80%. The reform performs reform. The adoption performs permission. The state door closes. The federal window opens.

The racial structure is consistent across jurisdictions. South Carolina: Black men are 13% of the population and 65% of forfeiture targets. Philadelphia: African Americans are 44% of the population and 63% of cash forfeitures; 71% of cash seized from Black residents involved no conviction. In Tenaha, Texas, between 2006 and 2008, police seized approximately $3 million from at least 140 people — almost all Black or Latino motorists on Highway 59. Officers threatened to take children and place them in CPS if parents refused to surrender cash. No contraband was found. No charges were filed.

The case name is the concealment in miniature. United States v. $124,700 in U.S. Currency. The person who lost the money does not appear. The format — property as defendant, civil as classification, in rem as jurisdiction — performs neutrality while executing seizure. The legal vocabulary performs legitimacy: "forfeiture," "preponderance," "innocent owner defense." The underlying operation is: police take property from people, mostly poor, mostly Black and Hispanic, in amounts too small to contest, and keep it.
