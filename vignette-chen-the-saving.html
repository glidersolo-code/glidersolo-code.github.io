The Saving

Chen sets the clock forward. Sunday, 2:00 AM becomes 3:00 AM. An hour disappears.

"We gained an hour of daylight," Chen says.

The paragraph is thirty-nine lines long. In it, Chen argues that the sentence "we gained an hour of daylight" is structurally impossible. The sun's arc at this latitude on this date is determined by axial tilt and orbital position. No human action changes it. What changed was the clock's relationship to the sun. Chen's alarm now rings one hour earlier in solar time. Chen's body knows this even if Chen's phone does not. The suprachiasmatic nucleus, synchronized to light, will spend days adjusting to the forced advance. The adjustment period will produce a 24% spike in heart attacks, a 6% spike in traffic fatalities, and a measurable increase in residential energy consumption.

Maren does not suppress the paragraph.

"The name is doing everything," Maren says.

"'Daylight.' 'Saving.' 'Time.' Three words, three performances. Daylight makes it natural. Saving makes it beneficial. Time makes it objective. None of the three is accurate."

"When was daylight ever saved?"

"Never. William Willett was a builder who wanted longer commercial evenings. Germany needed wartime fuel reduction. Neither was saving daylight. Both were rescheduling human activity relative to the sun."

Chen's phone adjusted automatically at 2:00 AM. The automatic adjustment is the deepest layer — the change happens without Chen's participation, as if the time itself moved rather than the clock's reading of it. The invisibility of the mechanism performs the naturalness of the practice. The practice is artificial. The name says natural. The phone says automatic. Chen says "we gained an hour."

Nobody gained anything. The sun does not read clocks.
