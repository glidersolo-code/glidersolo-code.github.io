The Burn

The UV Index is a dimensionless number representing the intensity of ultraviolet radiation at the Earth's surface, weighted by the erythemal action spectrum. James Kerr and C. Thomas McElroy at Environment Canada developed it in 1992. The WHO, WMO, UNEP, and ICNIRP jointly adopted it as a global standard in 2002. The number appears on weather forecasts, phone apps, and beach signage worldwide. It is reported as an integer — typically 1 through 11+ — with color-coded categories: low, moderate, high, very high, extreme.

The erythemal action spectrum — standardized by the Commission Internationale de l'Éclairage in 1987, based on work by McKinlay and Diffey — describes the relative effectiveness of different UV wavelengths at producing erythema: reddening of the skin. The spectrum was derived from studies of fair-skinned individuals, primarily Fitzpatrick skin types I and II. The UV Index reports the sun's capacity to redden skin that reddens easily. For a person with deeply pigmented skin — Fitzpatrick type V or VI — the same UV Index number corresponds to a categorically different biological response. The number does not adjust for skin type. The behavioral guidance does not adjust. The same number, the same icon, the same color bar.

The choice of erythema as the weighting function was not arbitrary but was consequential. Erythema is one biological effect of UV radiation. Others include cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer formation (the primary UV-induced DNA lesion associated with skin cancer), immunosuppression, photoaging, and vitamin D synthesis. Each has a different action spectrum — a different wavelength-dependent curve. The erythemal spectrum peaks at approximately 297 nanometers. The DNA damage spectrum peaks at shorter wavelengths and has a different shape. Diffey noted in the British Journal of Dermatology in 2002 that a UV exposure scenario optimized for low erythema does not necessarily minimize DNA damage. The UV Index reports one biological effect and is interpreted as reporting another. The proxy — sunburn — was chosen because it was the first UV effect to be systematically quantified and because it was visible. Reddening can be observed. DNA damage cannot.

The scale was designed for temperate latitudes near sea level — the conditions under which most Canadians live. At equatorial high altitudes, the UV Index routinely exceeds 15 and has been measured above 40. The "extreme" category begins at 11 and contains no further differentiation. A UV Index of 11 and a UV Index of 25 receive the same label, the same color, the same public health recommendation. The populations living at the highest UV exposures receive the least informative output from the system designed to warn them.

The UV Index is reported once daily — typically the solar noon maximum. It collapses temporal variation into a single number. Cloud cover, ozone column depth, surface albedo, and solar zenith angle all change throughout the day. The number at noon does not describe the morning or the late afternoon. It is a snapshot labeled as a day. A single integer, derived from spectrally weighted irradiance using an action spectrum calibrated for one skin type, representing one biological effect, at one time of day, in a scale designed for one latitude — printed beside a sun icon as though it described the relationship between a person and the sky.
