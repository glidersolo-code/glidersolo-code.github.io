The Serving

The Nutrition Labeling and Education Act of 1990 required standardized serving sizes on food labels. The FDA implemented this in 1993 by establishing Reference Amounts Customarily Consumed — RACCs — for approximately 150 food categories. The data source was the USDA's Nationwide Food Consumption Surveys from 1977-78 and 1987-88. The serving size on the label was not a recommendation. It was a backward-looking statistic: the amount people reported eating per occasion in the late 1970s and 1980s.

The distinction between description and prescription collapsed on the label. "Serving Size: 1/2 cup" was designed to standardize comparison — so that two ice cream brands could be compared per equal portion. But on the panel, in the format of the Nutrition Facts box, "Serving Size" reads as instruction. The format converts a survey result into a dietary norm. The consumer who reads "1/2 cup" does not think "this is what Americans reported eating in 1977." The consumer thinks "this is what I should eat." The RACC was meant to reflect reality. The label was meant to enable comparison. The combination produced guidance that neither the survey nor the regulation intended.

The gap between survey and shelf widened for decades. A bagel in 1977 weighed approximately 55 grams. The RACC was set at 55 grams. By 2000, the average bakery bagel weighed 100 to 110 grams. The consumer eating a modern bagel was consuming two servings of a food that presented as one physical object. The label described a 1977 bagel on a 2000 bakel. For beverages, the RACC was 8 fluid ounces. A 20-ounce bottle of soda — designed, sold, and consumed as a single unit — was labeled as 2.5 servings. The per-serving calories appeared manageable. The per-container calories required multiplication the label's design did not encourage.

In 2016, the FDA issued a final rule updating RACCs using 2003-2008 NHANES data. Ice cream moved from 1/2 cup to 2/3 cup. Beverages moved from 8 to 12 fluid ounces. The rule also required that packages "reasonably consumed at one eating occasion" be labeled as single servings, and that larger packages display both per-serving and per-package values in dual columns. The corrections addressed the most visible distortions — the 20-ounce soda, the single-serve bag of chips labeled as two servings. They did not address the structural issue: the serving size is a comparison tool that the consumer reads as a consumption guide.

The dual-column label, implemented by the 2020 compliance date, presents two frames. The per-serving column anchors the first impression. The per-package column provides context. The order matters: 230 calories per serving feels moderate before 920 calories per package feels excessive. Both describe the same pint of ice cream. The consumer's response depends on which number the eye encounters first — a sequence effect embedded in a layout that the regulation specified and the regulation did not acknowledge.

The deeper concealment is in the word "serving." A serving is, in ordinary language, what someone serves — an amount placed on a plate, chosen by the eater. In regulatory language, a serving is a statistical artifact derived from survey data, converted to a household measure, assigned to a food category, and printed on a label. The label uses the word in the regulatory sense. The consumer reads it in the ordinary sense. The gap between these two meanings is where the format does its work: the consumer who eats a pint of ice cream has eaten three servings of a food that arrived as one container, bought as one unit, and consumed as one experience. The label divides the experience into fractions. The consumer lives the experience as a whole. The serving size is the ratio between two realities — the label's and the eater's — and the label does not disclose which reality it represents.
