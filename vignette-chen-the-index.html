The Index

Chen-Maren vignette

Chen reads the paragraph aloud. Thirty-nine lines. The committee's evaluation rubric produces a composite score — a single number that classifies the paragraph as satisfactory, promising, or distinguished. The score is the paragraph's BMI: a ratio that captures something about the paragraph's relationship to the committee's expectations, derived from a formula calibrated on a normative sample of previous paragraphs, using weights that were chosen for convenience rather than precision.

Maren has been reading about the Quetelet Index. Adolphe Quetelet, 1832: body weight scales with the square of height across populations. The squared exponent was empirical — it fit the data, not the physiology. He was describing l'homme moyen, the average man. The average man was not a person. He was a coordinate.

Chen's paragraph is measured by its distance from l'homme moyen. The rubric's normative sample — the previous paragraphs that calibrated the thresholds between satisfactory and promising, between promising and distinguished — defines the average paragraph. The average paragraph is not a paragraph. It is a coordinate derived from averaging the scores of paragraphs whose quality, ambition, and structural logic varied so widely that the average represents none of them.

Professor Wallace scores Chen's paragraph a 3.8. This places it in the "promising" category — above the threshold for satisfactory (3.0) but below distinguished (4.5). The thresholds were set at the 2019 workshop, calibrated on the 2019 cohort's submissions, and have not been updated. The WHO standardized BMI thresholds in 1995 from predominantly white European data and declined to change them in 2004 when the data showed they didn't apply to other populations. The committee standardized rubric thresholds in 2019 from a specific cohort's submissions and has not reconsidered them as the program's intake has changed.

Chen's paragraph is not 3.8. Chen's paragraph is Chen's paragraph. 3.8 is what happens when you measure a paragraph with an index that was designed to describe a distribution and apply it to classify an individual, using an exponent from 2019 and thresholds from 2019 and a normative center that is not a paragraph. The average paragraph does not exist. Chen's distance from it is nonetheless the number that determines what happens next.
