Vignette Chen The Pour Pattern

Vignette

Chen's forensic science seminar had reached fire investigation. She projected a photograph of an irregularly burned floor — curved shapes, trailing edges, dark scorching in a puddle-like pattern.

"What is this?" she asked.

"Pour pattern," said the student nearest the screen. "Someone poured accelerant."

"How do you know someone poured accelerant?"

"Because — the pattern. It's shaped like a liquid was poured."

"You've just done something remarkable," Chen said. "You've named an observation after a cause, then used the name as evidence of the cause. Watch the circle. The floor has an irregular pattern. You call it a 'pour pattern' because it looks like liquid was poured. Now someone asks you how you know the fire was arson. You say: 'Because there are pour patterns on the floor.' The vocabulary has replaced the investigation."

She put up a second photograph. "This is a floor from a controlled burn in Jacksonville, Florida, 1990. No accelerant. A couch was set on fire in a house identical to one where six people had died. Fire scientist John Lentini recreated the conditions exactly. The result: every pattern you see in the first photograph also appeared in the second. Pour patterns. Crazed glass. Alligator charring. All of it. From a couch fire with no accelerant."

Silence.

"The entire vocabulary of fire investigation was built this way. 'Crazed glass' — named to imply the glass experienced something extreme. In fact, it's caused by water from the fire hose hitting hot glass. Every fire scene the department responds to will have crazed glass. 'Alligator charring' — named to suggest voracious, accelerant-fueled burning. In fact, all wood chars this way given sufficient heat and time. The NFPA showed different alligator patterns on the same wall in the same fire."

"So when did they fix the terminology?"

"NFPA 921, first edition, 1992. 'Pour pattern' became 'irregular pattern.' Two words changed. A verdict became a question."

"And before 1992?"

Chen put up a third photograph. A man's face. "Cameron Todd Willingham. Convicted 1992. Executed 2004. More than twenty 'indicators of arson' cited at his trial. Gerald Hurst — a Cambridge-trained chemist — reviewed every one and found no evidence of arson. His report was faxed to the governor's office before the execution."

"And?"

"The execution proceeded." Chen paused. "The vocabulary was corrected in 1992. The conviction occurred in 1992. The correction was a recommendation. The conviction was a sentence. The recommendation did not reach the jury. The sentence reached the man."

She turned off the projector. "Here is the exam question you will think about for the rest of the semester: What is the difference between an observation and a description? And here is the answer the fire investigation case provides: the difference is the name. If the name contains the cause, the observation is not an observation. It is a verdict wearing the clothes of a description. The investigator believes he is testifying to what he saw. He is testifying to what the vocabulary told him he saw."

Maren, in the back row, wrote in her notebook: The name was the finding. The finding was the name. Between them, no room for doubt. Which is the same as no room for innocence.
