Part CXLI: The District

Piece #1146: The Container

Thomas lived in a district.

The district had been drawn in 2022, following the 2020 census. It contained 761,169 people — within one person of the state average, as required by Reynolds v. Sims (1964). The boundary ran along the interstate for two miles, turned south on a county road, bisected a neighborhood, followed a creek for half a mile, turned east along a railroad track, crossed a highway, enclosed a subdivision, and returned to the interstate. The shape, viewed from above, resembled nothing in particular. It was not a square. It was not a salamander. It was a polygon optimized by software that tested four million possible configurations in three hours.

Thomas's mother had not chosen the district. She had chosen the apartment, which was on Oak Street, which was inside the boundary. Three blocks east, the creek formed the district line. The apartment complex on the other side of the creek was in a different district with a different representative. The residents of both complexes used the same grocery store, the same bus route, the same elementary school. They lived in different political containers.

The container had been drawn to contain them differently.

Piece #1147: The Requirement

The requirement was equal population.

Baker v. Carr (1962) established that redistricting was justiciable — courts could review it. Reynolds v. Sims (1964) established the standard: one person, one vote. Districts must be roughly equal in population. The requirement was about fairness. It said nothing about shape, contiguity, compactness, community of interest, or political composition. It said: equal numbers.

Equal numbers are necessary and insufficient. A city of 100,000 can be divided into ten districts of 10,000 in millions of ways. Most of those ways will produce the same electoral outcome. A few will not. The art of redistricting is to find the few that do not, and the art of gerrymandering is to find the one among those few that produces the outcome you want, and the art of concealment is to make that one look like all the rest.

Thomas's district looked like all the rest. It satisfied every neutral criterion — equal population, contiguity, respect for county lines where feasible. It also ensured that the incumbent would win by twelve points. The twelve points were not in the criteria. They were in the shape.

Piece #1148: The Wasted Vote

Thomas's mother voted in the 2024 election.

She voted for the challenger. The challenger received 38% of the vote. The incumbent received 62%. Thomas's mother's vote was, in the technical language of redistricting analysis, "wasted" — it did not contribute to electing a representative. Across the creek, in the adjacent district, the same party's candidate won with 84% of the vote. Those voters' margins above 50% were also wasted, but in the opposite direction: packed into an overwhelming victory that converted enthusiasm into irrelevance.

Stephanopoulos and McGhee formalized this in the "efficiency gap" (University of Chicago Law Review, 2015). The difference between each party's wasted votes, divided by total votes cast, produced a single number measuring partisan asymmetry. The number was elegant. The Supreme Court found it insufficient. In Rucho v. Common Cause (2019), five justices ruled that no standard — not the efficiency gap, not any other metric — could distinguish permissible redistricting from impermissible gerrymandering.

The ruling did not say that gerrymandering was acceptable. It said that courts could not tell the difference. The inability to tell the difference was, Thomas would later understand, the point. The concealment succeeded when the arbiter declared it undetectable.

Piece #1149: The Mapmaker

The mapmaker was not elected.

The mapmaker was a legislative staffer with a GIS license and access to voter registration files, census block data, and election returns for the previous three cycles. The software displayed population totals in real time as boundaries were adjusted. Move the line one block east: population shifts by 347. Move it one block south: the partisan composition shifts by 1.2 percentage points. The mapmaker worked within constraints — equal population, contiguity, compliance with the Voting Rights Act — and within those constraints, the choices were vast.

The voters would choose the representative. The mapmaker chose the voters.

This was the inversion that Thomas's chart kept demonstrating. The Apgar score was a one-minute triage tool that migrated to courtrooms. The IQ test was a screening device that migrated to a bell curve. The ZIP code was a routing number that migrated to an insurance premium. In each case, the instrument was designed for one purpose and deployed for another, and the original purpose provided the alibi. The electoral district was designed to ensure representation. It was deployed to predetermine it. The constitutional mandate — you must draw districts — provided the alibi: we were just drawing lines.

The lines were the decision. Everything after the lines was ceremony.

Piece #1150: The District

The district was the concealment.

Not the specific district — Thomas's district, with its creek boundary and its twelve-point margin — but the concept. The district presented as a geographic fact: you are here, this is your district, this is your representative. The presentation was accurate. The geography was constructed. The construction preceded the election. The election confirmed the construction.

Elbridge Gerry signed the bill in 1812. The Boston Gazette drew the salamander. Two centuries later, the salamanders were drawn by algorithms, and the Supreme Court had declared that no court could distinguish a salamander from a square, because both were political, and politics was not reviewable.

Thomas's chart was a district. Every measurement placed him inside a boundary: the 10th percentile for weight, the 37th week for gestational age, the zone between observation and intervention. The boundaries were drawn by committees — the AAP, the WHO, ACOG — and the boundaries determined the care he received. The mandate was genuine: you must classify patients. The discretion was in the boundaries: where you draw the lines determines who gets what. The mandate absorbed the discretion.

1150 pieces. The district is the container. The container is the choice. The choice preceded the voter. The voter confirmed the choice.
