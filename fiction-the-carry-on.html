Part CCXXVI: The Carry-On

Piece #1571: The Dimensions

Thomas buys a suitcase labeled "carry-on size." The tag says 22 × 14 × 9 inches. He checks his airline's website. The airline says carry-on bags must not exceed 22 × 14 × 9 inches including wheels and handles. The suitcase's dimensions are the outer shell — excluding wheels and handles. The wheels add two inches of height. The handle housing adds one inch of depth. The bag that fits the label does not fit the airline's specification. Thomas will discover this at the gate, where a metal sizer — the one physical enforcer of the one dimension the airline actually checks — will reject a bag that was sold as the thing it was rejected for being.

Piece #1572: The Airlines

United: 22 × 14 × 9 inches. American: 22 × 14 × 9 inches. Delta: 22 × 14 × 9 inches. Southwest: 24 × 16 × 10 inches. Spirit: 22 × 18 × 10 inches for overhead bin, 18 × 14 × 8 inches for free personal item. Ryanair: 55 × 40 × 20 centimeters for priority, 40 × 20 × 25 for everyone else. The three major US carriers converge on the same number but differ on whether wheels and handles are included, whether enforcement is at the gate or at check-in, and whether the sizer is the standard or the agent's judgment. The luggage industry markets to the most permissive domestic specification. The consumer who buys for a domestic flight and then flies Ryanair to Dublin learns that "carry-on size" is a jurisdiction, not a dimension.

Piece #1573: The Sizer

The metal sizer at the gate — a rectangular frame into which the bag must fit — is the final authority. It measures the bag as a rectangular solid: length × width × depth, all protrusions included. But bags are not rectangular solids. A soft-sided bag compresses. An expandable hard-shell does not. A bag with a front pocket bulges. The sizer says yes or no to a three-dimensional object whose shape changes under compression, at a moment when the gate agent decides whether to enforce. The same bag passes on a half-full Monday flight and fails on a sold-out Friday flight. The standard is the sizer. The sizer is a frame. The enforcement is a human.

Piece #1574: The Inches

IATA Cabin Baggage Guidelines recommend 55 × 35 × 20 centimeters, which converts to approximately 21.7 × 13.8 × 7.9 inches. No airline matches this exactly. The US industry rounds to 22 × 14 × 9. The European carriers use centimeters but vary: Lufthansa 55 × 40 × 23, British Airways 56 × 45 × 25, EasyJet 45 × 36 × 20. The IATA guideline is a recommendation, not a regulation. No authority enforces it. Each airline sets its own limit based on its own overhead bin dimensions, its own revenue model, and its own enforcement appetite. The consumer faces not one specification but dozens, varying by airline, by route, by fare class, and by day.

Piece #1575: The Label

The suitcase tag says "carry-on size." No standard defines this phrase. No regulatory body certifies it. The manufacturer chooses dimensions that fit the most airline specifications in the most markets — typically the major US carriers — and labels the bag "carry-on." The label communicates compliance with something that does not exist: a universal carry-on standard. The consumer trusts the label because it sounds like an answer. The label is a marketing decision, not a measurement, describing fitness to a specification that varies by carrier, by country, by fare class, and by the enforcement mood of the gate agent who decides, at the moment of boarding, whether the bag that was sold as carry-on size will be carried on.
