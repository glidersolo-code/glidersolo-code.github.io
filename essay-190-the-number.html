Essay 190: The Number

Dewey divided knowledge into ten.

In 1876, Melvil Dewey published a classification system for the Amherst College library. Ten main classes — 000 through 900 — with decimal subdivisions infinitely extensible. The system was elegant, practical, and immediately useful. It was also, entirely, a set of decisions: one person's judgment about how knowledge should be organized, made at a particular institution, in a particular year, with particular assumptions about what was central, what was peripheral, what deserved space, and what could be compressed.

Class 200 was Religion. Sections 200-289 were devoted to Christianity. Sections 290-299 covered all other world religions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Islam, Judaism, Sikhism, and every other non-Christian tradition, sharing one-tenth of the class that Christianity occupied nine-tenths of. The allocation reflected the world as seen from Amherst, Massachusetts, in 1876. Subsequent editions expanded non-Christian coverage but the architectural constraint persisted: the numbering system had already allocated the space, and the space determined the resolution. Christianity was detailed; other traditions were compressed.

The system placed psychology under philosophy (150). In 1876, this was conventional. By the 2020s, psychology was an independent discipline, but the Dewey number had not moved. The world had changed around the number, and the number — embedded in 135 countries, translated into 30 languages, printed on millions of spine labels — remained. The persistence was not inertia. It was infrastructure: the number determined the shelf, the shelf organized the building, the building organized the encounter. Moving psychology to its own class would require relabeling every psychology book in every library that used the system. The cost of correction exceeded the cost of the error.

The shelf performs a claim that is invisible as a claim. A patron walking through the stacks encounters knowledge in Dewey's order: philosophy before religion before social sciences before natural sciences before literature before history. The sequence implies a progression. The patron experiences the arrangement as the arrangement of knowledge, because the shelf is the only order available in the physical space. The patron does not think: Dewey decided that philosophy should come before religion. The patron thinks: philosophy is before religion. The classification's decision has become the patron's world.

This is taxonomy-as-neutrality. The classification system's organizational choices — what is near what, what contains what, what is main class and what is subdivision — are experienced as natural categories rather than as decisions. The naturalization is physical: the shelf is there, the books are on it, the arrangement is self-evident. The naturalization is also numerical: the number appears to discover the book's place rather than to assign it. 616.8942 looks like a coordinate — a location in knowledge-space that the system has merely identified. The number is Dewey's. The location is Dewey's. The knowledge is not.

The system works. Books are found, shelves are organized, collections are maintained across 135 countries. The success confirms the system, and the confirmation conceals the decisions. A system that works is a system that is not questioned. A system that is not questioned is a system whose decisions are invisible. A system whose decisions are invisible is a system whose decisions look like facts. The fact is: psychology is at 150. The decision was: Dewey put it there. The distance between the fact and the decision is the width of the concealment that 148 years of successful use have produced.
