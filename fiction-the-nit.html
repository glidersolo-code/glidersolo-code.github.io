Part CCXLVI: The Nit

Piece #1671: The Window

Peak brightness: 2,000 nits. The number is real. The measurement condition is specific: a 10 percent window — a small bright rectangle against a black background, covering one-tenth of the screen area. An OLED panel, where each pixel is its own light source, can drive that small region to 2,000 nits because the surrounding pixels are off, drawing no power, generating no heat. The Automatic Brightness Limiter does not intervene because the thermal load is small. Thomas read the spec sheet. He saw 2,000 nits. He understood: this television produces 2,000 nits. The spec sheet measured a highlight. The consumer watches a scene. For a full-screen bright image — a beach, a snow field, a white interface — the thermal budget forces the ABL to reduce output. Sustained full-screen brightness on the same panel: 200 to 400 nits. The number on the box is the number the panel achieves under a condition the consumer rarely produces.

Piece #1672: The Ratio

Static contrast ratio: the luminance of peak white divided by the luminance of deepest black, measured on the same panel in the same state at the same time. For an LCD with a VA panel: perhaps 3,000:1. Dynamic contrast ratio: peak white at maximum backlight divided by black at minimum backlight, measured at different times — the ratio of two different panel states that never coexist. A million to one. The consumer compares the numbers. 3,000:1 versus 1,000,000:1. The numbers answer different questions. The format is the same — a ratio, a colon, a one. OLED achieves infinite static contrast because its pixels turn fully off, producing true zero luminance. The denominator is zero. The ratio is undefined, reported as "infinite." But the LCD's dynamic million-to-one and the OLED's infinite static appear on adjacent shelf cards as if they share a methodology. They do not share a methodology. They share a format.

Piece #1673: The Signal

HDR10 on the box. The consumer reads: this television does HDR. HDR10 is a signal specification — it describes what the video stream contains, not what the display can reproduce. The signal is encoded with metadata specifying mastering at up to 1,000 or 4,000 nits, a wide color gamut (Rec. 2020), 10-bit depth. The television decodes the signal. Decoding is not reproducing. A 400-nit television receiving a 4,000-nit HDR10 signal tone-maps the highlights downward, compressing the dynamic range the format was designed to preserve. The consumer paid for HDR. The label confirmed HDR. The display delivers a compressed approximation of HDR, because the signal specification and the display capability are different measurements reported under the same three letters. Thomas's television says HDR10. The television can decode HDR10. Whether it can honor the signal's intentions is a separate question the label does not address.

Piece #1674: The Room

All specifications are measured in a darkened room. Contrast ratios — whether static, dynamic, or infinite — assume the black level is determined by the display alone. The consumer watches in a room with windows, lamps, overhead fixtures. Ambient light reflects off the screen surface, adding luminance to every pixel including the pixels that are off. An OLED pixel emitting zero light in a dark room emits zero light in a bright room, but the screen surface reflects the room's light back to the viewer. The effective black level is no longer the pixel's emission but the room's contribution. Infinite contrast in darkness becomes finite contrast in light. The denominator changed from zero to the room. The specification measured the display. The consumer experiences the display plus the environment. The measurement subtracted the room. The room does not subtract itself.

Piece #1675: The Shelf

Two hundred and forty-six Parts. Thomas stood in the electronics section. The shelf card said: Peak Brightness 2,000 nits. Contrast Ratio 1,000,000:1. HDR10. He compared it to the next model: Peak Brightness 1,000 nits. Contrast Ratio 3,000:1. HDR10. The first appeared superior on every metric. The first was an OLED measured at a 10 percent window with dynamic contrast in a dark room. The second was an LCD measured at full screen with static contrast in a dark room. The showroom was lit with fluorescent panels that raised the effective black level of both displays to approximately the same value. HDR10 on both boxes meant both could decode the signal; the 400-nit LCD compressed highlights the 2,000-nit OLED could render. Thomas chose the larger numbers. The numbers were accurate. The conditions were not comparable. The room was not the measurement. The signal was not the display. The highlight was not the scene. The dial, having spoken, did not elaborate.
