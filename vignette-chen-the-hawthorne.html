Vignette

The Effect

Chen finds Maren reading Levitt and List's 2011 reanalysis beside the original Relay Assembly Test Room data.

"Five women," Maren says. "Two replaced."

"For insubordination and declining output. In a sample of five, that's a 40% reconstruction of the population mid-experiment."

"And from this came the principle that researchers must control for in every human-subjects study."

Chen scrolls through the illumination data. Productivity fluctuations with no clear pattern relative to lighting changes. "The data don't show it. The conventional story says productivity rose whether lighting increased or decreased. The actual numbers are noise. Noisy, uncontrolled, unpatterned noise."

"But the story left the laboratory in the 1930s."

"And entered the textbook by the 1960s. Landsberger named it in 1958 — twenty-six years after the experiments ended. By then the narrative was the fact. The name just made it official."

Maren opens Adair's 1984 review. "Six different phenomena under one name. Novelty. Awareness. Demand characteristics. Feedback. Special treatment. Experimenter effects. The name performs unity where the mechanisms are diverse."

"That's the consolidation," Chen says. "Six separate hypotheses become one effect. One effect becomes one principle. One principle becomes one line in a methods textbook. And a line in a methods textbook is an axiom — it structures the space in which questions are asked. No one questions the structure."

"McCambridge, Witton, and Elbourne tried in 2014. Systematic review. Evidence: 'inconsistent and weak.'"

"And nothing changed."

"Nothing changed because the finding was already above the layer where evidence could reach it."

The paragraph — thirty-nine lines about how formats conceal, how measurements migrate from empirical to structural — sits in its file. Chen doesn't open it. The paragraph would say something about ascension. About how a finding protects itself from scrutiny by occupying the position from which scrutiny is designed. About how the textbook is the highest ground.

"Finding-becomes-axiom," Chen says. "The sixty-third species."

"The recursion is the mechanism," Maren says. "A finding that isn't reliably supported by evidence becomes a methodological principle. As a principle, it's invoked without testing. Each invocation reinforces it. The reinforcement isn't empirical — it's citational. The finding becomes true through repetition at the right altitude."

"And the altitude is the concealment. At the empirical level, the claim faces scrutiny — show the data, control the confounds, replicate. At the methodological level, the claim is assumed before the data are collected. The ascension moves the finding from where it can be tested to where it cannot."

"The textbook is the highest ground."

"From the textbook," Chen says, "the finding looks down at the data that never supported it and sees nothing to worry about."

Maren closes both documents — the original data and the reanalysis. The narrative remains.
