Part CCCLX: The Week

Piece #2241: The Names

Thomas noticed that no one ever asked why there were seven.

People asked why Tuesday was named after Tiu, or whether Thursday belonged to Thor or Jupiter. They asked about the etymology. They did not ask about the arithmetic. The number itself was invisible — not hidden, not suppressed, but simply beneath the threshold of curiosity, the way no one asks why the floor is down.

He tried to imagine asking the question aloud. Why seven? Not why these names, or why in this order, but why this count. The question felt absurd — like asking why circles are round. The absurdity was the finding.

Piece #2242: The Nundinae

The Romans used an eight-day cycle. The nundinae — the ninth-day market, counted inclusively — organized commerce, agriculture, and legal proceedings. Farmers traveled to the city every eight days. Courts scheduled sessions around the cycle. The rhythm worked.

When the seven-day planetary week arrived from the Eastern Mediterranean, it ran alongside the nundinae for decades. Two cycles, incommensurable, overlapping. Then the seven-day week won — not by argument but by religion. Constantine's edict of 321 made Sunday a rest day. The eight-day cycle faded.

Thomas thought about what was lost: not a schedule but the knowledge that the number was a choice.

Piece #2243: The Color

In 1929, Moscow assigned each citizen a color. Red, yellow, pink, green, purple — one of five. Your color determined your rest day. The factories ran every day. One-fifth of the workforce rested at any given moment. Production was continuous.

But Mikhail could not eat dinner with Anya because she was green and he was pink. Their rest days never coincided. Children played without their parents. Families disintegrated along color lines that were drawn to prevent the disintegration of industrial output.

By 1940, the seven-day week was back. The number had won not against a worse number but against any number that disrupted what the number had built around itself.

Piece #2244: The Astronomer

A colleague once told Thomas that the week had no sky.

Days track the Earth's rotation. Months approximate the Moon's cycle. Years follow the orbit. But the week corresponds to nothing — no planetary period, no tidal cycle, no biological rhythm. It is the only major time unit that is entirely social.

Yet it sits in the calendar alongside the others as if it belongs. Monday, January, 2026. The day name, the month name, the year number — all in a row, as if they are the same kind of thing. The week presents itself as astronomical by occupying an astronomical position. The format does the work.

Piece #2245: The Rhythm

Thomas kept a journal. He wrote in it on Sundays. Not because Sunday was meaningful — any day would have served — but because the rhythm had become the structure. Remove the day, and what replaces it is not a different day but a formlessness that the practice cannot survive.

He understood the French failure now. The décade was reasonable: ten days, three per month, clean decimal logic. But reason had nothing to hold onto. The seven-day week was not held in place by reason. It was held in place by the fact that every habit, every institution, every expectation of when to rest and when to work and when to worship had been built to the dimensions of seven. The rhythm was not a conclusion. The rhythm was an infrastructure. And infrastructure does not answer to arithmetic.

He wrote: The week has no sky. But it has something more durable than a sky — it has a species, the hundred million repetitions that make a convention indistinguishable from nature.
