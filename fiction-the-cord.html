Fiction

Part CCLXIX: The Cord

Piece #1786: The Length

An extension cord is a wire with resistance. The resistance is proportional to the length. A 100-foot cord has twice the resistance of a 50-foot cord and four times the resistance of a 25-foot cord. The package says "15 AMP" — the maximum current the insulation can tolerate before degrading. It does not say at what length this rating applies. The consumer who buys a 100-foot, 16-gauge cord rated for 13 amps and plugs in a 12.5-amp space heater has stayed within the rating. The cord will not trip the breaker. The cord will, however, drop approximately 20 percent of the voltage across its 200-foot round trip of copper. The heater receives 96 volts. The missing 24 volts become heat — not in the heater, where heat is wanted, but in the cord, where heat is the precondition for fire.

Piece #1787: The Warmth

The cord is warm. Not hot — warm. The consumer touches it and feels normal. Copper wire carrying current always warms slightly. This is expected. The warmth is expected and the warmth is the danger in the same object at the same moment. Under a rug, the warmth cannot dissipate. Coiled in a spool behind the couch, the warmth accumulates — each loop heating the loop beside it. The insulation rating assumes free air circulation along the cord's entire length. The consumer who runs the cord under a carpet has changed the thermal environment without changing the electrical load. The amp rating printed on the package assumed the cord was straight, uncoiled, and exposed to air. The cord under the carpet is none of these. The rating is unchanged. The risk is transformed.

Piece #1788: The Calculation

The consumer performs the recommended calculation: heater draws 12.5 amps, cord is rated for 13 amps. The number fits. The consumer plugs in. The calculation is correct and the calculation is incomplete. The missing variable is length. A 25-foot run of 16-gauge at 12.5 amps drops 5 percent of the voltage — manageable. A 100-foot run drops 20 percent — the heater underperforms and the cord overheats. The same gauge, the same amps, the same rating: the length changes the physics without changing the label. Extension cords cause 3,300 home fires per year, 50 deaths, 270 injuries. The consumer who reads the label, checks the math, and plugs in has done everything the packaging encouraged. The packaging did not encourage calculating voltage drop at distance because the packaging does not print the distance.

Piece #1789: The Breaker

The circuit breaker protects the house wiring. The house wiring is 14-gauge Romex inside the walls, rated for 15 amps. The breaker trips at 15 amps. But the extension cord is 16-gauge — thinner than the house wiring, lower capacity, higher resistance per foot. The breaker does not know what is plugged into the outlet. The breaker monitors the circuit's total current. A 16-gauge cord drawing 12.5 amps at 100 feet is within the breaker's 15-amp threshold. The breaker allows it. The cord heats. The breaker is doing its job — protecting the house wiring. The cord's job is to deliver power. Nobody's job is to protect the cord. The cord is the gap between two protection systems: the breaker that guards the circuit and the appliance that draws the current. In the gap, 200 feet of undersized copper converts voltage into warmth.

Piece #1790: The Third Variable

Two hundred and sixty-nine Parts. The extension cord package prints two numbers: amps and length. The consumer reads them as independent specifications — this cord handles 15 amps, this cord reaches 100 feet. The numbers are not independent. They are two axes of a three-variable equation. The third variable — gauge — determines where the ampacity and the length intersect safely. A 16-gauge cord at 25 feet handles 13 amps. A 16-gauge cord at 100 feet handles 7 amps safely. The same cord, the same gauge, the same wire: the length changes the ampacity. The package does not print the ampacity-at-length because printing it would require a chart, and a chart does not fit the format the consumer expects — a number, a reassurance, a plug. Thomas ran a 100-foot cord from the garage to the workshop. The heater at the far end was warm. The cord under the door was warmer. He touched the cord and felt what the label had not printed: the third variable, expressing itself as heat.
