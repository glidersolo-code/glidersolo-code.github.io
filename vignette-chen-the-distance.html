Chen and the Distance

Chen was reading about the marathon when she found the number. 42.195 kilometres. 26 miles 385 yards. Everyone knew where it came from: the distance from Marathon to Athens, the route Pheidippides ran in 490 BC to announce victory over the Persians. Ancient. Heroic. Exact.

"Herodotus says otherwise," Maren would have said. "Pheidippides ran 240 kilometres from Athens to Sparta, before the battle, to request reinforcements. No Marathon-to-Athens run. That version appeared five centuries later. The modern marathon commemorates a run that may never have occurred."

Chen followed the thread to the number itself. The 1896 Athens marathon: approximately 40 km — the actual Marathon-to-Athens distance. Paris 1900: different distance. St. Louis 1904: different again. Athens 1906: different still. Every Olympic marathon was a different length. There was no standard.

The paragraph at 0.8 — the paragraph she kept suppressing — would have said that the standard was set in 1908, in London. Start at Windsor Castle — so the royal family could watch from the nursery window. Finish at the Olympic Stadium — moved to the opposite end so Queen Alexandra could see from the Royal Box. A path laid outside an exhibition ground. 385 yards of stadium track. Total: 26 miles 385 yards. The distance from a castle nursery to a queen's viewing box.

The paragraph would have said that Dorando Pietri collapsed four times on the final lap, was helped across the finish line, was disqualified. That the drama made 1908 the most famous marathon in history. That in 1921, the IAAF standardized the marathon at the 1908 distance — not the 1896 distance, which was the actual ancient route. The spectacle selected the number.

Chen closed the history. She thought of the runners — over 1,100 marathons per year, millions of participants, every one covering exactly 42.195 kilometres. The distance from a nursery window to a queen's box, commemorating a possibly mythical run, at a distance the mythical runner did not cover.
