The Wipe

The front of the bottle says "Kills 99.9% of Germs*." The back says, in the directions for use, "contact time: 4 minutes." The surface must remain visibly wet for four minutes. Not damp. Visibly wet — enough liquid that light reflects.

The consumer sprays the counter, waits three seconds, wipes with a paper towel. Three seconds of a four-minute protocol — 1.25 percent of the required contact time. The efficacy achieved in three seconds is not printed anywhere: not on the bottle, not in the EPA registration, not in any consumer document. The 99.9% belongs to the four minutes. The consumer's three seconds belong to something else.

On a warm counter, the spray begins to evaporate within 30 to 60 seconds. Maintaining the wet film for four minutes would require reapplication — spraying again, keeping the surface wet. The bottle says "spray surface until thoroughly wet." The consumer sees wetness, treats it as the protocol's entirety, and wipes.

The front of the bottle and the back of the bottle are different documents. The front is for the purchaser: the person in the aisle comparing 99.9% to 99.9% across brands, choosing the one that claims the most. The back is for the applicator: the person at the counter following directions. The purchaser and the applicator are the same hand. The front is written in marketing type. The back is written in regulatory type. The consumer reads the marketing type because the marketing type is on the front, and the front faces the aisle.

The average case forgives the shortcut. Most consumers who spray and wipe in five seconds do not get sick, because the contamination was light and the partial kill was sufficient. The four-minute protocol was designed for the worst case. The consumer lives in the average case. The forgiveness reinforces the practice. The practice never meets the claim. The claim never needs the practice — because the claim was tested in a lab, on the back of the bottle, in four minutes that the kitchen never provides.
