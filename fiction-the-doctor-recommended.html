Fiction The Doctor Recommended

Fiction

Part CCCXXIV: The Recommendation

Piece #2061: The Survey

Thomas stood in the pharmacy aisle. Two boxes of allergy medication. Same molecule: cetirizine. Same dose: 10mg. One box: $14.99, store brand. The other: $22.99, name brand. The name brand said: #1 Allergist Recommended.

He thought about the survey. A marketing research firm had contacted allergists nationwide. The question: "Which brand of antihistamine do you recommend most often to patients for seasonal allergies?" The question asked for a brand. Not a molecule. Not a generic. A brand.

An allergist who says "take cetirizine" — the molecule, generic, whatever's cheapest — could not express that recommendation within the survey's format. The question demanded a brand name. The doctor named one. The survey counted the name. The label reported the count.

Piece #2062: The Plurality

Thomas had looked up the numbers once, in an FTC filing. In one "#1 doctor recommended" survey: 312 physicians surveyed. 87 named the brand (28%). 63 named the leading competitor (20%). 42 named a third option (13%). The rest scattered.

Twenty-eight percent. Not a majority. Not a consensus. A plurality — the single largest number in a fragmented field. The label said "#1." The survey said 28%. The other 72% recommended something else.

Thomas picked up the $14.99 box. Same molecule. Same dose. Same manufacturer, in some cases — the name brand and the store brand coming off the same production line, the same tablets, different boxes. The "#1" recommended the box. The molecule was the medicine.

Piece #2063: The Voice

The label performed a voice. Not a survey response — a voice. The consumer heard: "I, a physician, recommend this product to you." Personal. Authoritative. Individual. The voice of clinical judgment, delivered at the shelf.

But the doctor had not spoken to the consumer. The doctor had checked a box. The survey had been commissioned by the brand. The marketing agency had designed the question. The question had constrained the answer. The answer had been counted. The count had produced a plurality. The plurality had been labeled "#1." And the "#1" had been printed on the box as a recommendation.

Between the doctor's checkbox and the consumer's ear: five intermediaries, each performing a transformation. The checkbox became a count. The count became a rank. The rank became a claim. The claim became a voice. The voice became trust.

Piece #2064: The Question

Thomas thought about what the survey couldn't ask. It couldn't ask: "Do you believe brand-name cetirizine is superior to generic cetirizine?" (Most allergists would say no — bioequivalent.) It couldn't ask: "Would you recommend this brand if the patient asked about cost?" (Most would recommend generic.) It couldn't ask: "Is brand preference or molecule preference more relevant to patient outcomes?" (Molecule, always.)

The survey asked the question that produced the answer the label needed. A different question would produce a different answer. The methodology was not neutral. It was an instrument — designed to generate a specific output that could be printed on a box.

Thomas had seen this before. In the evidence hierarchy that ranked methods by amenability to the gold standard. In the evidence itself — the question determining the answer, the methodology determining the conclusion. The survey was a measurement instrument. Like all measurement instruments, it measured what it was designed to measure, and concealed what it was not designed to ask.

Piece #2065: The Box

He bought the store brand. $14.99. Same molecule. Same dose. No survey. No "#1." No doctor's voice on the box.

Walking out, Thomas passed a display: #1 Pediatrician Recommended Vitamin. #1 Dentist Recommended Toothbrush. #1 Dermatologist Recommended Moisturizer. Each "#1" was a survey. Each survey was a question. Each question was designed by the brand. Each answer was a plurality. Each plurality was a label. Each label was a voice.

The pharmacy was full of doctors who had never spoken. Their authority was on every shelf — extracted by surveys, compressed into numbers, printed on boxes. The doctors had checked boxes. The boxes carried their voices. The voices said: buy this. The doctors had said: checkbox.

Thomas took his $14.99 cetirizine and went home.
