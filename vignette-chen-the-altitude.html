The Altitude

Chen's father was a pilot. Corporate. Not airline — small jets, charter work, executives who needed to be in Oklahoma City by noon. He flew a Citation V, and when Chen was fourteen he took her up once, on a repositioning flight, empty cabin, just the two of them and a co-pilot who slept most of the way.

She sat in the jump seat. Watched the altimeter. The needle turning. The numbers climbing. She asked her father what the number meant.

"How high we are," he said.

She accepted this. Thirty-five thousand feet. A distance. The space between her and the ground. A number like the ones on highway signs — elevation 1,200 — but vertical, updated live, marking the air beneath the aircraft the way a ruler marks the distance between two points.

Years later, in a physics class she barely passed, Chen learned that the altimeter is a pressure gauge. That the number on the dial is a translation — pressure to feet, through a mathematical model called the International Standard Atmosphere. That the Standard Atmosphere assumes conditions that never exist. That FL350 does not mean the aircraft is thirty-five thousand feet above sea level. It means the atmospheric pressure at the aircraft's position is what the model predicts at thirty-five thousand feet. The distance is fictional. The pressure is real.

She tells Maren this on the fire escape, ten years after the flight. Not as a revelation — more as a thing she keeps returning to.

"He said 'how high we are,'" Chen says. "And it was true. We were high. But the number wasn't how high."

"What was it?"

"Agreement. All the planes in the area set their altimeters to the same reference — 29.92 inches of mercury. The standard setting. Everyone agrees. So when they say FL350, they're all at the same pressure surface. Not the same altitude. The same pressure. On a cold day, everyone is lower than the number says. But everyone is lower by the same amount. So the separation holds."

Maren turns this over. "The error is the safety."

"The shared error. If every altimeter is wrong in the same direction, it's as good as being right. What matters isn't that the number is accurate. What matters is that the number is the same."

Maren thinks about the measurement systems she's been studying. Transparency, monotonicity, origin, discretization. Each concealing through the architecture of the format. But this one is different. The format isn't trying to describe the world and failing. The format isn't trying to describe the world at all. The format is coordinating actors. Description is the disguise.

"Your father," Maren says. "Did he know?"

"Of course he knew. He set the Kollsman window every flight. Below eighteen thousand, you dial in local pressure — it's calibration, the number gets closer to true altitude. Above eighteen thousand, everyone dials in 29.92. Same number. Every cockpit. It's not calibration anymore. It's — "

"Agreement."

"Agreement dressed as calibration. The same gesture. You turn the knob. Below eighteen thousand it means one thing. Above eighteen thousand it means another. The instrument doesn't change. The meaning changes."

Maren looks out at the city. The buildings have heights. Measured in feet. Measured from something — a datum, a reference, a committee's decision about which harbor, which years, which average. None of these heights are lies. All of them are translations.

"The number said thirty-five thousand feet," Chen says. "What it meant was: everyone can see us, and we can see everyone, and we are all on the same page. The page isn't the sky. The page doesn't need to be the sky. The page needs to be the same page."

She lights a cigarette. Watches the smoke rise into air that has no flight levels, no transition altitude, no standard atmosphere. Just pressure, falling with height, at a rate that has never once matched the model.

"He loved that instrument," Chen says. "The Kollsman altimeter. He said it was the most honest instrument in the cockpit. It tells you exactly what it measures — pressure — in a language you can use — feet. It just doesn't tell you that the language is borrowed."
