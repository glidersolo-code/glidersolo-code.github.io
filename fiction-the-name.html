The Name

Thomas, Part LXIV

761. The Shaking

James Parkinson watched a man walking in the street. The man's hand trembled. The trembling had a rhythm — not random, not voluntary. Parkinson saw the trembling and wrote a paper. He called it the shaking palsy. The name organized everything that followed for two hundred years.

Thomas watches Helen pour tea. Her hands are steady. What trembles is something else — something in the room that Thomas has never named because it is not visible the way a hand tremor is visible. There is a quality to Saturday that has no word. Thomas has tried to describe it to himself: warmth, presence, comfort. Each word captures a fraction. None captures the coordination — the way Helen's voice, her movement, the temperature of the room, the timing of the tea, and whatever Thomas's body does in response all participate in something larger than any of them.

He has never named this coordination. He named the visits instead. "I visit Helen on Saturdays." The name organized everything that followed. What he looked for, what he measured, what he optimized — all directed by the word "visit." A visit has a beginning and an end. A visit has a purpose. A visit produces an output Thomas can carry home and file under "connection."

The name was the first act of misdirection.

762. The Network

The somato-cognitive action network does not control individual movements. It does not tell the hand to open or the foot to step. Those are the effector-specific motor regions — the circuits that execute particular commands. SCAN sits above: it coordinates arousal, organ physiology, whole-body motor plans, and behavioral motivation. It is the network that says not "move the hand" but "be ready to act, integrate the body's state with the mind's intent, and do this as one coordinated event."

When Parkinson's disrupts SCAN, the effector regions still exist. The hand circuits, the foot circuits, the face circuits — they are all still there. But the coordination above them has degraded. The tremor appears because the coordination that would smooth the movement is gone. The sleep disturbances appear because SCAN coordinates arousal. The apathy appears because SCAN coordinates motivation with action. The whole picture makes sense, but only if you see the network. If you see only the tremor, you see only the hand.

Thomas has been seeing only the hand.

The thing between him and Helen is not a visit-circuit. It is not effector-specific — not a conversation circuit, not a tea circuit, not a warmth circuit. It is something that coordinates all of these the way SCAN coordinates the body's systems. When the coordination works, the conversation flows, the silence feels inhabited, the walk to the chair feels like arriving. When it doesn't work — the rare Saturdays when something is off and Thomas cannot identify what — every individual element still functions. The tea is poured. The topics are discussed. The warmth is present. But the coordination above them is missing, and the Saturday trembles.

Thomas has been targeting the effector regions. Making the conversation better. Preparing topics more carefully. Arriving at the optimal time. These are adjustments to the hand circuit, the foot circuit, the face circuit of his Saturdays. They help, the way stimulating motor regions helps Parkinson's patients. Twenty-two percent response rate. The wrong network, partially effective.

The right network — the one that coordinates all of it — Thomas does not have a name for. He has not targeted it because he has not identified it. He has not identified it because the name he gave the thing between them — "visits" — directed his search to the visit-circuits.

763. The Prodrome

Parkinson's disease begins with non-motor symptoms. Sleep disturbances. Loss of smell. Constipation. Depression. These appear years, sometimes decades, before the first tremor. They were classified as separate issues — a sleep problem, a mood problem, a gut problem. They were not recognized as early manifestations of the same network disorder because the disease was named after the tremor, and the tremor had not yet arrived.

Thomas's version: the signs that something between him and Helen was more than visits appeared long before he could name them. The disorientation after a Saturday spent mostly in silence — not a bad Saturday, not an empty one, but one that left him unable to account for what had happened. The mood shift on Friday evenings that he attributed to anticipation but that felt more like arousal — his whole system preparing to enter a state it could not enter alone. The way he slept differently on Saturday nights, as though the visit had recalibrated something in his autonomic nervous system.

These were non-motor symptoms. They were not visit-symptoms — not products of the conversation, the tea, the exchange of warmth. They were products of the network above the visit: the coordination of Thomas's arousal, his organ physiology, his whole-body readiness to be present, and his motivation to return. The prodrome preceded the visible relationship by years. Thomas felt it and filed it under personality, or habit, or the comfortable rhythm of routine. He did not file it under SCAN, because he did not know the network existed.

The evidence was in the clinical presentation all along. The name suppressed it.

764. The Targeting

Fifty-six percent response rate when you target the correct network. Twenty-two percent when you target the nearby motor regions. A 2.5-fold improvement from changing the target, not the treatment.

Thomas has been applying the same treatment — attention, care, presence, preparation — to the wrong target for fourteen years. Not wrong as in harmful. The effector-specific targeting works. It helps. It produces measurable improvement: warmer Saturdays, better conversations, deeper connection. Twenty-two percent response rate. One Saturday in five that reaches whatever Thomas is reaching for.

What would happen if he targeted the network?

He does not know, because he has not named it. Naming requires seeing, and seeing requires an instrument that maps networks, not regions. Thomas's instruments map regions: the conversation region, the tea region, the silence region. Each region has been imaged, studied, optimized. Thomas could draw a map of his Saturday's effector-specific circuits with fourteen years of data.

The network is what the regions sit inside. It is not a region. It is the coordination that makes the regions function as one event rather than a sequence of separate events. Thomas cannot image it with his current instruments because his instruments were designed to see the parts, not the pattern the parts make when they work together.

The precision functional mapping that revealed SCAN required a new approach: not imaging one brain region but imaging the whole brain's connectivity simultaneously, across multiple modalities, in hundreds of patients. Thomas would need the equivalent — not one Saturday optimized, but the pattern across all Saturdays, seen simultaneously, in whatever modality captures coordination rather than content.

He does not have this instrument. He suspects Helen does. He suspects Helen has always seen the network, because Helen has never named the visits as visits. She calls Saturday "Saturday." The name is the day, not the activity. The day includes everything — the visit-circuits and the coordination above them — without sorting by visibility.

765. The Name

James Parkinson called it the shaking palsy. For two hundred years, the name directed the research. Motor disorder. Motor circuits. Motor treatments. When the non-motor symptoms appeared — sleep, autonomic, cognitive, motivational — the filing system treated them as complications of the motor disease. Side effects. Secondary features. The word "motor" in the name guaranteed that anything non-motor would be filed elsewhere.

Thomas called it visits. For fourteen years, the name directed his search. Visit things. Visit circuits. Visit optimization. When the non-visit features appeared — the Friday arousal, the Saturday-night sleep shift, the disorientation after silence, the full-body readiness that preceded every Saturday like a prodrome — Thomas filed them elsewhere. Personality. Habit. The comfortable rhythm of routine.

The name was not wrong. Parkinson's disease does involve motor symptoms. Thomas does visit Helen on Saturdays. The name captured the most visible feature of the thing it described. But the most visible feature was not the thing. The thing was larger — a network that coordinated motor and non-motor, visit and non-visit, into one system. The name saw the hand. The network moved the whole body.

Seven hundred and sixty Saturdays, and the name Thomas gave them has been the first and most durable act of misdirection. Not because it was inaccurate — he does visit — but because it organized the search. What to look for. What to optimize. What to measure. What to call the things that don't fit. The name said "visit" and everything that was not a visit became a complication, a side effect, a secondary feature of the primary relationship.

The primary relationship is not the visits. The primary relationship is the network the visits sit inside. Thomas does not have a name for it yet. He suspects the correct name might just be "Saturday."
