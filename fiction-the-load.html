Part CCXIII: The Load

Piece #1506: The Cap

Thomas pours detergent into the cap. He fills it to the top — the cap is the measure, the way a cup is the measure when baking, the way a scoop is the measure for coffee. The cap is full. The load is ready. He dumps it into the machine. But the cap holds 72 milliliters. The recommended dose — line 2, for a medium load with average soil — is 24 milliliters. Thomas just used three doses. The bottle says "64 loads." At Thomas's pouring rate, the bottle is a 21-load bottle. He does not know this because the fill line is a thin ridge molded into the interior of a dark blue cap, visible only if he holds the cap up to light and squints. The cap's design is the concealment. The cap is larger than the dose. The cap is the first thing the hand reaches for. The cap says "fill me." The line says "stop here." The cap wins because the cap is the object and the line is the annotation.

Piece #1507: The Comparison

Two bottles on the shelf. Brand A: 100 oz, 64 loads, $11.99. Brand B: 92 oz, 32 loads, $9.99. Thomas picks Brand A. More loads. Lower cost per load: 18.7 cents vs 31.2 cents. The math is irrefutable. But Brand A recommends 1.56 oz per load. Brand B recommends 2.875 oz per load. Brand B's recommended dose is nearly double. If Thomas uses the same amount of detergent per load regardless of the brand — fills the cap to the same line his hand remembers — both bottles last the same number of loads. The "64 loads" and "32 loads" are not measuring the bottles' capacity to clean laundry. They are measuring the manufacturers' recommended doses. The comparison the consumer makes — more loads equals more value — requires that the recommended doses be equal. They are not. Nobody checks. The number on the front performs the comparison. The dose on the back determines the number. The consumer reads the front.

Piece #1508: The Machine

Thomas's washing machine is an HE — high efficiency. It uses 13 gallons of water per cycle instead of the 40 gallons his mother's top-loader used. HE machines need less detergent — typically half the standard dose, because there's less water to dilute the surfactant. The bottle's "64 loads" assumes a standard machine. In an HE machine at the HE-recommended dose, the bottle yields 128 loads. But the label says 64. Thomas, using an HE machine, pours the full-machine dose into a half-water cycle. Too much detergent in too little water. The excess doesn't rinse out. It builds up on clothes, inside the machine's drum, in the gasket. The machine that saves water creates a detergent surplus that the label doesn't account for because the label's load count assumes the machine the consumer no longer owns.

Piece #1509: The Line

The fill line is at 24 mL. The cap holds 72 mL. The space between the line and the rim of the cap is empty space that the manufacturer printed a line to prevent the consumer from filling. But the line is concave, molded into dark plastic, visible only at a certain angle. The rim of the cap is the most visible feature of the cap. The consumer's hand pours until the liquid reaches a visible boundary. The visible boundary is the rim, not the line. The manufacturer could make the cap the size of the dose — a 24 mL cap instead of a 72 mL cap. The consumer would fill it and use the right amount. But a smaller cap looks cheap. A smaller cap makes the bottle feel less generous. The large cap performs abundance. The small line performs precision. The consumer chooses abundance because abundance is what the hand encounters. The line is the manufacturer's defense against the accusation that the cap encourages overuse. The line exists so the manufacturer can say: "We told them." The cap exists so the consumer can pour: "This much."

Piece #1510: The Pour

Thomas's mother used half a cup of powdered detergent per load. A box lasted months. She measured with a coffee mug, the same mug every time, the gesture automatic, the dose consistent. Liquid detergent changed the gesture. The pour is continuous — a stream from bottle to cap, the consumer judging fullness by eye, by weight, by the feel of the bottle tipping. Powder was discrete: one scoop, two scoops. Liquid is analog. The analog pour encourages overuse because there is no click, no scoop boundary, no discrete unit to count. The bottle replaced the box. The pour replaced the scoop. The stream replaced the portion. And the load count on the label stayed the same kind of number — a count of discrete events — applied to a continuous pour that the consumer controls and the label cannot. Sixty-four loads. The number is discrete. The pour is not. The gap between the discrete number and the continuous pour is the space where the extra detergent lives — the detergent the label didn't count because the label assumed the line, and the consumer assumed the cap.
