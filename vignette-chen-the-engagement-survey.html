Vignette Chen The Engagement Survey

Vignette

The Survey

Chen fills out the engagement survey. Question five: "My supervisor, or someone at work, seems to care about me as a person."

Chen's supervisor approved Chen's vacation request without comment. Chen's supervisor forwarded a birthday email from HR. Chen's supervisor has never asked about Chen's workload, which has increased 40% since two team members left and were not replaced.

Chen selects 3.

"What does caring mean in this context?" Maren asks.

"It means whatever I think it means. That's the problem. Twelve people on my team will each interpret 'cares about me as a person' differently. The scores will be averaged. The average will be reported as a team engagement number."

"And the number measures —"

"How we feel about our manager's interpersonal behavior. It does not measure the headcount freeze. It does not measure the pay freeze. It does not measure the fact that three of us rearranged childcare when the schedule changed in October."

The paragraph is thirty-nine lines long. In it, Chen argues that the twelve questions perform a specific displacement: they translate structural conditions (understaffing, stagnant pay, schedule instability) into interpersonal perceptions (does someone care, do opinions count, is there a best friend). The translation is the concealment. The action plan that follows the survey can only address what the survey measured. The survey measured the interpersonal surface. The action plan will address the surface. The depth — the conditions — will remain.

"The survey IS the intervention," Chen says.

"The asking IS the listening," Maren replies.

Chen submits. The 3 enters the system. Next year the survey will return. The twelve questions will be the same. Chen's conditions will be the same. Chen's score will be the same. The organization will have listened, again, to the same twelve questions that do not ask about the things that would change the answers.
