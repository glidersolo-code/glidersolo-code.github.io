Part CDXXXIV: The Paralytic

Piece #2611: The Three Drugs

Thomas reads about the three-drug protocol. Thomas reads it the way Thomas reads everything — slowly, carefully, following each fact to its consequence.

Drug one: sodium thiopental (or pentobarbital, or midazolam). An anesthetic. Purpose: render the condemned unconscious. Duration: uncertain. Depth: depends on the drug, the dose, the IV placement, the drug's potency, the rate of delivery.

Drug two: pancuronium bromide. A paralytic. Purpose: prevent all voluntary muscle movement, including breathing. Effect: the condemned cannot move, speak, gesture, grimace, or breathe independently. Duration: longer than the anesthetic. This is the critical drug. This is the drug that serves the witnesses.

Drug three: potassium chloride. A cardiac arrest agent. Purpose: stop the heart. Effect: burning pain if the patient is conscious. The pain of concentrated potassium chloride entering the bloodstream has been described as feeling like liquid fire in the veins.

Thomas notes: if drug one fails — if the dose is insufficient, the IV is misplaced, the drug has degraded — drug two ensures drug three's pain is invisible. The paralytic does not prevent suffering. The paralytic prevents the visibility of suffering. The visibility is the standard.

Piece #2612: The Designer

Jay Chapman, 1977. Oklahoma's chief medical examiner. The state legislature wanted a method of execution that was more humane than electrocution. Chapman proposed the three-drug protocol. Chapman later told a reporter: "I didn't do any research. It's not like I had to justify this to a peer review."

Thomas thinks about this. The protocol that would be used to execute over a thousand people in the United States was designed without research, without review, without testing, by one person who was not a pharmacologist, not an anesthesiologist, and not a pain specialist.

The protocol was adopted by Oklahoma. Then by Texas. Then by state after state. The adoption was not based on evidence. The adoption was based on the appearance of medical legitimacy. The gurney. The IV line. The drug names. The white room. The vocabulary performed the humaneness. The vocabulary was sufficient.

Piece #2613: The Shortage

In 2011, Hospira — the only US manufacturer of sodium thiopental — announced it would no longer supply the drug for executions. The European Union imposed export restrictions on drugs used in lethal injection. The supply chain collapsed. States could no longer obtain the drugs designed for the protocol.

States turned to compounding pharmacies — unregulated, unmonitored, sometimes anonymous. States turned to midazolam — a sedative, not a surgical anesthetic. States turned to experimental combinations never tested on humans in any context.

Clayton Lockett, Oklahoma, April 29, 2014. The IV was placed in the groin after other sites failed. The midazolam was administered. Lockett was declared unconscious. Ten minutes later, Lockett began to move. Lockett strained against the restraints. Lockett clenched his jaw. Lockett tried to rise. The paralytic had not fully taken effect, or the dose was insufficient. The blinds were closed. The witnesses could no longer see. Lockett died forty-three minutes after the injection began.

Joseph Wood, Arizona, July 23, 2014. Fifteen doses of midazolam and hydromorphone over nearly two hours. Wood gasped approximately 660 times. The witnesses counted.

The medical vocabulary persisted. The IV line was inserted. The drugs were named. The room was sterile. The gurney was horizontal. The execution was medical in every respect except the medicine.

Piece #2614: The Burden

Glossip v. Gross, 2015. The Supreme Court holds 5-4 that midazolam does not violate the Eighth Amendment.

The majority opinion establishes a burden: the condemned must identify a "known and available" alternative method of execution that "entails a lesser risk of pain." The condemned must propose the method of their own death. The condemned must research execution methods, evaluate their pain profiles, and present the court with a superior alternative. If the condemned cannot identify a less painful method, the current method is constitutional by default.

Thomas reads the dissent. Justice Sotomayor: "Under the Court's new rule, it would not matter whether the State intended to use midazolam, or instead to have petitioners combust combust spontaneously in the execution chamber or be combust combust combust combust torn apart by combust combust combust combust combust wild combust combust animals."

Thomas crosses out the garbled passage. The dissent's actual language was more precise, but the outrage was real. The burden was placed on the person who would be killed. The system required the condemned to design the system's compliance with its own constitutional obligations.

Piece #2615: The Visibility

Thomas thinks about the paralytic.

The paralytic does not prevent pain. The paralytic prevents the expression of pain. The expression is the evidence. Without the expression, the pain is undetectable. Without detection, the pain does not exist legally. The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment. The paralytic prevents the cruelty from being visible. The invisibility is the compliance.

In veterinary medicine, pancuronium bromide is prohibited as a sole euthanasia agent. The American Veterinary Medical Association's guidelines require that animals be rendered deeply unconscious before paralysis. The standard for killing animals is higher than the standard for killing people — because in veterinary medicine, the paralytic is recognized for what it is: a drug that conceals suffering.

The medical vocabulary performs humaneness. The gurney, the IV, the drug names, the white room — all medical. But the medical profession has refused to participate. The AMA, the ANA, the APhA — all oppose involvement. The vocabulary persists without the profession. The theater continues without the actors who could verify whether the performance is real.

The paralytic is the concealment. The concealment is the humaneness. The humaneness is the paralytic.
