The Bill

The tax bill says $4,200. The neighbor's says $840. The houses are identical — same floor plan, same lot size, same school district, same distance from the firehouse whose budget the tax funds. One owner bought in 2022. The other bought in 1985. The bill describes the house. The bill has never described the house.

In California, Proposition 13 froze every property's assessed value at its 1975-1976 level and permitted only two percent annual increases regardless of what happened to the market. Reassessment occurs only when the property changes hands. The word "assessment" — from the Latin assidere, to sit beside — implies that someone sat beside the property and formed a judgment. No one sits. The computer adds two percent to last year's number. The format performs judgment. The content performs arithmetic on a fixed date.

The result is a system in which the assessed "value" is a purchase date written in currency notation. Two identical adjacent properties carry different values that reflect nothing about the properties and everything about when the owners entered the market. The bill is present-tense. The determination is always past-tense.

But the temporal freeze is only the visible layer. Beneath it, in jurisdictions with and without acquisition caps, Christopher Berry reviewed forty million properties and found that the lowest-value homes are assessed at ratios twice as high as the highest-value homes. The errors are not random. They flow downhill. In Detroit, the average home sold in 2010 was assessed at eleven times its sale price. In Cook County, $2.2 billion was shifted from wealthy neighborhoods onto poor ones during a single assessor's tenure. The system's errors had a direction, and the direction was regressive, and the regressivity was invisible because the format — assessed value times tax rate equals tax owed — is identical for every property.

The uniformity clause appears in nearly every state constitution. Similar properties shall bear similar burdens. The clause is not enforced by the format of the bill. The format enforces the appearance of uniformity while the content distributes inequality. Five hundred billion dollars a year flows through a system that performs assessment while encoding acquisition, that performs uniformity while distributing regressivity, that sits beside nothing while billing everyone.

The numbers on the bill describe the house. The house was never asked.
