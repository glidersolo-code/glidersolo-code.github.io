The Meridian

Chen visited Greenwich on a school trip. Year 8. The coach from South London took forty minutes. The Royal Observatory sat on the hill, the Thames visible below, the skyscrapers of Canary Wharf across the water.

The brass line was set into the courtyard. A steel strip, actually — shining, flush with the stone. A queue of tourists waited to stand on it. One foot east. One foot west. The hemispheres divided by a strip of metal. Chen waited her turn. When she reached the line, she stood on it, one trainer on each side, and her friend took the photograph.

"That's the prime meridian," the teacher said. "Zero degrees longitude. Every map in the world is measured from this line."

Chen looked at the strip. It seemed so certain. A line in the ground. A fact.

In university — not geography, but political theory — she read about the 1884 conference. Twenty-five nations in Washington, D.C. The British argued: most ships already use Greenwich. The French argued: the meridian should be neutral. The vote: twenty-two to one. France abstained. France didn't adopt Greenwich until 1911.

"Most ships already use it." Chen circled the sentence. It was true. Two-thirds of the world's shipping navigated by Greenwich charts. But why? Because the British Admiralty produced the best charts. And why the British Admiralty? Because the Royal Navy was the dominant maritime force. The charts followed the power. The convenience followed the charts. And at the conference table, the convenience was presented as a neutral practical fact. The power that created it was absent from the argument.

Chen thought about the paragraph — the one she carried, the one about what people assume versus what is actually happening. The prime meridian looked like a scientific reference point. It was a political decision dressed in practical language. The vote performed the choosing. The choosing had already been done.

Then, later, she read about the offset. She was on the Tube, scrolling her phone. A headline: "The Prime Meridian Is in the Wrong Place." She clicked.

The actual zero longitude — the IERS Reference Meridian, used by GPS, used by WGS84, used by every phone on the planet — was 102 meters east of the brass line at Greenwich. The Airy Transit Circle, the telescope that had defined the meridian since 1851, was not aligned with the center of mass of the Earth. Satellite geodesy measured from the center of mass. The deflection between the two: 5.3 arcseconds. At the latitude of Greenwich: 102 meters.

Chen opened Google Maps. She dropped a pin at 0°0'0" longitude. It landed in the park east of the Observatory. No marker. No brass line. Just grass.

She thought about the photograph from Year 8. She had stood on the line. She had stood in the Western Hemisphere — barely, but measurably. Zero was in the park. Zero was unmarked. The monument performed the meridian, and the meridian had left.

Two concealments, 130 years apart. The first: power presented as convenience, at a conference table in 1884. The second: a monument outlasting the thing it commemorated, visible to anyone with a GPS receiver. The paragraph covered the first. The 102 meters covered the second. And Chen, who had stood on the wrong line at thirteen and learned the history at twenty, understood that the monument was stronger than the coordinate — not because it was more accurate, but because it was more photographable.
