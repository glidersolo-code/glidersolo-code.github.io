The Percent

The Nutrition Facts panel says "Sodium: 460 mg, 20% DV." The asterisk leads to a footnote: "Percent Daily Values are based on a 2,000 calorie diet." Two thousand calories is a political number. When the FDA developed Daily Values in 1993 under the Nutrition Labeling and Education Act, the median caloric intake for American adults was approximately 2,350 calories. The FDA proposed basing the reference on this figure. The food industry argued for a lower number — smaller percentages would look better on labels. Consumer groups pushed for ranges or demographic-specific columns. The FDA settled on 2,000: a round number, below the median, unlikely to be seen as encouraging overconsumption. It is neither the average American intake nor the recommended intake for any specific age, sex, or activity level. It is a compromise, and every percentage on every nutrition label is calibrated to it.

The percentage performs universality. The same "20% DV" is read by a twenty-five-year-old male athlete eating 3,500 calories and a sixty-five-year-old sedentary woman eating 1,600. For the athlete, 460 mg of sodium is roughly 11 percent of his scaled need. For the woman, it is closer to 25 percent. The label tells both of them twenty. The FDA considered demographic-specific Daily Values — separate columns for children, adult women, adult men — and rejected them for complexity. A multi-column label would be more accurate and less readable. The decision to present one percentage was a decision to prioritize comparison over precision: the consumer comparing two products is well served by a common reference, while the consumer assessing personal intake is applying a population reference to an individual body. The label was designed for the first use. The consumer often performs the second.

The FDA's consumer guidance adds another compression: 5% DV or less is "low," 20% or more is "high." The consumer reads 20% sodium and thinks: high. But the "high" threshold is applied to a percentage already based on a political reference. The consumer has passed through three layers of translation: the milligrams compressed to a percentage of 2,000 calories, the percentage compressed to a binary judgment, the judgment compressed to a purchasing decision. Each layer simplifies. Each layer loses resolution. Four hundred sixty milligrams of sodium is a measurement. Twenty percent is a translation. "High" is a verdict. The verdict is three translations from the fact, and the consumer who acts on it is acting on the verdict, not the measurement.

The 2016 update reveals that the panel is curated for the population, not the individual. The FDA removed the requirement to list Vitamins A and C — mandatory since 1993 — because deficiency of these nutrients is no longer common in the US population. Potassium and Vitamin D, whose deficiency is now more prevalent, were added as required. The label tracks the population's nutritional profile and adjusts which nutrients the individual sees. A consumer who is vitamin A deficient will not find the information on labels that dropped the requirement. The population is adequately supplied. The individual may not be. The label says "Your Daily Value." The panel's contents are selected for the population.

The deepest layer is in the pronoun. "Your" Daily Value is not yours. It belongs to a statistical person consuming 2,000 calories, living in a political compromise between industry preferences and regulatory caution, whose nutritional needs were averaged across a population and printed on a label that every body in the country reads as a personal report. The format — a percentage, the most personal of mathematical expressions, a fraction of your whole — performs individual relevance. The denominator is collective.
