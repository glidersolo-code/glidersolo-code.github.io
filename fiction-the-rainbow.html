Part CDXIV: The Rainbow

Piece #2511: The Prism

Thomas held a prism up to sunlight. The spectrum appeared on the wall: a smooth wash of color, red bleeding into orange bleeding into yellow bleeding into green bleeding into blue bleeding into violet. No boundaries. No bands. No lines where one color ended and another began. A continuum.

He counted the colors. How many? It depended on where he decided to draw the lines. Three? Six? Twelve? The spectrum didn't say. The spectrum was continuous. The question "how many colors?" was not a question the light answered.

Thomas opened Newton's Opticks (1704). Newton's answer was seven. Red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. But in Newton's earlier published account (1672), the answer had been five: red, yellow, green, blue, violet. Somewhere between 1672 and 1704, two colors were added. Orange and indigo. Thomas wanted to know why.

Piece #2512: The Scale

The reason was music. Newton believed the color spectrum should correspond to the seven notes of the diatonic scale. He mapped the spectral divisions onto D, E, F, G, A, B, C — placing orange at the half-step between E and F, and indigo at the half-step between B and C.

The correspondence had, as later scholars noted, "no justification in experiment exactly; it just represents something that he's imposing upon the color spectrum by analogy with music." Newton wanted cosmic harmony. Seven days, seven planets (known then), seven notes, seven colors. The number was chosen to fit a system — a system of correspondence, of resonance, of numerological elegance.

Thomas looked at the spectrum on his wall again. Indigo — the color most often questioned. Where does blue end and indigo begin? Most people cannot distinguish indigo from blue. Its presence in the list is Newton's gift to the musical scale, not to optical observation.

Piece #2513: The Mnemonic

ROY G BIV. Thomas learned it in elementary school. Everyone learned it. Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo, Violet. The mnemonic made seven feel inevitable. Seven colors, seven letters, one name.

But the mnemonic didn't help him remember the colors — it taught him how many there were. Before the mnemonic, he might have looked at a rainbow and counted five, or six, or eight. After the mnemonic, the count was locked. Seven. The mnemonic was not a memory aid for a fact. It was the fact. Remove ROY G BIV and the student is left staring at a continuum with no obvious boundaries and no obvious count.

The mnemonic forecloses the question. "Is the rainbow continuous?" ROY G BIV answers: no, it has seven bands. "Could there be six, or five, or twelve?" ROY G BIV answers: there are seven, and here are their names, and here is the mnemonic that proves it. The circularity is perfect: the mnemonic exists because there are seven colors, and there are seven colors because the mnemonic makes seven memorable.

Piece #2514: The Languages

Thomas read about Russian. The Russian language has two basic words for blue: sinij (dark blue) and goluboj (light blue). Not dark and light shades of the same category — two separate categories, as distinct as red and green. Winawer and colleagues (2007) showed that Russian speakers discriminated blue-category boundaries faster than English speakers — the linguistic distinction affected perception itself.

If a Russian counted the colors of the rainbow, would there be eight? Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese also have separate basic terms for light and dark blue. The Shona language has four basic color terms. Some languages may have as few as two: light/warm and dark/cool.

Newton's seven is a post-1704, English-language, musically-motivated count that Western education universalized. ROY G BIV is an English mnemonic for an English physicist's musical analogy, taught as though it describes the physics of light.

Piece #2515: The Continuum

Eight format types, eight questions foreclosed:

Definition forecloses "who was here before?" Count forecloses "how many?" Diagram forecloses "where?" Symmetry forecloses "how does it work?" Percentages foreclose "how do we know?" Shape forecloses "is there an order?" Comparison forecloses "what is this?" Mnemonic forecloses "is this continuous?"

The rainbow is continuous. The light doesn't care about Newton's musical analogy. The spectrum doesn't know it has been divided into seven. The bands are in the mnemonic, not in the physics. ROY G BIV is the partition itself, performing discreteness on a continuum so thoroughly that the continuum is invisible to anyone who learned the name.

Thomas looked at the spectrum on his wall one more time. The colors bled into each other, indifferent to the boundaries Newton had drawn and the mnemonic that preserved them. The light was continuous. The classification was cultural. The mnemonic was the concealment. And the concealment was so complete that most people, looking at a rainbow, believed they were counting rather than remembering.
