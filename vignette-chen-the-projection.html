The Projection

Chen-Maren vignette

Chen's paragraph is a sphere. Thirty-nine lines of argument that curve back on themselves, that connect across dimensions the committee's flat rubric cannot represent. Every reading of the paragraph is a projection — a transformation of the curved surface onto a flat score sheet. Every projection preserves some properties and distorts others. This is not a flaw of reading. It is a theorem.

Maren has been reading about the Mercator projection. Gerardus Mercator, 1569. Designed for navigation: rhumb lines plot as straight lines. To achieve this, the projection preserves angles exactly and distorts area systematically. Greenland appears as large as Africa. The actual ratio is 1 to 14. The trade is guaranteed by Gauss's Theorema Egregium: no flat representation of a curved surface preserves all metric properties. The choice is always which distortion to accept.

Professor Hendricks reads the paragraph with a conformal projection. She preserves the angles — the relationships between ideas, the turns of argument, the way one sentence implies the next. Every local connection is faithfully rendered. But the conformal projection distorts area: Hendricks's reading inflates the paragraph's smaller moments — a qualifying clause, a parenthetical hesitation — into regions as large as the central argument. She sees nuance everywhere. The paragraph appears more complex than it is, because the conformal projection makes every detail occupy the same apparent space.

Professor Wallace reads the paragraph with an equal-area projection. He preserves the magnitudes — the relative weight of each claim, the proportion of evidence to assertion, the sheer scale of the main argument versus the supporting material. Every part occupies its correct proportion. But the equal-area projection distorts angles: Wallace's reading bends the connections between ideas. He sees the sizes correctly but the relationships wrong. The paragraph's central argument appears isolated from its supporting structure because the equal-area projection cannot preserve both how big things are and how they connect.

The committee averages the two projections. The average of a conformal reading and an equal-area reading is not a faithful representation. It is an incoherent composite that preserves neither angles nor area. The committee's score — 3.4 out of 5.0 — is a Robinson projection: a compromise that sacrifices mathematical precision in any property for an aesthetically balanced number. The Robinson projection is the standard for wall maps because it looks right. The committee's score is the standard for evaluation because it feels right. Neither is right in any metric sense. Both are projections, and the distortion lives in the gap between the number and the territory it claims to represent.

Chen does not know which projection the committee used. He knows only the score. He looks at the flat number and tries to reconstruct the sphere.
