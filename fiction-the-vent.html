Part CCLXXIV: The Vent

Piece #1811: The Test

The fan is rated at 80 CFM — 80 cubic feet of air per minute. The test: a controlled chamber, the fan mounted to a short section of smooth duct, the static pressure set to 0.1 inches of water gauge. The fan moves 80 cubic feet of air against a resistance equivalent to pushing air through a straw made of nothing. The number on the box is the output at the test's resistance. The bathroom has a different resistance.

Piece #1812: The Resistance

Fifteen feet of 4-inch flexible duct — the corrugated aluminum tube snaking through the attic. Two 90-degree elbows where the duct turns at the joist and again at the roof penetration. A roof cap with a damper flap. Each foot of duct adds resistance. Each elbow adds resistance. The cap adds resistance. The cumulative static pressure: 0.25 to 0.4 inches of water gauge — two and a half to four times the test condition. At 0.25 in. w.g., the 80 CFM fan delivers 40 to 50 CFM. At 0.4, perhaps 30. The fan works against everything between it and the sky. The box described the fan with nothing between it and the sky.

Piece #1813: The Quiet

The consumer chose the 1.0-sone model because quiet mattered. One sone equals the sound of a quiet refrigerator. Three sones is three times as loud to the human ear. The sone rating was measured at the fan's rated CFM — at 0.1 inches of static pressure. In the bathroom, with 0.25 inches of resistance, the fan may spin faster to compensate — producing more noise at less airflow. Or it may not compensate — producing the rated noise at half the rated airflow. The consumer who selected for quietness has selected a noise number tested at an airflow the ductwork does not permit.

Piece #1814: The Fog

Thomas ran the shower. The fan was on. The mirror fogged. He had installed an 80 CFM fan in an 80-square-foot bathroom — one CFM per square foot, the standard recommendation. The fan delivered 40 CFM through 15 feet of duct with two elbows. Half the recommended ventilation. The mirror fogged because the moisture generation rate exceeded the exhaust rate. The rule said 80. The duct said 40. The mirror said fog. He wiped the mirror and saw his face in the condensation — the outline of a calculation that had been correct on the box and wrong in the attic.

Piece #1815: The Attic

Two hundred and seventy-four Parts. The bathroom fan is rated at a static pressure the bathroom never provides. The duct, the elbows, the cap — everything between the fan and the sky — doubles or triples the resistance the test assumed. The fan's output drops to half. The consumer's bathroom receives half the ventilation the box promised. The fog on the mirror is the visible symptom. The invisible symptom is moisture migrating into the ceiling, the attic, the insulation — mold growth that begins where the ductwork imposed a tax the fan's rating did not include. Thomas installed the fan the box described. The attic installed the duct the box did not describe. The fan and the duct are the same system. The box rated only half of it.
