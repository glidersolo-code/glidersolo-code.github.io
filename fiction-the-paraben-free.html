Fiction The Paraben Free

Fiction

Part CCCXXVI: The Absence

Piece #2071: The Shelf

Thomas stood in the skincare aisle. Every product announced what it lacked.

Paraben-free. Sulfate-free. Silicone-free. Phthalate-free. Aluminum-free. Formaldehyde-free. The labels were lists of absences. Each absence an accusation: the ingredient named was dangerous enough to advertise its removal.

He picked up a moisturizer. The front panel devoted more space to what the product did not contain than to what it did. Five "free from" claims. One active ingredient. The absences outnumbered the presences five to one.

Thomas turned the bottle. The ingredient list was on the back, in six-point type. The "free from" claims were on the front, in fourteen-point type. The hierarchy was clear: what the product lacked was more important than what it contained.

Piece #2072: The Study

One study. 2004. Darbre and colleagues. Twenty breast tumor samples. Parabens detected in the tissue.

Thomas had read the paper. The methodology: detect the presence of parabens in cancerous tissue. The finding: parabens were present. The inference the public made: parabens cause cancer.

But the study had no healthy tissue controls. No comparison to non-cancerous breast tissue. No dose-response curve. No proposed mechanism. The study detected presence. The public inferred causation. The distance between detection and causation was the distance between the study and the label.

Since then: four review cycles by the Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel. Each conclusion: safe as used. The FDA: no evidence of harm at cosmetic concentrations. The EU: safe at established levels. The estrogenic activity observed in laboratory conditions involved doses thousands of times beyond any cosmetic exposure.

The study launched a marketing category. The reviews did not.

Piece #2073: The Replacement

The paraben departed. Something took its place.

Water-containing products need preservatives. Without them, bacteria and fungi colonize the formula within weeks. The preservative is not optional. It is the product's defense against becoming a microbial culture.

The replacement: methylisothiazolinone. MIT. Effective against a broad spectrum of microorganisms. Also: Contact Allergen of the Year, 2013, as named by the American Contact Dermatitis Society. Epidemic levels of contact dermatitis in Europe and the United States, traced directly to MIT in "paraben-free" cosmetics and household products.

Thomas thought about this exchange. The consumer chose "paraben-free" to protect her skin. The replacement gave her contact dermatitis. The label that advertised safety delivered the very harm it promised to prevent — not from the ingredient it removed, but from the ingredient it introduced.

The label named the departure. The arrival went unnamed.

Piece #2074: The Format

"Free from." The format itself was the mechanism.

Every "free from" claim performed two things simultaneously: the safety of the product and the danger of the excluded ingredient. "Paraben-free" said: this product is safe (because it lacks parabens) AND parabens are dangerous (because this product advertises their absence).

The consumer's inference was reasonable. Manufacturers don't advertise the absence of safe ingredients. No product says "arsenic-free moisturizer" because no moisturizer contains arsenic. The "free from" format works because the consumer reasons: if they're telling me this isn't in here, it must be the kind of thing that could be in here, and that would be bad.

The format weaponized the reasonable inference. The consumer's logic was sound. The premise was false. Parabens are safe as used. The label said otherwise — not by stating they were dangerous, but by performing their danger through absence.

Piece #2075: The Reversal

Thomas put the moisturizer back. He picked up another — the one with parabens. Cheaper. Better studied. Preserved with an ingredient reviewed four times by independent toxicologists and cleared each time.

The label said nothing about what it lacked. It listed what it contained. The parabens were there, in six-point type, between the glycerin and the dimethicone.

No "free from" claims. No accusations of absent ingredients. No performance of danger. Just a list of what was present, doing what it was designed to do.

Thomas bought it. The absence of marketing was, in its own way, a kind of honesty.
