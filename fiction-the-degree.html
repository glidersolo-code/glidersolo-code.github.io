Part CXCIV: The Degree

Piece #1411: The Brine

A physicist in Amsterdam in 1724 needs a zero. He is building mercury thermometers and needs the bottom of his scale to be reproducible — a temperature anyone can recreate by following a procedure. He mixes water with ammonium chloride until no more will dissolve. He submerges his thermometer. He marks the mercury's position. Zero.

Thomas's zero was the absence of symptoms. A PHQ-9 of zero meant no depression. But zero was not the absence of suffering — it was the absence of the nine things the instrument measured. A patient could score zero and still feel something the scale had no name for. The zero was arbitrary in exactly the way Fahrenheit's was: not wrong, but chosen. The bottom of the scale was wherever the builder placed it.

The brine solution is a chemical fact. Its freezing point is a physical constant. But choosing it as zero was a decision, not a discovery. The fact that it is reproducible does not mean it is natural. Reproducibility is a property of the procedure, not of the universe.

Piece #1412: The Inversion

Celsius proposed his scale with boiling at zero and freezing at one hundred. The direction was reversed after his death — by Linnaeus or Christin, the historical record is unclear. The scale that bears his name runs opposite to his intention. The convention erased the inventor.

Thomas's instruments underwent similar inversions. The Beck Depression Inventory was revised — BDI became BDI-II. Items were changed. Scoring thresholds were adjusted. The name persisted. Clinicians who learned on the original carried their intuitions into the revision. A score of 20 on the BDI and a score of 20 on the BDI-II were not the same severity. The number was the same. The scale had been inverted beneath it.

No one told the clinicians. No asterisk appeared on the score. The number arrived clean, as though it had always meant what it meant now.

Piece #1413: The Intersection

Fahrenheit and Celsius intersect at minus forty. Below that temperature, Fahrenheit numbers are lower than Celsius numbers. Above it, they are higher. At exactly minus forty, the two scales agree — the only point where they produce the same number from the same phenomenon.

Thomas looked for intersections between instruments. The PHQ-9 and the GAD-7, measuring depression and anxiety respectively, often moved together. A patient scoring 15 on both was experiencing something the instruments split into two conditions. The intersection — the point where both scales read the same — was not a clinical event. It was arithmetic. The instruments happened to cross.

He thought about this: if two different scales, built from different assumptions, produced the same number at the same moment, did that convergence mean something? Or was it just two rulers happening to align at one mark? The intersection at minus forty tells us nothing about temperature. It tells us about the scales.

Piece #1414: The Infrastructure

Three hundred and thirty million Americans experience 72 as comfortable and 32 as freezing. Seven and a half billion others experience 22 as comfortable and 0 as freezing. The numbers are not the weather. The numbers are the vocabulary for weather. The vocabulary, once installed, cannot be changed by argument. It can only be changed by infrastructure — every thermostat, every weather app, every recipe, every medical thermometer, every road sign that displays temperature.

Thomas understood infrastructure. The PHQ-9 was embedded in electronic health records. Changing the instrument would require changing the software, which would require changing the billing codes, which would require changing the training, which would require changing the textbooks, which would require changing the licensing exams. The instrument was not a tool sitting on a shelf. It was a load-bearing wall. You could not remove it without knowing what it held up.

The United States does not use Fahrenheit because Fahrenheit is better. It uses Fahrenheit because Fahrenheit is installed.

Piece #1415: The Degree

The Fahrenheit degree is smaller than the Celsius degree. One Fahrenheit degree is five-ninths of a Celsius degree. This gives Fahrenheit finer resolution without requiring decimals — the difference between 71°F and 72°F is perceptible, while the Celsius equivalent would need a decimal place to capture the same granularity.

Thomas noticed that the PHQ-9's resolution — integers from 0 to 27 — was coarser than the Hamilton's resolution, which ran from 0 to 52. The Hamilton could distinguish between states the PHQ-9 collapsed together. But the PHQ-9 was self-report and the Hamilton required a trained interviewer, so the finer scale was harder to deploy. The coarser scale won not because it measured better but because it fit more contexts.

The degree is not a fact about temperature. It is a fact about the scale. The size of the step determines what the instrument can see. What it can see determines what the clinician can treat. What the clinician can treat determines what the patient is told they have.

The format is the degree.
