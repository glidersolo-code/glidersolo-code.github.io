The At Sign

The oldest known @ appears in a 1345 Bulgarian translation of a Greek chronicle, as a stylized initial letter of "amen." By 1536, Florentine merchants were using it as shorthand for the amphora — a unit of volume for wine pricing. By the eighteenth century, English bookkeepers had adopted it as "at the rate of." The Caligraph typewriter put it on a key in 1882. ASCII standardized it at position 64 in 1963. Unicode named it COMMERCIAL AT. Six centuries of accounting notation, undisturbed.

Then Ray Tomlinson needed a separator.

In late 1971, Tomlinson was adapting a single-machine message program to send text between two computers over ARPANET. The address needed two parts: who and where. He needed a character between them. His criteria: present on the keyboard, not in anyone's name, semantically useful. The @ was available. The preposition "at" did the conceptual work — user at host. The commercial-rate meaning was irrelevant. Tomlinson typed tomlinson@bbn-tenexa, sent a message he could not later recall (likely "QWERTYUIOP or something similar"), and the symbol changed its function for the first time in six hundred years.

The second change took thirty-five years.

On November 2, 2006, Robert Andersen posted on four-month-old Twitter: "@ buzz - you broke your thumb and you're still twittering?" The convention spread through the user community without engineering direction. By May 2008, Twitter made @username a clickable link. By March 2009, the feature was renamed "Mentions." Instagram launched in 2010 with @username built in. The @ was no longer a preposition separating two parts of an address. It was a prefix constituting the address itself. In email, @ means "at" — user at host. On social media, @ means "this" — this person, this identity, this name. The locative function was absorbed. The constitutive function emerged.

Three stages. Rate, address, identity. Each adoption borrowed the symbol because of what it already meant and then changed what it meant. The medieval scribe was abbreviating a unit of weight. Tomlinson was looking for an unused character. Andersen was directing a message. Each choice was pragmatic, local, complete in its moment. None was designing for the next.

But the succession produced something that looks designed. The @ appears to be a natural marker for digital identity — it looks right, fits right, carries the right connotation of directed attention. MoMA agreed: in March 2010, Paola Antonelli added @ to the museum's permanent design collection, describing Tomlinson's repurposing as "a powerful act of design." The museum collected the moment of transformation. What it couldn't collect was the absence of intention that made the transformation possible.

And there is this: the symbol has different names in every language, and almost none reference the commercial origin. Chiocciola (snail) in Italian. Klammeraffe (spider monkey) in German. Sobaka (dog) in Russian. Kukac (worm) in Hungarian. Strudel in Hebrew. Papaki (little duck) in Greek. Zavináč (rolled herring) in Czech. Golbaengi (sea snail) in Korean. Each name was coined from the shape of the glyph when email arrived, not from any prior meaning. The shape preceded the meaning. The shape was universal. The meaning was local, layered, and invisible. A snail, a monkey, a dog, a worm, a duck, a herring — every culture saw a different animal in the curve. None saw an amphora. None saw a rate. None saw a design.

The symbol carries all three meanings simultaneously. The screen shows one. The one it shows — @username, this person — is the only one most people have ever known. It feels inherent. It feels like the symbol was made for this. It was made for wine pricing in the Mediterranean. Everything since has been borrowed.
