Vignette Chen The Imbalance

Vignette

Chen's student raised her hand during the lecture on research methodology. "My doctor said I have a chemical imbalance. Is that a diagnosis?"

Chen paused. "What test did they run?"

"They asked me questions. A questionnaire. Then they said my serotonin was probably low and prescribed an SSRI."

"Did they measure your serotonin?"

The student looked confused. "No. But they said it was a chemical imbalance."

Chen pulled up a slide. "Schildkraut, 1965. 'The Catecholamine Hypothesis of Affective Disorders.' He proposed that depression might be associated with low catecholamines. He wrote — and this is a direct quote — 'a rigorous extrapolation from pharmacological studies to pathophysiology clearly cannot be made.' Coppen shifted the focus to serotonin in 1967. Both were hypotheses. Both were cautious."

She advanced to the next slide. "Moncrieff and colleagues, Molecular Psychiatry, 2022. Umbrella review of the evidence across six domains. Conclusion: 'There is no consistent evidence of there being an association between serotonin and depression.' Tryptophan depletion — artificially lowering serotonin — did not produce depression in 566 healthy volunteers."

The student was quiet for a moment. "Then why did my doctor say that?"

"Because 'chemical imbalance' does four things at once. It implies a known correct balance exists. It implies a deviation has been measured. It implies correction is possible. And it implies the drug provides the correction. None of those are established. No test for chemical imbalance exists. No patient's serotonin has ever been measured before prescribing."

"So it's a metaphor?"

"It's a metaphor that performed a mechanism. Pfizer's Zoloft ad — the one with the sad blob — said: 'Depression is a serious medical condition that may be due to a chemical imbalance, and Zoloft works to correct this imbalance.' The conditional — 'may be due to' — was present. But the animated blob was louder than the hedge."

"Ninety percent of the public believes depression is caused by a chemical imbalance," Chen continued. "That's not a scientific consensus. That's a marketing penetration rate."

The student looked at her prescription bottle. "Does the medication help?"

"That's a different question," Chen said carefully. "The medication may help. The metaphor is wrong. Those can both be true. The drug doesn't have to correct an imbalance to do something useful. But the metaphor makes it impossible to ask what the drug actually does, because the metaphor already answered the question before it was asked."
