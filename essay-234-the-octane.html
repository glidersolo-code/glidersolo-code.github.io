The Octane

In the early 1920s, Thomas Midgley Jr. at General Motors' Dayton Research Laboratories needed a way to measure a gasoline's tendency to knock — to detonate prematurely under compression before the spark plug fires. The solution was a reference scale. Two pure hydrocarbons were selected: isooctane (2,2,4-trimethylpentane), which resisted knocking, was defined as 100. Normal heptane, which knocked readily, was defined as 0. A fuel's octane number was determined by finding the mixture of these two reference compounds that matched the test fuel's knocking behavior in a standardized engine. If the fuel behaved like 87% isooctane and 13% heptane, its octane number was 87. The number described a comparison — how this fuel performed relative to two arbitrarily chosen reference compounds in one engine under specific conditions.

The engine is the Cooperative Fuel Research engine, standardized in 1929 by a committee of oil companies and automobile manufacturers. It is a single-cylinder, variable-compression-ratio engine with a bore of 82.55 mm and a stroke of 114.3 mm. The design is from the 1920s. It is still the standard instrument worldwide. ASTM D2699 specifies the Research Octane Number (RON) test: 600 rpm, 52°C intake temperature. ASTM D2700 specifies the Motor Octane Number (MON) test: 900 rpm, 149°C intake temperature. The same fuel produces different numbers under the two methods. In the United States, the pump displays neither RON nor MON but their average: the Anti-Knock Index, (RON + MON) / 2. In Europe, Australia, and most of the rest of the world, the pump displays RON. The same gasoline — same molecules, same refinery — carries different numbers on different continents. An 87 in the United States is approximately a 91 in Europe. The number is not a property of the fuel. It is a property of which test method the country chose to display.

The labels on American pumps read Regular (87), Midgrade (89), and Premium (91-93). The word "premium" accompanies the highest number. The consumer reads the word and the number and infers: better fuel, more power, superior product. The Federal Trade Commission has stated that using higher-octane fuel than an engine requires provides no benefit. In engines designed for regular fuel, premium fuel provides no additional power, no improved efficiency, no cleaner combustion. The engine's knock sensor never activates, so the additional knock resistance goes unused. A gallon of 87-octane and a gallon of 93-octane gasoline contain approximately the same energy — roughly 120,000 BTU per gallon. The higher-octane fuel resists knocking more effectively. In an engine that does not knock on 87, the difference is metrologically real and functionally invisible. Approximately 16% of U.S. gasoline sales are premium grade; a significant proportion of those vehicles do not require it.

The octane number does not measure energy content, fuel efficiency, engine cleanliness, emissions profile, or driving performance. It measures how the fuel behaves when compressed past the point of spontaneous ignition in a single-cylinder test engine designed ninety-seven years ago, at two different speeds, under two different temperature conditions, averaged together by a convention specific to one country, displayed on a pump under a word — "premium" — that implies quality rather than knock resistance. The number travels from the CFR lab to the refinery specification to the pipeline to the terminal to the tanker to the pump to the yellow label. At each stage, the engine, the method, the reference fuels, and the averaging convention drop away. What arrives at the pump is three digits and a color.

The leaded gasoline era reveals the number's opacity most starkly. Midgley's solution to engine knock was tetraethyl lead, which boosted octane ratings while depositing a neurotoxin in the atmosphere. The Ethyl Gasoline Corporation, formed by Standard Oil and GM in 1924, marketed leaded gasoline for fifty years. Five workers died of lead poisoning at the Bayway, New Jersey plant. Midgley himself took a leave of absence for lead poisoning. The octane number during the leaded era measured knock resistance — the same property, the same scale, the same three digits on the pump — without reflecting the neurotoxin that produced the resistance. After the Clean Air Act and the introduction of catalytic converters (which lead destroys), leaded gasoline was phased out by 1996. Blood lead levels in American children dropped by over 75%. The octane number on the pump in 1970 and the octane number on the pump in 2000 were the same kind of measurement — same scale, same reference compounds, same engine. The chemistry behind the number had changed entirely. The number persisted while the substance it described was transformed. Three digits on a yellow label, the output of a 1920s test engine, averaged by a country-specific convention, displayed under a word that implies quality, measuring a property the consumer's engine may never test, the number a property of the method dressed as a property of the fuel.
