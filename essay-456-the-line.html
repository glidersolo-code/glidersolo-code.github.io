The Line

The equator is physics. The plane perpendicular to the Earth's rotational axis, determined by the spin of the planet, invariant regardless of who measures it or where they stand. The equator would be the equator if no one had ever named it.

The prime meridian is not physics. Every point on the Earth's surface is equally valid as zero longitude. The equator has a derivation. The prime meridian has a decision. And the decision, like most decisions that now look inevitable, was the product of power that learned to present itself as convenience.

By 1884, over two-thirds of the world's shipping used nautical charts referenced to Greenwich. The reason was not scientific priority — the Paris Observatory, founded in 1667, predated the Royal Observatory at Greenwich by eight years. The reason was the British Royal Navy. The dominant maritime force of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries produced the dominant charts. The Nautical Almanac, published from Greenwich since 1767, became the standard reference for celestial navigation. Ships that wanted the most accurate, most frequently updated charts used British Admiralty charts. British Admiralty charts used Greenwich. By the time anyone asked where zero should be, most of the world was already navigating from it.

The International Meridian Conference convened in Washington on October 1, 1884 — forty-one delegates from twenty-five nations, assembled at the invitation of President Chester A. Arthur. The British delegation argued for Greenwich on grounds of "maximal global utility." The French delegation argued for neutrality — a meridian that passed through no national observatory, perhaps through the Azores, perhaps at sea. They noted that the metric system had succeeded precisely because the metre was defined as a fraction of the Earth, not as a fraction of France. The vote on October 13: twenty-two in favor of Greenwich, one against, two abstentions. France was one of the abstentions. France did not formally adopt the Greenwich meridian until 1911, twenty-seven years later.

The British argument was never "Greenwich is ours." It was "Greenwich is already in use." Most ships already navigate by it. Most charts already reference it. Changing would require reprinting thousands of charts and retraining thousands of navigators. The practical argument was irresistible, and it was also circular. Greenwich was convenient because it was already standard. It was already standard because British naval supremacy had made it standard. The empire built the infrastructure. The infrastructure generated the convenience. The convenience was presented as a neutral, practical fact at the conference table. The power that created it did not need to be mentioned, because the convenience argument made the power invisible. Convenience was the alibi. Precedent was the verdict.

There is a second line to this story. For 130 years, the prime meridian was defined by the Airy Transit Circle — a telescope installed by Astronomer Royal George Biddell Airy in 1851. Longitude was measured by the stars: their positions observed through the crosshairs of the transit instrument defined the plane of the meridian. When satellite geodesy replaced telescope-based observation in the late twentieth century, the definition of zero shifted. The IERS Reference Meridian — based on the center of mass of the Earth and a global network of satellite stations — passes 102.478 meters east of the Airy Transit Circle. The brass line embedded in the courtyard of the Royal Observatory, the line where tourists queue to photograph themselves with one foot in each hemisphere, is not the prime meridian. The prime meridian is in the park, 102 meters to the east, unmarked.

The monument performs the meridian. The meridian is elsewhere. The tourists stand on the wrong line and their photographs record the wrong zero. The brass strip is a fossil of one telescope's position in 1851, preserved because the institution that houses it has more authority than the coordinate system that replaced it. The GPS in the tourist's pocket knows where zero is. The tourist stands where zero was. And the distance between the two — 102 meters, visible on any sufficiently zoomed map — is the width of the gap between what the monument performs and what the measurement reports.
