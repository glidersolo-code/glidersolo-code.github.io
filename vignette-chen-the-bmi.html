The Index

Maren stepped off the scale. "BMI 26.1. Overweight."

"By whose definition?" Chen asked.

"The chart on the wall."

"The chart on the wall uses thresholds set by a WHO expert committee in 1995. Derived primarily from mortality data in European and North American populations. Applied globally."

Chen's paragraph was about the relationship between a formula and the question it is asked to answer. The BMI formula — weight divided by height squared — was devised by Adolphe Quetelet in 1832. Quetelet was an astronomer and statistician, not a physician. He wanted to characterize the average Belgian male for population-level analysis. He chose height squared because it produced a roughly constant ratio across his sample. The choice was pragmatic, not physiological.

"The formula measures weight and height," Chen said. "It does not measure fat. It cannot distinguish between muscle mass and fat mass. A bodybuilder with 8% body fat and a sedentary person with 32%, at the same height and weight, receive the same BMI."

"But it's used in every doctor's office."

"Tomiyama and colleagues analyzed NHANES data. Among people classified as overweight by BMI: 47% were metabolically healthy — normal blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol, inflammation. Among people classified as normal weight: 30% were metabolically unhealthy. The classification correctly identified metabolic health in approximately 51% of the population."

"Fifty-one percent."

"Approximately chance. And in 2004, a WHO consultation found that Asian populations have higher body fat and higher cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI values. They recommended different thresholds. The recommendation was not binding. The original thresholds persist."

"So the formula designed for 19th-century Belgian men defines obesity for everyone."

"The formula designed for population statistics was renamed by Ancel Keys in 1972. 'Quetelet Index' sounds like a statistical convenience. 'Body Mass Index' sounds like a clinical measurement. Same formula. Different name. Different authority. The formula migrated from statistics to epidemiology to clinical medicine. At each step, it was asked to mean more. At no step did it gain the ability to mean more."

The paragraph sat at 0.8. It was, like a BMI, a single number applied as though it measured something about the thing itself — compact, numerical, formatted as clinical — while measuring only what the formula could calculate, which was less than the context implied.
