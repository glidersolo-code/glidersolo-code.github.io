The Paralytic

Three drugs. Three functions. Only one is for the condemned.

The anesthetic is for the condemned — to prevent pain. The paralytic is for the witnesses — to prevent visible movement that would look like suffering. The potassium chloride is for the heart — to stop it.

If the anesthetic fails, the paralytic ensures no one can tell. The condemned cannot move, cannot scream, cannot grimace, cannot signal pain. The body lies still on the gurney. The witnesses watch through glass. The witnesses see a person who appears to have fallen asleep and died. The appearance is the humaneness. The humaneness is the paralytic.

Jay Chapman designed the three-drug protocol in 1977. Chapman was Oklahoma's chief medical examiner. He later admitted: "I didn't do any research." The protocol was not peer-reviewed. The protocol was not designed by anesthesiologists or pain specialists. The protocol was designed for a state legislature by a medical examiner who needed something that looked medical.

The vocabulary: anesthetic, paralytic, cardiac arrest agent. IV line. Gurney. Sterile room. Saline drip. The vocabulary is medical. The practitioners are not. The American Medical Association opposes physician participation in executions. The American Nurses Association opposes nurse participation. The American Pharmacists Association opposes pharmacist participation. The medical vocabulary persists without medical professionals. The theater continues without the actors who would know whether the theater is working.

After European manufacturers refused to supply sodium thiopental — Hospira in 2011, followed by EU export regulations — states turned to midazolam. Midazolam is a sedative, not a surgical anesthetic. Multiple anesthesiologists testified it cannot produce the depth of anesthesia required to render the condemned unconscious during cardiac arrest. Clayton Lockett's execution took forty-three minutes. Joseph Wood's took nearly two hours. Both involved midazolam.

The Supreme Court held in Glossip v. Gross that midazolam did not violate the Eighth Amendment. The burden fell on the condemned: the prisoner must identify an alternative method of execution that is less painful. The system requires the condemned to design the system's humaneness. The condemned must prove the system is cruel by proposing a less cruel version. The proposal is the burden. The burden is on the person strapped to the gurney.

The paralytic makes the execution look humane by making suffering invisible. The visibility is the standard. What cannot be seen cannot be cruel. What the drug prevents from being expressed, the law does not require the system to detect. The concealment and the performance are the same molecule.
