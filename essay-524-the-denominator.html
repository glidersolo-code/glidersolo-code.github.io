Essay 524: The Denominator

The infant mortality rate is deaths before age one divided by live births. The numerator counts tragedy. The denominator counts arrival. The division produces a number — 5.4 per 1,000, in the United States, circa 2022 — and the number ranks countries. The US ranks behind Japan, Finland, Sweden, Norway, and most of Western Europe. The ranking communicates healthcare quality. The ranking does not communicate the denominator.

What counts as a live birth? The World Health Organization definition: any product of conception that, after separation from its mother, breathes or shows any evidence of life — heartbeat, pulsation of the umbilical cord, movement of voluntary muscles — regardless of gestational age or weight. The US applies this definition literally. A 22-week fetus weighing 400 grams that takes a single breath and dies three minutes later is registered as a live birth. The breath enters the denominator. The death enters the numerator. Both are counted.

France does not register births below 22 weeks of gestation as live births. The Netherlands uses a 24-week viability threshold. Norway historically excluded births under 28 weeks. Japan classifies some periviable births showing brief signs of life as late fetal deaths — stillbirths — rather than live births. The same biological event — a 23-week delivery with a transient heartbeat — is a live birth in Baltimore and a stillbirth in Paris. The infant mortality rate in both countries reflects this classification before it reflects any difference in care.

Births at 22 to 25 weeks gestation have mortality rates exceeding 50%. The US counts all of them. Countries with narrower definitions exclude them from both numerator and denominator. The US rate is inflated by the inclusion of a population that other countries' bookkeeping systems do not recognize as having been born alive. The denominator is doing work. The denominator is filtering.

Kramer and colleagues demonstrated this in 2002: international differences in infant mortality are "at least partly the result of inter-country differences in registration of infants near the borderline of viability." Chen, Oster, and Williams confirmed in 2016 that the reporting artifact is "quantitatively important." After adjusting for registration differences, the US disadvantage narrows — but does not disappear. The residual gap concentrates in postneonatal mortality, deaths between one month and one year, suggesting real differences in social support and access to care that no definitional adjustment can explain away.

The definition does two things at once. It determines who is counted, and it disappears from the count. The number 5.4 does not carry an asterisk. The ranking does not include a footnote on every news broadcast. The phrase "infant mortality rate" performs as though the words "infant," "mortality," and "rate" mean the same thing everywhere. The first word — infant — already contains the divergence. What is an infant? A 23-week delivery with a heartbeat? Depends on who is filling out the form.

The denominator is not a neutral administrative category. The denominator is a boundary — drawn differently in different countries, invisible in the resulting number, and determinative of the ranking that the number produces. The rate performs as a measure of how well a country cares for its children. The rate is partly a measure of how broadly a country defines which arrivals count as children.
