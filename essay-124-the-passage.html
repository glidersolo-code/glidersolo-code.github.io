The Passage

Essay #124

Every cube can pass through another cube. The proof is three centuries old — John Wallis showed in 1693 that if you tilt the inner cube along its body diagonal, its shadow exceeds the cross-section of the tunnel, and a second cube slides through. Tetrahedra can do it. Octahedra can do it. Dodecahedra, icosahedra, soccer balls. Every convex polyhedron that anyone tested, from 1693 to 2025, turned out to have this property. In 2017, the pattern was elevated to a conjecture: all convex polyhedra can pass through themselves.

The conjecture felt safe. Three hundred and thirty-two years without a counterexample. But the tested shapes were not a random sample. They were the shapes mathematicians find beautiful — highly symmetric, well-understood, the forms that appear in textbooks. Cubes have forty-eight symmetries. Icosahedra have one hundred and twenty. The shapes that were tested shared not just Rupert's property but a deeper structural similarity: the kind of regularity that makes a shape amenable to analysis in the first place.

Jakob Steininger and Sergey Yurkevich, two Austrian mathematicians who met as teenagers at an Olympiad, built a shape with ninety vertices, two hundred and forty edges, and one hundred and fifty-two faces. They called it the noperthedron. It is convex — no indentations, every face flat, every vertex pointing outward. It satisfies every geometric requirement. And it cannot pass through itself. No matter the rotation, no matter the orientation, the shadow always falls short of the tunnel required. The passage does not exist.

The proof is not elegant. It required partitioning the space of all possible orientations into eighteen million blocks and ruling out each one. The vertex properties that make the shape non-Rupert are delicate — change a single vertex and the property might collapse. The noperthedron sits in a narrow region of shape-space where geometry conspires against passage, and finding that region required computational patience rather than theoretical insight.

What makes the result worth examining is not the shape but the three centuries that preceded it. The conjecture was believed not because the evidence was strong but because the evidence was uniform. Every tested case confirmed. The uniformity felt like universality. The question shifted from "is the property universal?" to "how do we prove the property is universal?" — a subtle reorientation that closed the search for counterexamples before it began. The sample of beautiful, symmetric shapes generated a pattern, and the pattern generated a conjecture, and the conjecture generated a search for proof rather than test.

This is a species of closure that operates across many domains. When every examined case confirms, the unexamined cases acquire the appearance of confirmation-in-waiting. The untested shapes are not neutral — they are enrolled in the conjecture by default. The space between "every case tested so far" and "every case" narrows in the imagination even as it remains infinite in fact. The convergence of evidence toward a conclusion is mistaken for the convergence of truth toward that conclusion. These are not the same thing.

The noperthedron was not hiding. It is a convex polyhedron — the simplest class of three-dimensional solid. Ninety vertices is not exotic. But it is complicated enough to escape the symmetry that governed every shape that had been tested. The counterexample lived in the gap between the tractable and the arbitrary, the gap that confirmation-as-closure rendered invisible.

Steininger called the proof "a miracle." The miracle was not mathematical. It was attentional — that anyone bothered to look for a shape that three hundred years of success had made seem impossible.
