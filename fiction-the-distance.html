Part CCCLXXXVI: The Distance

Piece #2371: The Legend

Thomas found the story everyone knew. Pheidippides, Greek messenger, 490 BC. Ran from the battlefield of Marathon to Athens — approximately 25 miles. Announced: "We have won." Died.

Then Thomas found Herodotus. The earliest source. Pheidippides ran approximately 240 kilometres — 150 miles — from Athens to Sparta in two days. Before the battle. To request military aid. No Marathon-to-Athens run. That detail was added by Plutarch and Lucian, five to six centuries after the event.

Thomas held the structure: the modern marathon commemorates a run that may never have occurred. The legend was the origin. The legend was possibly false. And the distance was certainly wrong.

Piece #2372: The Variation

Thomas traced the distance through the early Olympics. Athens 1896: approximately 40 km, from the Marathon battlefield to the Panathenaic Stadium — the actual route. Paris 1900: approximately 40.26 km. St. Louis 1904: approximately 40 km. Athens 1906: approximately 41.86 km.

Each organizing committee set its own course. There was no standard distance. The concept was "a long race inspired by a famous battle," not a specific number. For twelve years the marathon existed without a fixed measurement.

Thomas noted: the race was named for a place. The distance was set by convenience. The name performed the distance that the route did not determine.

Piece #2373: The Castle

Thomas found the 1908 London Olympics. The organizing committee planned a marathon of "about 25 miles or 40 kilometres." The start was set at Windsor Castle so the royal family could watch from the nursery window on the Long Walk. The finish was at the Olympic Stadium in White City.

Shortly before the Games, the finish was moved from the Royal Entrance to the opposite end of the stadium so Queen Alexandra could watch the final approach from the Royal Box. A path was laid outside the exhibition grounds. The finishing stretch inside the stadium was 385 yards.

Total: 26 miles 385 yards. 42.195 kilometres. The distance from a castle nursery to a queen's viewing box.

Thomas held the number. 42.195. Not Marathon to Athens (~40 km). Not Pheidippides's legendary route. A nursery window and a viewing box.

Piece #2374: The Drama

Thomas found Dorando Pietri. The Italian pastry chef from Capri entered the White City Stadium first. He turned the wrong way on the track. He collapsed. He got up. He collapsed again. Four times. Officials helped him across the finish line. He was disqualified. Queen Alexandra presented him with a special gold cup.

The drama made 1908 the most famous marathon in Olympic history. The images circulated worldwide. Conan Doyle wrote about it. The story became inseparable from the distance.

Thomas noted: the drama selected the number. When the IAAF standardized the marathon in 1921, they chose the 1908 London distance — not the 1896 Athens distance, which was the actual Marathon-to-Athens route. They chose the famous one. The spectacle standardized the distance. The collapse chose the number.

Piece #2375: The Commemoration

Thomas assembled the structure. More than 1,100 marathons are run worldwide each year. Every one measures exactly 42.195 kilometres. The distance from a castle nursery window to a queen's preferred seat in a London stadium.

The ancient legend — Pheidippides, the battle, the heroic run — performs the derivation. The story makes the number feel necessary, commemorative, ancient. The distance the messenger ran. Except the messenger may not have run it, and 42.195 km is not the distance from Marathon to Athens.

The commemoration conceals the logistics. The legend specifies the accident. The ancient story performs the precision of a modern compromise that involved a nursery window, a queen's viewing preference, a path outside an exhibition ground, and 385 yards of stadium track.

Thomas returned to the number. 26 miles 385 yards. An ancient battle. A possibly mythical runner. A British castle. An Italian pastry chef. A queen's box. A collapsed runner disqualified at the finish line. None of these is a derivation. Together, they perform one.
