The Quota

The formula was: 2% of each nationality's population as recorded in the 1890 census.

The year was the variable that did the work. The 1890 census predated the peak of Southern and Eastern European immigration. Congress could have used 1910 or 1920 — both available, both more recent. The Eugenics Committee's report was explicit: the 1890 baseline would "change the character of immigration, and hence of our future population, by bringing about a preponderance of immigration of the stock which originally settled this country." Under the formula, Germany's annual quota exceeded 55,000. Italy's was 4,000. Italian immigration fell more than 90%.

The mathematics was correct. Two percent of the 1890 Italian-born population was, in fact, approximately 4,000. The mathematics was also the concealment — not because it was wrong but because the format of a formula implies neutrality. The formula calculates. The choice of which year's census to calculate from is not a calculation. It is a preference. The preference was expressed in the language of arithmetic, and the arithmetic made it look like administration.

Forty-one years later, the Hart-Celler Act abolished the national-origins system. The reform was genuine: the explicitly discriminatory formula was replaced with per-country caps of 20,000, applied equally to all nations. Every country received the same number. The formal equality was the new concealment. Mexico, with 40 million people and prior annual migration of roughly 235,000, received 20,000. Luxembourg, with 350,000 people, also received 20,000. Per-capita access to immigration varied by more than a hundredfold between countries. The cap on Mexico reclassified approximately 200,000 annual migrants as illegal overnight — not by changing the migration but by changing the number.

The family reunification provision, designed by its congressional sponsors to preserve the European-origin demographic base, produced the opposite: new immigrants from Asia and Latin America established themselves, sponsored family members, and the demographic composition shifted in the direction the sponsors had hoped to prevent. The architects miscalculated. The structure did not.

Concealment evolves. The 1924 formula was barely concealed — the eugenicist origins were documented, the racial motivation was stated in committee reports. The 1965 replacement was more durable because it appeared to have solved the problem. Equal caps. Equal treatment. The equality was formal. The inequality was structural. The formal equality made the structural inequality harder to name, because the name for what the 1924 Act did — discrimination — no longer seemed to apply. The problem had been abolished. What remained was just a number, applied equally to everyone.
