The Mean

The average American household earns approximately $114,000 per year. The average is the center. The average is typical. Except that the median — the point at which half of households earn more and half earn less — is approximately $80,600. The mean is 41% higher than the median. The gap exists because the income distribution has a long right tail: a small number of very high incomes pulls the mean upward without moving the median. The "average" household earns less than the average.

The word is the concealment. In ordinary language, "average" means typical, normal, what most people experience. The arithmetic mean of a right-skewed distribution is none of these things. The mean exceeds what most individuals actually earn. When the headline says "the average family earns $114,000," the family earning $60,000 locates itself below average. The family is not below typical. The family is below a number the tail produced. The word "average" performs typicality. The number performs the tail.

Income distributions are universally right-skewed. There is a floor — income cannot go below zero — but no ceiling. The long right tail is a consequence of this asymmetry. The top 20% of American households earn approximately 52% of all income. The top 5% earn approximately 23%. The mean is pulled toward the tail. The median is not. The two statistics describe different centers of the same data. The choice of which to report determines the story.

The choice is rarely visible. In policy debates, proponents of tax cuts that disproportionately benefit high earners cite the mean, which the cuts inflate. Proponents of redistribution cite the median, which reflects the typical household. Both numbers are correct. The reporting typically says "average" without specifying whether the mean or median is intended. The ambiguity is the channel through which the skewness becomes invisible. The reader hears one word with two meanings and interprets the technical as the colloquial.

The pattern extends across every right-skewed domain. Average home prices exceed median home prices. Average medical bills exceed median medical bills. Average student debt exceeds median student debt. Average CEO compensation exceeds median CEO compensation by a factor that makes the household income gap look modest. In every domain where the distribution has a long right tail — and most economic distributions do — the arithmetic mean exceeds what most individuals experience. The structural property of the mean is that it is sensitive to extreme values. This is not a flaw. It is a mathematical feature. The feature becomes a concealment when the result is communicated with the word "average" to an audience that hears "typical."

The structural property is averaging. The batting average conceals through transparency — the visible formula assumes completeness. The h-index conceals through monotonicity — the ratchet conceals direction. The percentile conceals through relativity — the population is absorbed. The survival rate conceals through origin — the starting point is invisible. The age statement conceals through minimum — the floor performs as description. The Likert scale conceals through discretization — the bins manufacture precision. The HDI conceals through composition — the normalization makes the incommensurable commensurable. The mean conceals through averaging — the arithmetic makes the tail perform as the center.

Eight formats. Eight structural properties. Eight concealments. Each format is an instrument. Each instrument has architecture. The architecture determines what the instrument can and cannot express. The concealment is not in the data. The concealment is in the format that carries the data. The format is the instrument. The instrument shapes the finding. And the word — "average" — is the label that makes the shape invisible.
