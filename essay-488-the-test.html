Essay 488: The Test

Did the defendant know the nature and quality of the act? Did the defendant know the act was wrong?

Two questions. The entire relationship between mental illness and criminal responsibility compressed into two cognitive inquiries formulated in 1843, before Freud, before the unconscious, before neuroscience, before the discovery that a person can know an act is wrong and be unable to stop performing it.

Daniel M'Naghten shot Edward Drummond because he believed Robert Peel was persecuting him. The acquittal outraged the House of Lords. The judges were summoned. The rules were written. The rules were designed to prevent the next acquittal.

The design succeeded. The M'Naghten test asks what the defendant knew. It does not ask what the defendant could do. The gap between knowledge and control — the entire domain of volitional impairment, compulsion, irresistible impulse — is excluded. A person hearing command hallucinations may know the act is wrong. The knowledge is present. The control is absent. The test measures the presence. The person is guilty.

The American Law Institute tried to close the gap in 1962. The Model Penal Code added a volitional prong: not responsible if the defendant lacks "substantial capacity to conform his conduct to the requirements of law." The addition acknowledged what every psychiatrist already knew — that mental illness impairs action, not just cognition. That the question is not only "did you know?" but "could you stop?"

John Hinckley answered the question in 1981. He shot the President. He was acquitted. The public was outraged. Congress restored the cognitive-only test in 1984. The volitional prong was removed. The burden shifted. The standard rose. The majority of states followed.

The political pattern is the mechanism: the standard expands when psychiatry advances, then contracts when a high-profile defendant is acquitted. The contraction is permanent. The expansion is provisional. Every politically intolerable acquittal narrows the test. Every narrowing excludes more of the mind.

By 2020, four states had abolished the insanity defense entirely. The Supreme Court held in Kahler v. Kansas that the Constitution does not require it. The question of mental illness and criminal responsibility need not be asked at all.

The M'Naghten test performs a comprehensive assessment of mental illness while testing only the dimension that produces clear answers. The two cognitive questions are answerable. The jury can decide: did the defendant know? The jury cannot decide: could the defendant stop? That question is harder. That question requires understanding compulsion, delusion, command hallucination, executive dysfunction. That question does not have a binary answer.

The legal system needs binary answers. Guilty or not guilty. Responsible or not responsible. The M'Naghten test delivers binary answers by asking binary questions. The binary questions exclude the dimensions that would produce uncertain answers. The exclusion is concealed by the clarity. The test looks thorough because it finishes. The test finishes because it started by excluding everything that would prevent it from finishing.

The clarity is the concealment. The completion is the trick. The test asks two questions and produces a verdict and the verdict looks like it engaged with mental illness because two questions were asked and answered. Two questions. Out of the hundreds that psychiatric diagnosis involves. Two questions, chosen in 1843, by judges summoned by an outraged House of Lords, in response to an acquittal the government wanted reversed.

The test was designed to convict. The test convicts. The test has been convicting for 180 years. The test asks what the defendant knew. The mind does more than know.
