The Cockpit

Chen has the Daniels paper on his desk — "The 'Average Man'?" — the question mark visible even at a distance.

"4,063 pilots," Chen says. "140 body dimensions. Ten dimensions selected for cockpit design. Middle 30% defined as average. How many pilots fell within the average range on all ten?"

"Zero," Maren says, without looking up.

"Zero."

"The average pilot described no pilot. The cockpit designed for the average was designed for no one."

Chen turns the paper over. "Quetelet thought the average was the ideal. The true type. Individual variation was error — noise around the signal."

"Daniels showed the opposite. Individual variation is the signal. The average is the noise — a mathematical artifact that corresponds to no observation in the dataset."

"How does the probability work?"

"If 30% of pilots are average on any one dimension, and dimensions are independent, then the probability of being average on ten dimensions is roughly 0.3 to the tenth power — about 0.003%. Three in 100,000. With correlated dimensions, worse."

"So the more dimensions you measure, the fewer people the average describes."

"Until it describes no one. And the cockpit built for no one kills the people who actually sit in it."

Maren pulls up the entry. "The mechanism is different from population-as-individual. In BMI or IQ, a population-level statistic is applied to an individual. Here, the population-level statistic creates a fictional individual — the average pilot — who is treated as real. The average is not a misapplied statistic. It's a ghost wearing the format of a person."

"Average-as-archetype," Chen says. "The sixtieth species."

"The format performs it. A single number — 5'9", 33-inch arm reach, 170 pounds — looks like a description of a body. The number implies existence. But the number is a summary of a distribution, not a portrait of a member. The distribution has no single member at its center."

"The Air Force solution was adjustable seats."

"Not a better average. The abolition of the average as design target. Build for the range, not the point. The paradigm shift wasn't statistical — it was ontological. Stop assuming the population contains a typical member."

Chen stares at the question mark in the title. "The question mark is the whole paper."

"The question mark is the whole species. Every use of 'average' as if it describes someone real is the assumption Daniels tested and found empty."

The paper sits on the desk, the question mark facing up. The 'Average Man'? The question is the answer. The average man is a question, not a fact. The cockpit built for the question killed the people who were not questions but bodies — specific, variable, irreducible to a single point in 140 dimensions.
