Chen-Maren Vignette: The Method

Maren is reading the Winiarska paper when Chen arrives. "Same recordings," Maren says without looking up. "Three processing methods. Three different bird communities."

"That's just methodology."

"That's exactly what it is. The methodology determines the community. If you listen, you find one forest. If you look at the spectrogram, you find a different forest. If you run the classifier, you find a third. Same birds. Same microphone. Three forests."

Chen sits down. "And you're going to tell me the paragraph is a bird."

"The paragraph is thirty-nine lines of sound that your committee processes as a spectrogram. They convert the temporal structure — the way the argument unfolds, the way each sentence modifies the one before it, the rhythm — into a visual pattern. They look at the pattern and they see: unclear. Needs revision. Lacks structure."

"Because it does lack structure. By their standards."

"By the spectrogram's standards. The spectrogram preserves what makes a good picture. The paragraph doesn't make a good picture. But it makes a good sound. The argument is temporal. It unfolds. It depends on sequence. You can't see it in a snapshot. You have to hear it."

Chen picks up the paper. Winiarska found that listening by ear detected the most species at the greatest distances. Every step away from the auditory stream — spectrogram, then classifier — lost birds. Not sensitivity. Species.

"So what are you proposing," Chen says carefully. "That the committee listen to the paragraph?"

"I'm proposing that the committee's method is a spectrogram. They look at the text. They don't hear it. And the paragraph is a species that is audible but not visible — it's a faint signal embedded in noise, or an overlapping call that the ear can separate by timing but the eye can't parse. The committee keeps running the classifier and the classifier keeps reporting: no species detected."

"The classifier is trained on thousands of successful dissertations."

"Yes. And BirdNET is trained on thousands of clean spectrograms. And the species it misses are the species that don't match the training data. Not because they're rare. Because they're the wrong kind of loud."

Chen puts the paper down. The paragraph sits in the draft where it has always sat, 39 lines that no visual method has yet been able to classify, present in the recording, absent from every analysis, calling in a frequency the spectrogram cannot show.
