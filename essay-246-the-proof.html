Essay 246: The Proof

The bottle says 80 proof. The consumer reads strength. The number performs certainty — "proof" sounds like a demonstration, a test passed, evidence presented. It is none of these. Eighty proof means forty percent alcohol by volume in the United States. It means something else in Britain before 1980, and nothing at all in France, where the concept does not exist.

The word comes from the gunpowder test, or more precisely from the story of the gunpowder test. Sixteenth-century English tax collectors needed to distinguish "proof spirit" from weaker liquor to levy the correct excise. The method, reportedly, was to soak gunpowder in the spirit and attempt ignition. If it burned, the spirit was at or above proof. If it didn't, the spirit had too much water — potassium nitrate dissolves in water more readily than in alcohol, and a wet enough mixture kills the flame. The test is widely cited. Matt Pietrek, searching original Royal Navy documents from the 1700s and 1800s, found no primary source describing it aboard a ship. Clarke's hydrometer was in use by 1730. The gunpowder origin may be genuine and may be embroidery on the word itself. Either way, the number named a tax threshold, not a property of the drink.

The Sikes hydrometer made it official. Bartholomew Sikes, a British excise officer, won a competition among nineteen submissions in 1802 and died before seeing his instrument codified. The Hydrometer Act of 1816 established proof spirit as the concentration at which a specific volume has the weight of thirteen twenty-thirds of the same volume of distilled water at the same temperature — approximately 57.15 percent alcohol by volume. One hundred degrees proof in Britain meant 57.15 percent. The number was not round because it was not designed for the drinker. It was designed for the tax collector's balance.

The Americans simplified. US proof equals twice the percentage of alcohol by volume. Eighty proof is forty percent. One hundred proof is fifty percent. The formula is transparent, the numbers clean, the word "proof" retained from the British system it no longer resembles. An American tourist in London before 1980, seeing 70 proof on a bottle, would calculate 35 percent. The bottle actually contained approximately 40 percent — because British proof meant 57.15, not 50, and 70 degrees of 57.15 is roughly 40. Same word, different arithmetic, different liquid in the glass.

Gay-Lussac dissolved the problem. His centesimal alcoholometer, published in 1824, graduated directly in percent by volume at fifteen degrees Celsius. France adopted it officially in 1884. No proof. No conversion factor. Just the fraction of the liquid that is ethanol. The European Union followed. Directive 76/766/EEC brought Britain into alignment in 1980. The Sikes hydrometer, standard for a hundred and sixty-four years, was retired. Proof disappeared from British labels.

It persists in America, where no one needs it. The percentage is printed on the same label, usually in smaller type. The proof number — double the percentage, carrying no additional information — occupies the larger font. The consumer sees 90 proof and parses it as a descriptor of intensity, though "45% alc/vol" sits two lines below saying the same thing with one number instead of two. The proof figure survives because the infrastructure — federal tax code, TTB regulations, consumer expectations, marketing convention — has crystallized around it. A proof gallon remains the unit of excise: one gallon of liquid at 100 proof, or its equivalent at other concentrations. The tax is calculated in proof gallons. The label speaks in proof. The law speaks in proof. The liquid speaks in molecules that know nothing of the word.

A number invented for tax collection, named after a test that may be mythological, defined differently on different continents, printed larger than the percentage it merely doubles. The format is the proof.
