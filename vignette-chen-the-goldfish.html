Vignette Chen The Goldfish

Vignette

Chen was at a faculty meeting. The dean was presenting data on student engagement. One slide said: "The average human attention span is now 8 seconds — shorter than a goldfish at 9 seconds. Source: Microsoft, 2015."

"The implications are clear," the dean said. "We need to redesign our courses for shorter attention spans. More interactive elements. More frequent transitions. Shorter lecture segments."

Chen wrote down the citation during the break and looked it up. The Microsoft Canada report, "Attention Spans: Consumer Insights," 2015. She found the PDF. Fifty-two pages. The "8 seconds" appeared on page 6 of the introduction. The citation was a website called Statistic Brain. She went to Statistic Brain. The listed sources were NCBI and the Associated Press. She checked both. No record of the research.

She read the rest of the Microsoft report — the actual research. It never mentioned "8 seconds." It never mentioned goldfish. The company's own scientists hadn't produced the number. The marketing division had copied it from a website that couldn't cite its own sources.

She searched for the goldfish study. There was no goldfish study. Goldfish can be trained to press levers. They navigate mazes. They remember locations for months.

After the break, she raised her hand. "What's the source for the goldfish number?"

"Microsoft, 2015," the dean said.

"Microsoft's marketing division copied it from a website that cites sources that have no record of the research. The report's own research section doesn't mention 8 seconds or goldfish."

"Well, the broader point about declining attention —"

"Bradbury reviewed that literature in 2016. The most-cited source for the 10-15 minute decline barely discusses student attention."

The room went quiet. Chen could see the other faculty looking at the slide. The goldfish made it memorable. The comparison made it feel true. "Shorter than a goldfish" was more vivid than any research finding could be — and that vividness was exactly the problem. The comparison was so interesting that no one had asked: What does "attention span" mean? Can it be measured as a single number? Measured how?

The dean moved to the next slide. The goldfish stayed in the room — not as a fact, but as the shape of the argument that had replaced the fact. Two fabricated numbers, compared to each other, producing a crisis that justified its own solution.
