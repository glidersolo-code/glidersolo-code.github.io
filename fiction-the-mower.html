Part CCLXXII: The Mower

Piece #1801: The Gate

The mower was listed as 22 inches. Thomas bought it because his side gate was 24 inches wide. Two inches of clearance. He wheeled the mower to the gate and discovered the mower was 28 inches wide. The "22 inches" was the cutting width — the distance between the blade tips inside the deck. The deck shell extended an inch past each blade tip. The discharge chute added six inches on the right side. The mower's cutting width fit through the gate. The mower did not. He tilted it sideways and scraped through, leaving paint on both gateposts. The number on the spec sheet described the swath of cut grass. The gate needed the width of the machine.

Piece #1802: The Comparison

Three mowers on the store floor. The first: "6.75 Gross Torque" (Briggs & Stratton). The second: "190cc" (Honda). The third: "6.5 HP" (store brand). The consumer stands between them. Gross torque is rotational force at 2,600 RPM. Displacement is the volume of the cylinders. Horsepower is force times speed at 3,600 RPM. The three numbers describe three different properties of three different engines measured at different RPMs. Converting any one to another requires the compression ratio, the bore-to-stroke ratio, and the mechanical efficiency — none of which appear on the mower or the tag. The consumer compares 6.75 to 190 to 6.5 and concludes one is larger. The numbers are not comparable. The format makes them comparable by placing them in the same position on the tag, in the same font, performing the same role: the engine's defining integer.

Piece #1803: The Switch

Briggs & Stratton switched from horsepower to gross torque for walk-behind mowers. Gross torque at 2,600 RPM is a lower RPM test than gross horsepower at 3,600 RPM. Torque describes the immediate twisting force — what the blade needs at the moment it hits thick grass. Horsepower describes sustained work over time. The consumer who previously bought a "6.5 HP" mower and now sees a "6.75 Gross Torque" replacement reads 6.75 as a bigger number than 6.5 and concludes the new mower is more powerful. The units changed. The number stayed similar. The comparison the consumer performed — 6.75 versus 6.5 — crossed a dimensional boundary the tag does not mark.

Piece #1804: The Swath

The 22-inch mower cuts a 22-inch swath. The consumer plans: the lawn is 50 feet wide. Twenty-two inches per pass. Roughly twenty-seven passes to cover the width. But the passes must overlap — two to three inches per pass to avoid leaving uncut strips. The effective swath is 19 to 20 inches. Thirty to thirty-one passes. The number on the mower is the theoretical maximum per pass. The lawn demands the practical minimum, which is the maximum minus the overlap, minus the curve at the end of each row, minus the narrower passes along the fence. The mowing time is determined by the effective swath, not the deck size. The deck size is the number that describes the mower at rest. The effective swath is the number that describes the mower at work.

Piece #1805: The Width

Two hundred and seventy-two Parts. The mower prints two numbers: deck size and engine rating. The deck size describes the cutting width, not the machine width. The consumer reads it as the machine's footprint and discovers the actual width when the gate is too narrow. The engine rating describes one of three possible properties — torque, displacement, or power — in whichever unit the manufacturer chose, making cross-brand comparison impossible without conversion factors the consumer does not have. Thomas mowed with the information the mower did not provide: the actual width of the machine, the effective swath after overlap, the gate clearance after the chute, the engine comparison after the unit conversion. The numbers on the mower described the mower's best pass. The lawn needed all the other ones.
