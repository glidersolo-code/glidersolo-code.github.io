Part CCLIII: The Bond

Piece #1706: The Tube

The tube says "bonds up to 4,000 PSI." Thomas bought it to fix a ceramic mug. The handle had broken cleanly at the base. He squeezed a bead of epoxy onto each surface, pressed them together, held them for sixty seconds, and set the mug on the counter. The 4,000 PSI was tested under ASTM D1002: degreased aluminum coupons, abraded to a specified roughness, bonded at a controlled thickness of 0.005 inches, cured for 72 hours at room temperature, pulled apart in a tensile machine. Thomas's surfaces were ceramic with residue from the original glaze fracture. His preparation was a wipe with a paper towel. His bond line was approximately 0.030 inches — six times the test thickness. His cure time was overnight. The adhesive's potential was 4,000 PSI. The conditions he provided may deliver 400.

Piece #1707: The Surface

Surface preparation is the variable adhesive manufacturers understand and consumers do not perform. ASTM D2651 specifies solvent degreasing, followed by mechanical abrasion, followed by a second solvent wipe. The solvent removes oils and contaminants. The abrasion creates surface roughness that increases the bonding area at the microscopic level. The second wipe removes abrasion debris. The consumer touches the surface with fingers — depositing skin oils — wipes with a dry cloth, and applies adhesive. Skin oils are a barrier. Moisture is a variable: cyanoacrylate polymerizes in the thin film of moisture between surfaces and needs moisture to cure, but excess moisture weakens the bond. Epoxy is hydrophobic in its uncured state and needs dry surfaces for full adhesion. The surface the consumer provides is not the surface the adhesive was tested on. The gap between the headline strength and the achieved strength begins at the fingertip.

Piece #1708: The Mix

Two-part epoxy requires mixing. The tube says "mix equal parts." The ratio is not decorative. Epoxy cures through a stoichiometric cross-linking reaction — the resin and hardener molecules must be present in the correct proportion for complete polymerization. Excess resin remains uncured and soft. Excess hardener remains unreacted and brittle. A 60/40 mix instead of 50/50 can reduce strength by 30 to 50 percent. The consumer estimates by eye, squeezing approximately equal ribbons onto a piece of cardboard, stirring with a toothpick for thirty seconds. Incomplete mixing leaves pockets of unmixed resin or hardener — areas of partial cure surrounded by fully cured matrix. The tube said "mix equal parts." The consumer heard "approximately." The chemistry heard "exactly."

Piece #1709: The Clock

"Sets in 5 minutes." "Full strength in 24 hours." The consumer reads "sets in 5 minutes" and loads the joint after dinner. "Setting" is handling strength — enough rigidity that the parts do not shift. Full cure is chemical completion — the cross-linking reaction that produces rated strength. At 5 minutes, the epoxy may have achieved 5 to 10 percent of its ultimate strength. At 24 hours, 80 percent. At 72 hours under optimal conditions, 100 percent. The consumer who loads the repaired mug handle the next morning — filling the mug with hot coffee, lifting by the handle — is loading a joint at perhaps 60 percent of its 72-hour strength, at perhaps 10 percent of its rated strength due to preparation variables, with hot liquid that further softens the uncured epoxy through thermal exposure. The mug handle held. It held because the load was small. It did not hold because the adhesive achieved its rating.

Piece #1710: The Repair

Two hundred and fifty-three Parts. Thomas's mug held for three weeks. On the twenty-second day, the handle came off in his hand. He was holding a full mug of coffee. The coffee went onto his shirt. He looked at the break surface — one side had adhesive, the other was clean ceramic. The bond had failed adhesively, not cohesively: the epoxy let go of the ceramic, not of itself. The adhesive's internal strength was fine. The adhesive's grip on the unprepared, unabraded, oil-contaminated ceramic surface was the failure point. Thomas bought a new tube. Same brand. Same "4,000 PSI." This time he would hold the pieces together longer. The adhesive was not the problem. The preparation was the problem. The number on the tube described what the adhesive could do. The surface described what the consumer would allow. The tube, having spoken in PSI, did not mention the fingertip.
