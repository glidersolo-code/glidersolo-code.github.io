The Impedance

The smart scale displays thirteen metrics: body fat percentage, muscle mass, bone mass, body water, visceral fat rating, BMR, metabolic age, subcutaneous fat, protein percentage, skeletal muscle mass, body fat mass, lean body mass, and BMI. Each number appears to one decimal place. Each occupies its own line in the app. Each is presented as an independent measurement of a distinct compartment of the body, the way a blood panel reports separate values for glucose, cholesterol, hemoglobin, and creatinine — each from its own assay, each measuring its own analyte.

The scale measured two things. Weight, via load cells: genuinely accurate to a tenth of a kilogram. And electrical impedance, via electrodes on the platform: a weak alternating current at 50 kilohertz, passing up one leg, across the pelvis, and down the other. One number. One frequency. One pathway through part of the body.

Everything else is arithmetic.

The foundational physics treats the body as a uniform cylinder. Impedance is proportional to the cylinder's length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area. Height squared divided by resistance — the "impedance index" — becomes the core predictor variable. Lukaski demonstrated this in 1985. Sun and colleagues refined it in 2003, deriving sex-specific prediction equations from 1,829 subjects in the NHANES III database: 1,474 white, 355 Black, ages 12 to 94, validated against a four-compartment reference model combining densitometry, dilution, and absorptiometry. The canonical form: FFM = a(Ht²/R) + bWeight + cAge + dSex + eXc + f. The impedance index alone explains approximately 82% of the variance in fat-free mass. Adding body weight raises it to 92%. Age and sex contribute marginally. The impedance measurement — the thing the scale actually performed, the fifty microamps through the feet — accounts for the remaining fraction. The equation does the work. The sensor confirms it.

From fat-free mass, the remaining twelve metrics cascade. Body fat mass equals total weight minus fat-free mass. Body fat percentage follows. Muscle mass is estimated as a fraction of fat-free mass. Bone mass is assigned as approximately 6-7% of fat-free mass — a fixed proportion, not an independent measurement. Bone is dielectric and invisible to impedance; the current passes through it the way light passes through glass, without interaction. When compared to DEXA, bone mass estimates from consumer BIA carry errors of 34% to 93%. Visceral fat rating is computed from demographic variables — age, sex, height — with minimal impedance input, because foot-to-foot devices route current through the legs and pelvis; the trunk, where visceral fat resides, contributes less than 5% of total impedance. Metabolic age is derived from estimated lean mass through standard metabolic equations. Subcutaneous fat is total fat minus visceral fat, both estimates. Protein is a fixed fraction of lean mass.

Thirteen metrics from one measurement, through cascading regressions of increasing indirection, each formatted as its own result.

Tinsley and colleagues tested fifteen BIA devices — fourteen consumer-grade, one research-grade — against a four-compartment reference model in seventy-three healthy adults. Body fat constant error ranged from -3.5% to +11.7% across devices. Standard error of estimate: 3.1 to 7.5 percentage points. Two devices measuring the same person on the same morning could disagree by more than ten percentage points. The devices were consistent: test-retest precision errors were under half a percentage point. The scale gives the same answer reliably. The answer may be wrong by eight points, but it is wrong the same way each time. Precision without accuracy — the appearance of stability from a number that may be systematically off.

Frija-Masson and colleagues tested three consumer scales against DEXA in a hospital population. All three underestimated fat mass: by 2.2, 3.7, and 4.4 kilograms respectively. The conclusion: "Smart scales are not accurate for body composition and should not replace DEXA in patient care." A DEXA scan uses dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry to independently measure bone mineral density, lean tissue, and fat tissue in every region of the body. The consumer scale performs the display grammar of the clinical scan from a single impedance number routed through two feet.

The confounds are physical. Drinking 500 milliliters of water can shift body fat estimates by two to nine percentage points — water lowers impedance, and the equation interprets lower impedance as more lean mass. Exercise within two hours, food within three hours, menstrual cycle phase, ambient temperature, foot calluses, time of day: each shifts the impedance reading without changing body composition. The number the consumer tracks as a stable property of the body is primarily a measurement of hydration state at the moment of measurement. The display reports "Body Fat %." The decimal point performs a stability the measurement does not contain.

The equations are proprietary. Consumer manufacturers do not publish them. The reference populations that calibrated them are unidentified. Sun's equations were derived from a specific demographic sample; users who do not match that sample — in ethnicity, age, body composition range, activity level — encounter systematic errors that the display cannot disclose because the display does not know which equation generated it. Firmware updates change the equations silently. Eighty-two percent of major firmware updates across seven leading brands introduce statistically significant baseline shifts. A user's body fat percentage changes overnight not because the body changed but because an engineer adjusted a coefficient. The trend graph shows improvement. The improvement was written in code.

A $25 Renpho scale claims thirteen body composition metrics. A $400 Withings Body Scan claims forty. Both present results in the visual grammar of clinical measurement: percentage signs, mass units, decimal precision, colored trend arrows, progress graphs, segmental body maps. The consumer reads the display and concludes: the scale measured my body fat, my muscle mass, my bone density, my visceral fat, my metabolic age. The scale measured impedance. The equation did everything else. And the equation is invisible — proprietary, population-specific, subject to silent revision, and formatted with the precision of the measurement it replaced.
