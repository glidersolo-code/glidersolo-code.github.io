Part CCXCIII: The Cockpit

Piece #1906: The 4,063

I am one of 4,063 pilots measured at Wright Air Development Center in 1950. They measured 140 dimensions of my body. Height. Weight. Sitting height. Arm reach. Thigh length. Knee height. Chest circumference. Wrist breadth. Head length. Head breadth. Neck circumference. One hundred and forty numbers, each describing one axis of my body in space.

When they averaged all 4,063 of us, they got a composite. The average pilot. Average height, average reach, average torso, average everything. The cockpit would be built for him.

They asked Gilbert Daniels to check. How many of us matched the average on the ten most important dimensions? He defined "average" generously — the middle 30% of each range. Then he counted.

Zero.

Not zero out of a million. Zero out of 4,063. The composite described none of us. The body the cockpit was being built for was a body that no pilot possessed.

I am average on three of the ten dimensions. Above average on four. Below average on three. This particular configuration — this specific body, not the average but a real arrangement of bones and muscles and tendons — is what sits in the seat, reaches for the throttle, presses the pedals. The cockpit was designed for a ghost. My body, the one doing the flying, was the deviation.

Piece #1907: The Average Man

I am l'homme moyen. Adolphe Quetelet invented me in Brussels in 1835. He was an astronomer, accustomed to averaging astronomical observations to find the true position of a star — the signal under the noise. He applied the same logic to human bodies. Measure many people. Average them. The average is the true type. Individual variation is error.

I was not a mathematical convenience. I was an ontological claim. I was the real human being — the Platonic ideal — and the 4,063 pilots were approximations of me. Their departures from my dimensions were departures from the norm, from the intended design, from the truth.

For a century, this belief designed the world. Cockpits, school desks, clothing, medical dosages, aptitude tests, hiring criteria — all built for me, the average, the type, the typical case. The assumption was so deep it was invisible: of course there is a typical body. Of course the average describes someone. Of course the central tendency is the center.

Daniels showed that I don't exist. When the dimensions multiply, the probability of any individual matching me on all of them approaches zero. I am a point in 140-dimensional space that no human body occupies. I am a mathematical ghost inhabiting a cockpit that my nonexistence makes dangerous.

The irony: Quetelet thought individual variation was noise. Daniels showed that individual variation is the signal. The bodies that deviate from me are the bodies that exist. I, the average, am the noise.

Piece #1908: The Adjustable Seat

I am the solution that followed from the discovery. I am the adjustable seat, mandated by the Air Force in 1954, two years after Daniels published "The 'Average Man'?"

Before me, the seat was fixed. The cockpit was designed for a body of specific dimensions — the average body — and the pilot adapted. If the pilot's arms were too short for the throttle, the pilot leaned. If the legs were too long for the pedals, the pilot compressed. The body adjusted to the architecture. The architecture assumed a body that didn't exist.

After me, the architecture adjusts to the body. I slide forward and back. I raise and lower. The pedals extend. The straps tighten or loosen. The helmet comes in sizes. The design principle is no longer: build for the type. The design principle is: build for the range.

The shift seems small — a sliding track, a lever, an adjustment mechanism. But the philosophical move is enormous. Fixed design assumes that the population contains a representative individual whom the design serves. Adjustable design assumes that the population contains no representative individual and the design must accommodate variation. The first is Quetelet's world. The second is Daniels'.

I migrated. Automobile seats. Office chairs. Standing desks. Consumer products of every kind. The adjustable world is Daniels' legacy — not a new average but the recognition that no average suffices.

Piece #1909: The Ten Dimensions

I am the middle 30%. The generous definition of "average" that Daniels used — not the mean exactly, but a range around the mean, the fattest part of the bell curve, the zone where the most people cluster for any single dimension.

On any one dimension, about 30% of the 4,063 pilots fall within me. That's a lot. A third of the population. The average, defined this way, describes a substantial portion of people — for that one dimension.

But bodies have more than one dimension. And the probability of falling in the average range on two independent dimensions is not 30% but roughly 30% × 30% = 9%. Three dimensions: roughly 2.7%. Ten dimensions: roughly 0.003%. At ten dimensions, three out of 100,000 people would be expected to match the average on all ten.

Daniels found zero out of 4,063. The actual result was worse than the independent-probability estimate, because body dimensions are correlated — a long arm often accompanies a long torso — and the correlations further reduce the probability of being average everywhere.

I am the middle 30%, and I contain the mathematical proof that averages cannot compose. You can be average on any one dimension. You cannot be average on all dimensions. The more dimensions you add, the emptier I become. The average pilot needs 140 dimensions to be defined, and 140 dimensions to be impossible.

Piece #1910: The Ghost

I am the average. I exist in the spreadsheet and not in the cockpit. I exist in the specification document and not in the body. I exist in the design brief and not in the population. I am the number that performs typicality while describing no one.

Every domain that uses me as a design target inherits the same problem. The average student, for whom the curriculum is paced, learns at a speed that matches no student's speed. The average patient, for whom the dosage is calculated, has a metabolism that matches no patient's metabolism. The average worker, for whom the workstation is built, has a body that fits no worker's body. The average citizen, for whom the policy is designed, lives a life that matches no citizen's life.

I am a ghost. Not a metaphorical ghost — a mathematical one. I am a point in multidimensional space computed by aggregation and corresponding to no observation in the dataset that produced me. I am a statistical artifact treated as a physical fact. The format — a single number for each dimension — performs my existence. The number implies that someone is this height, this weight, this reach. The number is accurate. The implication is false.

Daniels' discovery was not that the average is wrong. The average is a perfectly valid computation. His discovery was that the average is uninhabited — that the point it identifies in multidimensional space is empty, that the body it describes is a body no one has, and that designing for this empty point is designing for no one.

The cockpit built for me killed pilots. Not because I was poorly computed — the average was exact. Because I was treated as a person. The numbers were real. The body they described was not.
