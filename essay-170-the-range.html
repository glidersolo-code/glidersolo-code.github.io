The Range

The reference interval for a clinical laboratory test is the central 95% of results from a reference population. The convention was formalized by Henrik Solberg for the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry in 1983, building on the statistical tradition that Fisher had seeded decades earlier: 5% is the threshold of significance, the line between what counts and what does not. In laboratory medicine, the line separates normal from abnormal. A result at the 2.6th percentile is normal. A result at the 2.4th percentile is abnormal. The clinical distance between these two results is negligible. The categorical distance is total.

The arithmetic is not hidden. Every clinical chemistry textbook states it: 5% of healthy individuals will have at least one abnormal result on any single test. But the arithmetic compounds in ways the textbook states and the system ignores. A comprehensive metabolic panel tests fourteen analytes. If each has a 5% false-exclusion rate and the tests are approximately independent, the probability that a healthy individual has all fourteen results within normal: 0.95 raised to the fourteenth power, approximately 0.49. A coin flip. Add a complete blood count with differential — twenty or more reported values — and the probability drops further: 0.95 to the twentieth, approximately 0.36. A 64% chance that a perfectly healthy person will have at least one result flagged as abnormal.

The flagging is automatic. Electronic health record systems compare each result to the reference interval stored in the database and apply a visual marker — red text, bold font, an asterisk, the letter H or L — to any result outside the range. The flag is designed to prevent oversight: a clinician reviewing a panel of twenty results should not miss the one that falls outside the expected interval. The design is sound. The consequence is structural. In a panel of twenty results for a healthy person, one or two flags will appear by mathematical necessity. The flags draw the eye. The eye reads them as findings. The findings generate clinical activity: a note in the chart, a comparison to prior results, sometimes a repeat test, sometimes a referral. The activity is recorded. The chart accumulates evidence of clinical attention that is partly directed at the patient's condition and partly directed at the arithmetic of the testing system. The chart does not distinguish between these.

The problem is known. Benjamini and Hochberg published the false discovery rate method in 1995 — a statistical framework for adjusting significance thresholds when multiple tests are performed simultaneously. The Bonferroni correction is older still. Both are standard in research. Neither is applied in clinical laboratory reporting. Each result on a metabolic panel is evaluated against its reference interval independently, as if it were the only test being run. The system that generates twenty results simultaneously does not account for the simultaneous generation in its flagging. The research community recognized the multiplicity problem and built corrections. The clinical community recognized the multiplicity problem and did not apply them. Not because the corrections are unknown. Because the infrastructure — the laboratory information systems, the EHR displays, the clinical workflows, the medicolegal documentation standards — is built around the independent flag.

The patient who receives a twenty-test panel every three months accumulates flags. Some flags represent genuine clinical concerns: a medication level drifting out of therapeutic range, a trend in kidney function. Other flags represent the five percent — a result that is the patient's physiological normal, sitting just outside the reference population's statistical boundary. The chart records both. Both are red. Both generate the same clinical response pattern: flag, note, investigation. Over time, the chart becomes a document of clinical complexity that is partly the patient's complexity and partly the system's arithmetic. The patient carries both. The system reads both as the patient's. The range defines the normal. The normal defines the flags. The flags define the chart. And the chart, which is the system's record of itself paying attention, cannot distinguish between attention directed at the patient and attention directed at its own conventions — because the conventions are invisible within the system that created them, both red, both flagged, both entered as findings, both evidence that someone was watching.
