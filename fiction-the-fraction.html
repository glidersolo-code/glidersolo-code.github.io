Part CCI: The Fraction

Piece #1446: The Vestige

A gas station sign says $3.49⁹⁄₁₀. The nine-tenths is a vestige of the 1930s, when gasoline cost ten cents a gallon and a fraction of a cent was a fraction of the price. At $3.50, the fraction is 0.026 percent. It has crossed from competitive tool to format fossil. But it persists because every station uses it, and removing it would make the posted price appear one cent higher than every competitor.

Thomas recognized vestiges in clinical instruments. The PHQ-9 asks about "poor appetite or overeating" — a single item that collapses two opposite symptoms (anorexia and hyperphagia) into one question. This made sense when the instrument was being kept to nine items for brevity. It makes less sense now that the instrument is administered electronically and length is less constrained. But the item persists because changing it would break the scoring, invalidate the normative data, and require revalidation. The format has crystallized around the compromise.

Vestiges persist not because they serve the present purpose but because removing them would cost more than keeping them. The fraction and the collapsed item are both load-bearing — not because they carry weight, but because the structure around them was built assuming they are there.

Piece #1447: The Equilibrium

No individual gas station benefits from removing the fraction. Removing it would make the price appear one cent higher. The fraction is a Nash equilibrium — a state from which no single actor can profitably deviate. The convention is stable because deviation is punished, not because the convention is optimal.

Thomas saw Nash equilibria in clinical practice. Every clinic uses the PHQ-9 for depression screening. Any clinic that switched to a different instrument — the MADRS, the Hamilton, the CES-D — would lose comparability with the rest of the system. Their scores would not integrate with the population data, the billing thresholds, the EHR benchmarks. The PHQ-9 persists not because it is the best instrument but because it is the instrument everyone uses. Deviation is punished by incompatibility, not by quality.

An equilibrium is not an optimum. It is a state from which no one can move alone. The difference between the two is the difference between "this is the best option" and "this is the option we are stuck with." The format does not distinguish between them.

Piece #1448: The Anchor

Left-digit anchoring: the consumer reads $3.49⁹⁄₁₀ as $3.49 because the brain processes the first digits and uses them as an anchor. The fraction falls outside the perceptual window. The sign says $3.50. The brain reads $3.49.

Thomas knew about anchoring in clinical contexts. A patient told "your depression score is 14" hears "14" and anchors to it. The clinician says "moderate." The patient hears "14 out of 27" and computes a ratio — just over half — which feels like middle. If the clinician said "your score corresponds to the 85th percentile of symptom severity in the general population," the patient would anchor differently. The same number, framed differently, produces different perceptions of the same reality.

The anchor is the format's most powerful tool. It determines what the recipient processes first. What is processed first becomes the reference point. What is the reference point becomes the meaning. The fraction ensures the anchor is $3.49. The score ensures the anchor is 14. The format selects the anchor. The anchor selects the meaning.

Piece #1449: The Precision

A round number — $3.50 — looks like an estimate. A fractional number — $3.499 — looks like a calculation. The consumer trusts calculations more than estimates. The fraction performs specificity. It says: this price was computed, not chosen.

Thomas noticed the same effect in reported statistics. "About 70% of patients respond" sounded like a guess. "68.4% of patients respond" sounded like a finding. The decimal place performed precision. The precision performed credibility. The number with a decimal was no more accurate — the confidence interval was wide enough that both "about 70%" and "68.4%" were equally defensible. But "68.4%" was more trusted because it looked more measured.

The appearance of arithmetic replaces the fact of rounding. The format that seems most precise is not necessarily the most accurate. It is the one that most effectively performs the act of having been computed. The fraction on the gas sign and the decimal in the journal article are performing the same operation: making the viewer believe the number was arrived at through a process more rigorous than judgment.

Piece #1450: The Two Cents

Twenty gallons at $3.499 costs $69.98. Twenty gallons at $3.500 costs $70.00. The difference is two cents. Two cents on a seventy-dollar transaction. The consumer saves two cents but perceives saving twenty — because the sign says $3.49 and the competitor says $3.50, and the gap between 49 and 50 feels like one cent per gallon, which on twenty gallons feels like twenty cents. The actual savings: two. The perceived savings: ten times larger.

Thomas thought about perceived versus actual improvement. A PHQ-9 drop from 14 to 9 — crossing the threshold from "moderate" to "mild" — felt like a categorical improvement. A PHQ-9 drop from 19 to 14 — staying within "moderately severe" to "moderate" — felt like the same category. The same five-point improvement, one crossing a boundary and one not. The actual change in symptom burden was identical. The perceived change was determined by the category boundary.

The two cents and the five points share a structure: the actual difference is small, but the format amplifies it by placing the difference on the relevant side of a perceptual or categorical boundary. The fraction ensures the price falls on the left side of the dollar digit. The score ensures the severity falls on the comfortable side of the threshold. What the format moves is not the reality but the perception.

The format is the fraction.
