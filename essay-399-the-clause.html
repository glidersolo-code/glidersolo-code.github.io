The Clause

A contract arrives. Page fourteen. Section 9.3: Dispute Resolution. "Any dispute arising out of or relating to this Agreement shall be resolved by binding arbitration." The section occupies half a page in a forty-seven-page document. The worker signs the contract on the first day. The employer drafted the clause. The worker needed the job.

Sixty point one million American workers — 56% of the private-sector non-union workforce — are bound by mandatory arbitration procedures as a condition of employment. Eighty million consumers are bound by arbitration clauses in credit card agreements. Ninety-two percent of prepaid card agreements include them. Seventy-five percent of consumers whose agreements contained these clauses did not know they existed. Not unaware of the implications. Unaware of the existence.

The word in the clause is "agree." Both parties agree. But one party wrote the clause and the other party scrolled past it. The format of the contract performs negotiation — two parties, equal, choosing terms together. The function of the contract performs imposition — one party drafted, the other complied. The word "agreement" is the concealment character. It performs mutuality where none occurred.

The class action waiver is embedded in the arbitration clause. AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion (2011): the Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that the Federal Arbitration Act preempts state laws prohibiting class action waivers. Epic Systems v. Lewis (2018): extended to employment contracts. The waiver eliminates the only mechanism by which small-dollar systematic harm becomes visible. A company that overcharges $30 from 80 million consumers faces no aggregate liability. Each consumer faces an individual arbitration where the legal costs exceed the claim. The rational response to a $30 injury is to absorb it. The waiver does not change the forum. It eliminates the cause of action by making it economically irrational to pursue.

The outcomes confirm the asymmetry the clause conceals. Employees win 21.4% of mandatory arbitration cases — compared to 36.4% in federal court and 57% in state court. Median award in arbitration: $36,500. Median award in federal court: $176,426. Against repeat employers — those who appear in multiple arbitrations — employees win 16.9% of cases, roughly half the rate against first-time employers. The arbitrator selected by the employer who loses is less likely to be selected again. The selection mechanism performs neutrality. The incentive structure performs loyalty.

The Federal Arbitration Act was enacted in 1925 to govern disputes between commercial merchants of comparable bargaining power — two shipping companies resolving a cargo dispute. Circuit City v. Adams (2001) extended the statute to employment contracts. Concepcion extended it to consumer adhesion contracts. A statute written for two merchants choosing a specialized forum now governs a worker who was told to sign or not start. The legislative history documents one context. The judicial interpretation supplies another. The 1925 statute is a shell whose content has been replaced while its authority remains.

The courthouse still exists. The jury still exists. The right of appeal, the rules of discovery, the body of precedent — all still function. For other people. The clause is a private gate on a public road. The word on the gate is "agreement." The gate performs choice. Behind the gate, a forum where the repeat player wins 83% of the time.
