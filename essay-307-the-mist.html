The Mist

The humidifier says "output: 4 gallons per day." The 4 gallons are tested at 20 percent relative humidity.

AHAM Standard 611 specifies the conditions: 70 degrees Fahrenheit dry-bulb, 20 percent RH. At 20 percent, the vapor pressure deficit between the water surface and the room air is near its maximum — the air is extremely dry, evaporation is fast, and the machine delivers its highest output. As the room's humidity rises toward the consumer's target — typically 40 to 45 percent in winter — the deficit narrows. The evaporation rate decreases proportionally. A humidifier rated at 4 gallons per day at 20 percent RH may deliver 2 to 2.5 gallons at 35 percent and less at 40. The headline number describes the machine's performance at the condition of maximum need, which is also the condition the consumer is trying to leave behind. The number is accurate. The condition is the one the consumer will not stay in.

The "covers rooms up to 500 square feet" claim compounds the problem. The room-size calculation uses the output capacity at the test condition, assumes an 8-foot ceiling, assumes a specific air exchange rate, and assumes a specific starting humidity. A leaky older home — 3 air changes per hour — replaces humid indoor air with dry outdoor air six times faster than a tight new-construction home at 0.5 ACH. A 200-square-foot bedroom in a 1950s house may demand more humidification than a 500-square-foot bedroom in a 2020 house. The box measured square footage. The room provided square footage, ceiling height, construction tightness, and outdoor conditions. The label controlled for one variable. The physics depends on four.

Tank capacity is the reservoir's volume — how often the consumer refills. It is not the output rate and not the moisture delivered to the air. Two humidifier technologies complicate even this simple measure. An evaporative unit draws air through a wet wick; the evaporation rate self-regulates because the humidity differential governs the physics. An ultrasonic unit vibrates water into microscopic droplets; the output rate is set by the consumer's knob, not by the room's condition. An ultrasonic unit in a tight room can over-humidify to 60 percent while the consumer believes more is better. It can also aerosolize the minerals dissolved in hard tap water — calcium, magnesium, silica — depositing fine white dust on surfaces and in airways. An evaporative unit leaves the minerals in the wick. Same "4 gallons per day" on both boxes. Different substances in the air.

A capacity measured at a humidity the consumer will not remain at, projected onto a room whose construction the label does not know, filled from a tap whose mineral content the label does not disclose, via a technology whose mechanism the label does not distinguish. The consumer reads "4 gallons" and inhabits a room that receives 2.
