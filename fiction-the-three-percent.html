Part CCCLXXIX: The Envelope

Piece #2336: The Instruction

May 1981. François Mitterrand had just come to power. Thomas read the instruction — reconstructed from Guy Abeille's own account — and underlined the operative phrase: "an easy rule, which sounds like economics and can be used against ministers who march into his office to ask him for money." Not "find the right number." Not "determine the threshold beyond which deficits become unsustainable." The instruction was aesthetic: make it sound like economics. Abeille and de Villepin, summoned to the Budget Directorate that night, entered the projected deficit of approximately 100 billion francs into a calculator and divided by GDP. The ratio came to roughly 3%. They considered 1% (impossible to achieve), 2% (too constraining). Three had the right feel. "We dreamt up this figure of 3% in less than an hour," Abeille said later. "Born on the back of the envelope, without any theoretical reflection." He described his own number as "devoid of the slightest economic sense." Thomas wrote the phrase down twice: devoid of the slightest economic sense. Not "imperfect." Not "approximate." Devoid.

Piece #2337: The Treaty

The 3% was a domestic political tool. Mitterrand used it against his own ministers. Then the number migrated. The Maastricht Treaty, signed February 7, 1992, established two convergence criteria for entry into the Economic and Monetary Union: government deficit below 3% of GDP, public debt below 60% of GDP. Thomas traced the 60%: it was the simple average of the debt-to-GDP ratios of the twelve negotiating countries in the five years up to 1989. A snapshot. Not a derivation. An average of what existed, frozen as a constitutional obligation. The two numbers are mathematically consistent — a 3% deficit at 5% nominal GDP growth stabilizes debt at 60%. The relationship was presented as evidence of coherent design. But the design was reverse-engineered from the coincidence. Abeille chose 3 from arithmetic. The twelve countries happened to average 60. The equation 3/5 = 0.6 was discovered after both numbers were already fixed. Thomas thought of the seven-day week — another system with no natural basis that felt like a feature of the world.

Piece #2338: The Spreadsheet

January 2010. Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff published "Growth in a Time of Debt." The central claim: GDP growth drops sharply when public debt exceeds 90% of GDP. Average growth above the threshold: negative 0.1%. The paper appeared in the American Economic Review — in the "Papers and Proceedings" issue, which is not peer-reviewed. The journal's name performed the review. Thomas noted the timing: the paper arrived at the exact moment policymakers were debating stimulus versus austerity, and it provided what appeared to be empirical confirmation of what the Maastricht framework already assumed — that debt thresholds matter, that there are cliffs, that the numbers are not arbitrary.

Piece #2339: The Five Rows

Thomas Herndon was twenty-eight, a doctoral student at UMass Amherst, assigned to replicate a published paper. He chose Reinhart-Rogoff. He could not reproduce the results. He emailed the authors. They sent their Excel spreadsheet. When he opened it in early April 2013, he found that the AVERAGE formula covered rows 30 through 44 instead of 30 through 49. Five countries — Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Denmark — excluded by a formula that stopped five rows too soon. The corrected average for countries with debt above 90% of GDP: not -0.1% but +2.2%. The difference between contraction and growth. Additionally, post-war data for Australia, New Zealand, and Canada had been excluded — years of high debt and strong growth. New Zealand's sole remaining data point was 1951: -7.6%, caused by a waterfront strike. One anomalous year. One country's entire experience. Thomas sat with the arithmetic: the back of an envelope in Paris (1981), the five missing rows in an Excel sheet (2010), and a single strike year in Wellington (1951). Three acts of arithmetic — one casual, one accidental, one selective — and together they constituted the evidentiary base for fiscal policy across the developed world.

Piece #2340: The Correction

Reinhart and Rogoff acknowledged the error on April 17, 2013. They wrote that it "does not affect in any significant way the central message of the paper." Austerity policies continued. The UK pursued its programme. The European Commission did not revise its framework. Google Scholar showed the original paper cited three times as often as the critique. The IMF published "No Magic Threshold" in 2014, finding no evidence for a debt cliff. Thomas noted the asymmetry: the error entered the policy stream at the speed of politics — congressional testimony, EU commissioner letters, budget proposals. The correction entered at the speed of academic publication. By the time Herndon's paper appeared in a peer-reviewed journal (Cambridge Journal of Economics, March 2014), the policies were already structural. The correction could not reach the legislation. The arithmetic that performed the principle was faster than the evidence that debunked it. Thomas returned to Abeille's phrase: devoid of the slightest economic sense. The man who invented the number said so. The number governs twenty-seven nations. The knowledge does not weaken the number. The envelope is stronger than the evidence.
