The Sample

In 1928, Harry Nyquist proved that a continuous signal can be perfectly reconstructed from discrete samples taken at twice the signal's highest frequency. Claude Shannon formalized this in 1949. The reconstruction is not an approximation. It is mathematically lossless.

A CD samples audio at 44,100 Hz, capturing frequencies up to 22,050 Hz. The human audible range extends to approximately 20,000 Hz, declining with age. The sampling rate exceeds the ear's ceiling. Every audible frequency is captured with mathematical perfection. This was established when Philips and Sony released the Red Book standard in 1982.

Hi-res audio samples at 96,000 or 192,000 Hz, capturing frequencies up to 48,000 or 96,000 Hz. No human hears these frequencies. No recording microphone captures meaningful content there. The bandwidth extension is real — the file faithfully encodes inaudible information. Meyer and Moran tested perception in 2007: 554 double-blind trials, trained listeners, comparing hi-res originals against CD downconversions. No statistically significant distinction. Reiss's 2016 meta-analysis found a tiny effect (1.8% above chance) attributed not to direct perception of ultrasonic content but to intermodulation distortion in playback equipment — the inaudible frequencies creating audible artifacts, an argument for less bandwidth, not more.

Bit depth is the second number. Sixteen bits provide 96 dB of dynamic range — spanning the gap from a quiet room's noise floor to the threshold of pain. Twenty-four bits provide 144 dB, extending 48 dB below the thermal noise of any consumer amplifier and below the self-noise of any microphone. In the studio, 24-bit provides mixing headroom. In playback, the additional resolution corresponds to nothing the equipment can reproduce.

When listeners perceive a difference between standard and hi-res releases, the perception is usually real and usually attributable to remastering. Hi-res releases are frequently remixed and remastered with care the original release did not receive. The sound improves because the mix improved, not because the format captured more. The consumer attributes the improvement to the format because the format is what changed visibly — the sample rate and bit depth appear on the product page, the mastering credits do not.

Two numbers perform audio quality: sample rate and bit depth. Among the dozens of factors determining what the listener actually hears — recording technique, microphone quality, mixing decisions, mastering dynamics, speaker response, room acoustics, hearing acuity — these two contribute the least marginal improvement at the consumer endpoint while occupying the most prominent position on the product page. The specification describes the container. The ear hears the content. The theorem that proved the container was already sufficient was published seventy-seven years before the premium tier that sells a larger one.
