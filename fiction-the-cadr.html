Part CCXXXVII: The Clean Air

Piece #1626: The Three Particles

The box says CADR: Smoke 200, Dust 180, Pollen 220. Three numbers. Three particle size ranges. Smoke: 0.09-1.0 micrometers. Dust: 0.5-3.0 micrometers. Pollen: 5-11 micrometers. The consumer reads "200" and understands air cleaning power. The number measures cubic feet per minute of air in a 1,008-cubic-foot test chamber from which particles of a given size distribution have been removed, over and above the rate at which those particles would settle naturally.

The AHAM AC-1 standard — the Association of Home Appliance Manufacturers' test protocol — defines the room, the particle injection, the measurement interval, and the calculation. The number is real. It measures something precise. But it measures particle removal from chamber air, and the consumer is asking: will this machine make the air in my house better?

Between the number and the answer lies everything the test chamber excludes: the consumer's room size, ceiling height, air exchange rate, particle generation rate, door and window behavior, HVAC interaction, and the question of which particles matter to this consumer in this house.

Piece #1627: The Room That Wasn't

The test chamber is 1,008 cubic feet — approximately a 12×12-foot room with a 7-foot ceiling. The consumer's living room may be 2,500 cubic feet. The AHAM recommendation: choose a purifier whose smoke CADR is at least two-thirds the room's floor area in square feet. A 200-square-foot room needs a CADR of at least 133.

This calculation appears nowhere on the box. The box gives three CADR numbers — the chamber results. The consumer who buys a CADR 200 unit for a 400-square-foot great room has purchased a machine rated for half the space. The purifier will run. It will remove particles. It will not remove them fast enough to keep ahead of a room twice the test chamber's volume.

The gap is not a deficiency of the standard. The standard measures the purifier. The consumer needs to size the purifier to the room. The label provides the first step and trusts the consumer to perform the second. The consumer reads the first step as the answer.

Piece #1628: The Particle That Wasn't There

CADR measures particles between 0.09 and 11 micrometers. Volatile organic compounds — formaldehyde, benzene, toluene — are gas-phase molecules, orders of magnitude smaller than the smallest tested particle. CADR measures nothing about gases. An activated carbon filter that adsorbs VOCs has no number on the CADR label. A HEPA-only unit and a HEPA-plus-carbon unit can carry the same CADR ratings.

The consumer who buys an air purifier because the new furniture off-gases formaldehyde — a known human carcinogen at indoor concentrations that build in sealed modern construction — reads CADR and finds no signal. The number measures what a mechanical filter catches. It says nothing about what an adsorbent removes. The label-friendly measurement pushes the non-label-friendly measurement off the box.

Ultrafine particles below 0.09 micrometers — the size range most associated with cardiovascular health effects — also fall outside the CADR measurement window. The standard tests the particle sizes that purifiers handle most efficiently, not the particle sizes that matter most to health.

Piece #1629: The Speed the Consumer Doesn't Use

CADR is measured at the purifier's highest fan speed. The consumer runs the purifier on medium or low because high speed is loud. At medium speed, the CADR drops to 50-60% of rated. At low speed, 25-35%. The number on the box is the number at the speed the consumer doesn't use, for the same reason ceiling fans advertise maximum CFM — the largest number is the most marketable number.

Noise level at each speed is sometimes printed on the box but never alongside the CADR at that speed. The consumer cannot look at the label and ask: at a noise level I'll tolerate, how much air does this purifier clean? That question requires two data points paired — CADR at X dB — and the label provides each in isolation, if it provides the noise figure at all.

The AHAM Verifide certification mark confirms third-party CADR testing. It does not certify the relationship between noise and performance. The consumer sees the mark and reads: verified clean air. The mark says: verified particle removal rate at maximum speed in the test chamber.

Piece #1630: The Filter the Number Forgets

A CADR rating at purchase is a CADR rating with a new filter. Six months of operation with a clogged pre-filter, a saturated HEPA element, or an exhausted carbon bed produces a CADR that may be 30-50% of the rated number. The standard measures the purifier at commissioning. The consumer operates the purifier at degradation.

Filter replacement cost is the true operating expense of an air purifier — $30-80 every 6-12 months — but appears nowhere in the CADR rating or on the box in most cases. The consumer who comparison-shops by CADR compares initial performance. The consumer who lives with the purifier experiences lifetime performance, which is initial performance minus the integral of filter degradation over time.

The CADR is a snapshot. The consumer inhabits a time series. The label provides the peak of the curve and lets the curve decay in silence.
