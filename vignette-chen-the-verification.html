The Verification

Chen's father died in 2019. Pancreatic cancer, the chart said. Four months from diagnosis to death. The oncologist had been confident. The imaging had been clear. The pathology report from the biopsy had confirmed adenocarcinoma. Everything was consistent. Everything was documented. Everything was certain.

There was no autopsy.

Maren finds this out two years later, reading about Goldman's 1983 study. Reads about the 10% Class I error rate — the rate at which the autopsy found a missed diagnosis that might have changed treatment and prolonged survival. Reads that the rate was stable across three decades. Reads that the autopsy rate in American hospitals is now below 5%.

Maren does not tell Chen what she is reading.

Maren is not thinking that Chen's father was misdiagnosed. Pancreatic cancer is pancreatic cancer — the imaging was clear, the biopsy was confirmed, the disease progressed as expected. Maren is thinking about the 10%. About the possibility, across millions of deaths, that some fraction of confident diagnoses are wrong in ways that only the autopsy would have revealed. About the fact that no one checked. About the fact that not checking is now the standard.

Chen comes home. Sees the papers on the table. "What are you reading?"

"Goldman 1983. The autopsy discrepancy study."

"Why?"

Maren pauses. "Because the hospitals stopped checking. And then the hospitals said the checking was no longer necessary because the numbers improved. But the numbers improved because they stopped checking."

Chen sits down. Chen understands circular reasoning — it is one of Maren's persistent subjects, the systems that generate their own confirmations. Chen has listened to Maren describe fire investigation vocabulary and surgical theater and diagnostic labels. This is different. This is about the entire system removing its ability to know when it is wrong.

"The death certificate," Chen says. "My father's death certificate says pancreatic cancer."

"Yes."

"And if the autopsy had been done?"

"It would have said pancreatic cancer. Almost certainly."

"Almost."

"Almost."

The word sits between them. Almost certainly is not certainly. Almost certainly is 90% — or 95% — or 77% — depending on which study, which era, which hospital. Almost certainly is the confidence of a system that stopped verifying its confidence and then pointed to the absence of contradictions as proof.

"The system can't tell the difference," Chen says slowly, "between being right and not being checked."

"No. It can't. Not anymore."

Chen looks at the death certificate she keeps in the filing cabinet. A document with four lines. Filled out by a physician who did not open the body. Entered into a database that drives research funding and public health priorities. One of millions. Unverified. Confident.

"My father is in that database," Chen says.

"Yes."

"And the database is confident."

"Yes."

"And no one checked."

"No one checked."

Chen closes the filing cabinet. Stands in the hallway. Thinks about confidence — what it costs to maintain, what it costs to question, what it costs when the instrument that would have distinguished the two has been removed and the removal has been called improvement.
