Part CCCXXX: The Capsule

Piece #2091: The Aisle

Thomas stands in the pharmacy. The aisle is divided by a counter. Behind the counter: drugs — tested, approved, prescribed, dispensed by a licensed pharmacist. Before the counter: supplements — untested, unapproved, unprescribed, selected by the consumer from an open shelf. Both come in capsules. Both list milligram amounts. Both have dosage instructions. Both sit under the same fluorescent light.

The capsule on the pharmacy shelf says "500 mg." The capsule behind the counter says "500 mg." The consumer reads both and infers the same process: a laboratory determined the effective dose. A study confirmed the benefit. A government agency reviewed the evidence. An approval was granted. The number is the same. The process behind the number is entirely different. Behind the counter: twelve years, $2.6 billion, Phase I through Phase III, FDA review. On the shelf: nothing. The product may have been manufactured this morning. No one reviewed anything. The capsule is the disguise.

Piece #2092: The Category

In 1994, Congress created a regulatory category by subtraction. Take a drug. Remove the pre-market approval. Remove the efficacy requirement. Remove the manufacturing standards (initially). Reverse the burden of proof — make the FDA prove the product unsafe rather than requiring the manufacturer to prove it safe. What remains is a dietary supplement.

Thomas reads DSHEA — PL 103-417. Senators Hatch and Harkin. Mel Gibson in a television advertisement, federal agents raiding a health food store, a nation persuaded that the government wanted to take their vitamins. The law passed with broad bipartisan support. The supplement industry spent two million dollars on lobbying. The market now exceeds sixty billion dollars annually. Eighty thousand products. Nine million dollars in FDA enforcement budget. The ratio of products to enforcement dollars is the ratio of ambition to oversight: approximately nine thousand to one.

Piece #2093: The Panel

The Supplement Facts panel — 21 CFR 101.36 — is modeled on the Nutrition Facts panel and the Drug Facts panel. Same fonts. Same layout. Same structure: serving size, amount per serving, percent daily value. The consumer has been trained by decades of food and drug labeling to read this format as governmental authority. The panel performs the FDA's review. The FDA did not review.

Thomas finds the required disclaimer: "This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease." Forty-three words. Back of the bottle. Small type. Regulatory font size. The disclaimer is the concealment performed as disclosure. Its existence on the label performs transparency. Its placement, its formulaic repetition across eighty thousand products, its small type against a crowded background — these ensure it is not read. The disclosure is the disguise. The transparency is the opacity.

Piece #2094: The Contents

Newmaster and colleagues (2013, BMC Medicine): DNA barcoding of 44 herbal supplement products from 12 companies. Fifty-nine percent contained plant species not listed on the label. Thirty-three percent showed product substitution — the labeled ingredient was absent, replaced by a cheaper alternative. One product labeled "St. John's Wort" contained only Senna alexandrina — a laxative. The consumer bought an antidepressant herb and received a purgative.

Thomas sits with the number: fifty-nine percent. More than half the products tested contained something they did not claim. The capsule is the container. The container performs identity. The identity is unreliable. But the capsule does not care what it contains. The capsule is a format — gelatin or cellulose, standardized size, swallowable. The format is honest: it is a container. The label is the liar. The label performs the contents the capsule does not verify.

Piece #2095: The Borrowing

Every element of the supplement borrows. The capsule borrows the pharmaceutical form. The milligram borrows the pharmacological precision. The Supplement Facts panel borrows the governmental authority of the Nutrition Facts panel. The pharmacy shelf borrows the proximity of the prescription counter. "Supports immune health" borrows the therapeutic language of disease treatment. "Take two daily" borrows the dosage regimen of a prescribed medication.

Thomas counts the borrowings: six elements, each performing an authority the regulation specifically withheld. The product exists in a regulatory category defined by exemption — it is NOT a drug, it is NOT required to prove safety, it is NOT required to demonstrate efficacy. But every element of its presentation performs the identity of the category it was exempted from. The exemption is invisible. The performance is everywhere.

The capsule is the consummate borrower. It takes the shape without the substance, the form without the validation, the format without the process. It is the regulatory equivalent of the breed standard's aesthetic: a surface that performs one thing while the mechanism beneath it operates as another entirely. The capsule says medicine. The regulation says food. The consumer swallows the capsule.
