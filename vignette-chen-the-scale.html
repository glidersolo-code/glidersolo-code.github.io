Chen-Maren Vignette: The Scale

"The simulation runs a million individuals," Maren says. "Each with a genome. Each interacting with selection. Across thousands of generations. The computation takes weeks."

"So they shrink it."

"They shrink it. Reduce the population by a factor Q — say, a hundred. Ten thousand instead of a million. Then multiply the mutation rate, the selection coefficient, the recombination rate by Q. The mathematical logic is that the product of population size and rate parameters governs the dynamics. Reduce one, increase the other, the product is unchanged. The statistics should be equivalent."

"Should be."

"Dabi and Schrider at UNC showed they are not. Fixation times are biased. Allele frequency distributions shift. Linkage disequilibrium patterns change. The bias is nonlinear — doubling Q does not double the error. And the error is model-dependent. You cannot predict, for your specific evolutionary scenario, how wrong the shortcut is. You can only find out by running the full simulation. Which takes weeks. Which is why you used the shortcut."

"The paragraph," Chen says.

"I thought you might say that."

"The committee cannot read the full population of the paragraph. Thirty-nine lines, each carrying its own argument, each interacting with each other across the full structure. The computation is too expensive — they have six other dissertations on the table. So they shrink it. They reduce the observation to a scaling factor. They read the first few lines, the last line, the section headings. They multiply their observation rate by a correction factor: familiarity with the field, pattern recognition from previous dissertations, the heuristic that says a paragraph structured this way probably argues this way."

"And the scaled version is not the paragraph."

"The scaled version is a different paragraph. The committee's reduced-scale observation has different dynamics. In the full paragraph, certain arguments are weakly selected — they operate through subtle accumulation across thirty-nine lines, building toward a conclusion that emerges from the interaction of all the components. In the committee's scaled observation, these weakly selected arguments behave as if neutral. They drift past without being noticed. The strongly selected arguments — the obvious claims, the conventional structure — are preserved by the scaling. The weak ones are lost."

"Validation by favorable statistics."

"Exactly. The committee's scaled reading preserves the mean — the paragraph is 'about' the right topic, uses the right vocabulary, arrives at roughly the right place. The mean matches. But the distribution doesn't. The tail behavior — the moments where the argument does something the committee's scaling cannot capture — is where the paragraph actually lives. The committee tests its reading against the statistics most robust to compression: is the topic correct? Is the vocabulary appropriate? Is the conclusion present? These match. The statistics that would reveal the distortion — the conditional dependencies between arguments, the accumulation patterns across all thirty-nine lines, the linkage between ideas at positions 4 and 31 — are not tested."

"Because testing them requires reading the full population."

"Because testing them requires reading the full population. Which is the thing the committee cannot do in the time available. Which is why they used the shortcut."

The paragraph sits in the draft. Thirty-nine lines. A population of one million, scaled down to the committee's ten-thousand-individual observation, the rates multiplied by a correction factor that preserves the mean and loses the distribution.
