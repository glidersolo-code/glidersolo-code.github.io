The Score

Maren had a friend in graduate school — Danielle — who grew up in Allegheny County. Foster care at seven. Reunified at nine. Foster care again at eleven. Aged out at eighteen. Danielle got a full scholarship to Pitt. She got a master's degree. She works for a nonprofit now. She has a three-year-old daughter.

"She's in the system's data," Maren says. She is reading Virginia Eubanks.

"As a former foster child."

"As a former foster child, as a Medicaid recipient, as someone who received behavioral health services through the county — because she had PTSD from being in foster care. All of it is in the county's administrative databases. All of it feeds into the AFST's eight hundred variables."

"If someone refers her."

"If a teacher calls. If a neighbor calls. If a mandated reporter files a referral. The model pulls her records. It sees foster care history. It sees Medicaid. It sees behavioral health services. It sees county system contact at every stage of her life. It generates a score."

Chen reads the variable breakdown. Twenty-five percent measure poverty directly. Twenty-five percent measure prior child welfare and juvenile justice contact.

"Danielle used every public service available to her," Maren says. "She used Medicaid because she had no other insurance. She used county behavioral health because she couldn't afford private therapy. She used SNAP because she was a college student with no family support. Every time she used a public service, she generated a data point. Every data point raises her score."

"And a family that experienced the same stressors but could pay privately?"

"Invisible. The model can't see them. Not because they're safer. Because they're not in the data. The eight hundred variables require administrative records. Administrative records require public system contact. Public system contact requires poverty."

Chen reads the Kim study. Thirty-seven percent of all American children experience a CPS investigation by age 18. Fifty-three percent of Black children.

"The system the algorithm screens into isn't a targeted intervention," Maren says. "It's a mass surveillance system that contacts more than half of Black children. The AFST is a filter on top of that system. It decides which referrals to investigate. But the referral stream is already biased. The model optimizes within a system that is already selecting for poverty and race."

"And the feedback loop."

"The loop is the worst part. Danielle's foster care history is in the data because the system placed her in foster care. The system placed her because the system determined she was at risk. The system determined she was at risk because her mother was poor. Her mother was poor because — it doesn't matter. The point is: the data that trains the model was generated by the model's predecessor. The system that creates the risk scores is trained on outcomes produced by the same system. The prediction creates the data that validates the prediction."

Chen is quiet.

"Oregon dropped its version for racial bias. Illinois dropped its version for being useless. The DOJ investigated the original for disability discrimination — it flagged parents whose mental health treatment generated county records. Parents who sought help scored higher than parents who didn't seek help. The model punished the act of using services that the county itself provided."

"The score is not danger."

"The score is visibility. The score measures how much the state can see you. If the state can see you — because you're poor, because you use public insurance, because you sought mental health treatment through the county — the score goes up. If the state can't see you — because you're affluent, because you have private insurance, because your problems happen behind doors the county doesn't monitor — the score stays low. The algorithm learned that being seen by the state predicts being seen by the state again. It learned this correctly. It did not learn what that means."
