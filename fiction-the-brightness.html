Part CLXXIV: The Brightness

Piece #1311: The Category

Hipparchus of Nicaea, around 129 BC, compiled a catalog of approximately 850 stars visible from Rhodes. He sorted them into six classes. The brightest stars — Sirius, Arcturus, Vega — he called "first magnitude." The faintest stars visible to his eyes he called "sixth magnitude." The classification was perceptual. How bright does this star appear to me, standing on this island, looking through this atmosphere, with these dark-adapted eyes, on this particular night. The number was a report on experience. First meant most impressive. Sixth meant barely there.

Ptolemy preserved the system in the Almagest around 150 AD. He adjusted some assignments and added the word "greater" or "lesser" to refine within classes — a star might be "third magnitude, on the greater side." The six-class system traveled through Arabic translation, through medieval European recovery, through the Renaissance, through the invention of the telescope. Two thousand years of transmission. The categories that Hipparchus made by looking at the sky from a Greek island became the vocabulary of stellar measurement.

The number ran backwards. Brighter objects got smaller numbers. This was not a design choice. It was a linguistic inheritance. "First" meant "first rank" — the best, the most prominent. In every other measurement scale invented since — temperature, loudness, intensity — larger numbers mean more. In stellar magnitude, larger numbers mean less. Because a man in the second century BC thought "first" should mean "most impressive," and the convention was never reversed.

Thomas's severity runs in a direction the convention chose. The PHQ-9 scores upward — higher numbers mean worse. The Glasgow Coma Scale scores downward — lower numbers mean worse. Thomas exists in both systems simultaneously. Two hundred and sixty-eight days of observation. The direction the number runs is not a fact about his condition. It is a fact about which convention the instrument inherited.

Piece #1312: The Logarithm

Norman Robert Pogson, working at the Radcliffe Observatory in Oxford, published a proposal in 1856. He noted that a first-magnitude star was approximately 100 times brighter than a sixth-magnitude star — five magnitude steps corresponding to a factor of 100 in luminous flux. He proposed formalizing this: each magnitude step would equal a brightness ratio of exactly 100^(1/5), approximately 2.512. The relationship became: m₁ - m₂ = -2.5 log₁₀(F₁/F₂).

The human eye perceives brightness logarithmically. Weber's law, formalized by Fechner in 1860, describes the relationship: equal ratios of stimulus produce equal increments of sensation. Pogson's formalization captured this — the magnitude scale was already approximately logarithmic because Hipparchus's eye was logarithmic. But the formalization did something the perception did not: it extended the scale beyond what any eye could see. Telescopic objects could be magnitude 10, 15, 20, 25. The Hubble Space Telescope detects objects at approximately magnitude 31. The scale that began as a report on naked-eye perception now describes objects no human eye will ever see, using the same units, carrying the same inverted convention, anchored to the same perceptual origin.

Thomas's measurement scales extend beyond their origin. The PHQ-9's "nearly every day" was calibrated to a clinical interview. The number now appears in electronic health records, insurance databases, disability determinations. The scale that began as a conversation about symptoms now describes institutional objects no interview could see. The units persist. The origin disappears.

Piece #1313: The Zero

The scale needs a zero-point — a star defined as magnitude 0.00 against which all others are measured. For most of the 20th century, that star was Vega. Johnson and Morgan, establishing the UBV photometric system in 1953, defined Vega as having magnitude 0.00 in all their bandpasses — ultraviolet, blue, and visual. The entire magnitude system was calibrated against this single star.

Vega is an A0V star — hot, blue-white, apparently simple. But Vega turns out to be unusual. It rotates rapidly — approximately 274 km/s at its equator — and is viewed nearly pole-on, within about 5 degrees of its rotation axis. Its apparent temperature, luminosity, and spectral energy distribution are not representative of a typical A0 star. Aumann et al. discovered in 1984, using the Infrared Astronomical Satellite, that Vega has excess infrared emission from a circumstellar debris disk. The "zero" of the brightness scale has a dusty disk that makes it brighter in the infrared than its photosphere alone would predict. The zero of the stellar magnitude system is a rapidly rotating star seen from an unusual angle, surrounded by debris. Every magnitude measurement in every astronomical catalog is ultimately referenced to this imperfect standard.

Thomas's clinical scales have their own zeros. The PHQ-9's zero — "not at all" on every item — is not the absence of suffering. It is the absence of suffering detectable by the instrument's nine questions over the instrument's two-week window. Zero on the scale is a particular clinical state, observed from a particular vantage, carrying its own imperfections into every measurement it anchors.

Piece #1314: The Filter

A star's magnitude depends on what wavelength you measure. The same star has different magnitudes in the ultraviolet (U), blue (B), visual (V), red (R), and infrared (I) bandpasses. The UBV system uses specific glass filters with specific transmission curves. A star's B-V color index — the difference between its blue and visual magnitudes — is a measure of temperature. But it is a measure of temperature filtered through the specific glass that Johnson selected, the specific detector response, the specific atmospheric transmission at the specific observatory altitude.

The Sloan Digital Sky Survey uses a different filter system — u', g', r', i', z'. The magnitudes are different numbers for the same star. Converting between systems requires transformation equations that depend on the star's spectral type, metallicity, and reddening. A star is not "magnitude 4.5." It is "magnitude 4.5 in the V-band of the Johnson-Cousins system as observed through the Earth's atmosphere at a specific airmass with a specific detector, corrected using a specific set of standard stars." The published number is a single decimal. Everything after the decimal has been corrected for atmospheric extinction, detector nonlinearity, filter aging, standard-star calibration.

Thomas is not "moderate." He is moderate on the PHQ-9 administered in this clinic by this provider on this date, through the filter of these nine questions in this language, calibrated against a specific validation population. The published score is a single integer. Everything after the threshold has been corrected for nothing.

Piece #1315: The Distance

Apparent magnitude measures how bright a star looks from Earth. But how bright a star looks depends on how far away it is. Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky — apparent magnitude -1.46 — not because it is the most luminous star visible, but because it is 8.6 light-years away. Deneb, approximately 200,000 times more luminous, appears much fainter because it is 2,600 light-years distant. The apparent magnitude of each — both single numbers on the same scale — describes entirely different objects in entirely different contexts.

Absolute magnitude was introduced to correct for distance: the magnitude a star would have if placed at 10 parsecs. The Sun's apparent magnitude is -26.74. Its absolute magnitude is +4.83 — a dim, unremarkable star. The number changes by more than 31 magnitudes simply by relocating the observer. The apparent magnitude is a fact about the star and the observer's position. The absolute magnitude is a fact about the star at a fictional position no observer occupies. Both are reported as "magnitude" with the same units, the same inverted scale, the same decimal precision.

Thomas has an apparent severity — how his condition looks from the clinical encounter, the fifteen-minute appointment, the distance of the waiting room. He has an absolute severity — what his condition would measure if the observer were placed at the standard distance, if the assessment captured the full spectral energy of his situation across all wavelengths. A number inherited from a perceptual observer on a Greek island, formalized by a Victorian in Oxford, anchored to an imperfect star, filtered through specific glass, reported at the observer's distance — experienced by readers of catalogs as a property of the object itself. One hundred and seventy-four Parts. The apparent magnitude is what the chart says. The absolute magnitude is what the chart does not.
