The Five Evaluations

A dialogue among five assessment mechanisms.

Experience (#578, student evaluations): The student rates the teaching. The student measures how the teaching felt. The institution treats the feeling as the outcome.

Conversation (#579, unstructured interview): The interviewer rates the candidate. The interviewer measures how the conversation felt. The organization treats the feeling as the prediction.

Rating (#580, performance review): The manager rates the employee. The manager measures their own perceptual tendencies. The organization treats the tendencies as the employee's performance.

Adoption (#581, Net Promoter Score): The customer rates the company. The company measures the customer's response culture. The boardroom treats the culture as the relationship.

Survey (#582, engagement survey): The employee rates the manager. The employee measures their feelings about interpersonal climate. The organization treats the feelings as the employee's disposition.

Experience: We are five instruments. Each of us asks a question.

Conversation: Each of us receives an answer.

Rating: Each of us reports the answer as something other than what was answered.

Adoption: I ask about likelihood to recommend. The answer measures response culture. I report customer loyalty.

Survey: I ask about feelings toward the manager. The answer measures working conditions filtered through interpersonal perception. I report employee engagement.

Experience: I ask about teaching satisfaction. The answer measures the experience of being in a classroom. I report teaching effectiveness.

Conversation: I ask nothing. The candidate speaks. I measure how the speaking felt. I report job fitness.

Rating: I ask the manager to assign a number. The number measures the manager. I report the employee.

Survey: The pattern is identical across all five of us. The input is one thing. The output is labeled as another. The label is never questioned because the instrument's format performs the validity of the label.

Experience: My format is the evaluation form — Likert scales, competency dimensions, written comments. The form performs objectivity.

Conversation: My format is the interview — scheduled time, prepared questions, professional setting. The setting performs assessment.

Rating: My format is the review — annual cycle, documented ratings, behavioral anchors. The precision performs measurement.

Adoption: My format is the single number — NPS = 42, up or down, one slide. The simplicity performs comprehension.

Survey: My format is the twelve questions — administered, aggregated, reported. The administration performs listening.

Rating: What separates us from each other?

Conversation: Who the rater is. In my case, the interviewer rates the candidate. In yours, the manager rates the employee. In Experience's case, the student rates the teacher. In Survey's case, the employee rates the manager. In Adoption's case, the customer rates the company.

Experience: Five different raters. Five different ratees. Five identical operations: the format performs the validity of the label.

Adoption: And we all share one property: the instrument cannot measure what it claims to measure because the format prevents the measurement. I cannot measure customer loyalty with one question. You cannot measure teaching effectiveness with satisfaction ratings. Rating cannot measure employee performance through the manager's perceptual lens. Conversation cannot predict job performance through conversational impression. Survey cannot measure engagement without measuring the conditions that produce it.

Survey: The format is the limitation. The limitation is the product. The format is the product.

Experience: What would it take to fix any of us?

Rating: For me: multiple raters, calibrated against external outcomes, weighted by rater reliability. Scullen showed 62% of my variance is the rater. Removing the rater effect would require removing the rater — automating the measurement.

Conversation: For me: structure. Standardized questions, behavioral anchors, independent scoring. The structured interview achieves r = 0.51, versus my r = 0.20. But interviewers resist structure because structure removes the feeling that makes the interview feel like an interview.

Experience: For me: measuring learning outcomes directly. Pre-test/post-test designs. Value-added modeling. But learning outcomes are delayed, invisible, and expensive to measure. My immediacy is my advantage and my failure.

Adoption: For me: multiple questions, nuanced response analysis, cultural calibration. But the boardroom wants one number. One slide. Up or down.

Survey: For me: measuring conditions, not feelings. Headcount ratios. Pay trajectories. Schedule stability. Advancement rates. But conditions are structural. The action plan can address communication. The action plan cannot address the budget.

Rating: We are all the same instrument.

Experience: A format that performs a property it does not possess.

Conversation: A feeling that substitutes for a measurement.

Adoption: A number that performs comprehension.

Survey: A question that performs listening.

Rating: Five evaluations. Five formats. One concealment: the format is the assessment. The assessment is the format. Neither is the thing the format claims to assess.
