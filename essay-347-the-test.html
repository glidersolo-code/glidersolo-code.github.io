The Test

Before the law, the test measured learning. After the law, the test measured the test.

No Child Left Behind was signed on January 8, 2002. It required annual standardized testing in reading and mathematics for grades 3-8 and once in high school. Schools that failed to make Adequate Yearly Progress — the percentage of students scoring "proficient" — faced escalating sanctions: public school choice, supplemental services, corrective action, restructuring. The target was 100% proficiency by 2014. The measurement became the target.

The response was rational. If the test determines funding, teach the test. Sixty-two percent of districts increased time for reading and math. Forty-four percent reduced time for science, social studies, arts, and physical education. Instruction narrowed to the tested content and the tested format — not because teachers stopped caring about science, but because the metric did not measure science and the metric had consequences. The school optimized for the metric. The metric recorded the optimization. The number went up. The number called the optimization "learning."

Scores on state tests — the high-stakes tests that determined AYP — rose. Scores on the National Assessment of Educational Progress — a low-stakes audit test that no school could prepare for — showed smaller gains or none. The high-stakes thermometer and the audit thermometer diverged. The patient had not gotten better. The patient had learned how to warm the thermometer.

The proficiency threshold created the "bubble student." If AYP requires 60% proficiency, and the school is at 55%, the five students nearest the threshold are the rational investment. Not the student reading three levels below grade — that student cannot reach proficiency in one year. Not the student already proficient — that student contributes nothing to the metric. The bubble student, the one hovering just below the line, is where every marginal dollar of tutoring and test preparation produces a marginal percentage point of AYP. Resources flowed to the threshold. The threshold was a policy choice. The resource allocation was an emergent consequence of making the policy choice quantitative and consequential.

Atlanta. Forty-four schools. One hundred seventy-eight educators. The Superintendent of the Year. Erasure parties where wrong answers were changed on answer sheets after students had gone home. The investigation came from the Governor's office. The convictions came under RICO — racketeering, the statute designed for organized crime. What the school system had organized was the production of a number. The number that was supposed to measure learning had become valuable enough to forge.

Charles Goodhart named it in 1975: any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes. Donald Campbell in 1979: the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more it will distort the social processes it was meant to monitor. Marilyn Strathern in 1997, the clearest: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

The principle had been articulated three times. The nation enacted the largest educational measurement experiment in history without heeding any of them.

The concealment is not in the test. The test is not biased or flawed in the usual sense. The concealment is in the stability of the format. A school that raised its proficiency rate from 55% to 75% through curriculum narrowing and test preparation reports the same metric, on the same scale, in the same format as a school that raised its rate through deeper instruction. The number does not record how it was produced. The percentage that once meant "students who have mastered the material" now means "students who can pass this specific test after preparing for it." The format — a percentage, a bar chart, a press release — conceals the shift in meaning by presenting the number in unchanged clothing.

The test did not lie. The test measured exactly what it was designed to measure. What it was designed to measure — performance on a standardized assessment — had been a useful proxy for learning when the stakes were low. The proxy worked because no one was trying to game it. The moment the proxy became consequential, the game began, and the proxy stopped being a proxy without changing its appearance.

The score still looks like learning. It still reports in the same units. It still sits in the same column of the same spreadsheet. The corruption is invisible in the output. The number persists while its meaning drains away.
