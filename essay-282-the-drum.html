The Drum

The washing machine is rated at 5.0 cubic feet. The consumer reads this as a measure of laundry capacity — how much the machine can wash. The 5.0 refers to the total internal volume of the drum, measured with water under DOE 10 CFR Part 430, Appendix J. The drum holds 5.0 cubic feet of water. It does not hold 5.0 cubic feet of laundry. Clothes are three-dimensional, compressible, and irregular. The manufacturer's recommended fill level for optimal cleaning and agitation is approximately 60 to 70 percent of drum volume. A 5.0-cubic-foot drum optimally loaded holds about 3.0 to 3.5 cubic feet of loosely placed clothes. The consumer who fills the drum to its rated capacity overpacks by 40 percent. The clothes cannot tumble. The detergent cannot circulate. The machine delivers its worst cleaning performance at its rated capacity.

The test procedure defines the problem. "Capacity" in DOE testing means drum volume — a geometric measurement of the cylindrical interior. No federal test measures usable laundry capacity — the volume of clothes the machine can effectively clean. The word "capacity" appears on both the spec sheet and in the consumer's understanding, but the two refer to different volumes. The spec sheet measures the container. The consumer reads the contents. The gap — approximately 40 percent — is invisible in the number.

The marketing race amplifies the distortion. In 2010, the average top-load washer drum was 3.5 cubic feet. By 2024, it had grown to 5.0 — a 43 percent increase in drum volume. The growth is real: physically larger drums do hold more laundry. But the usable-fill ratio has not changed. The consumer benefits proportionally — more clothes per load — while the marketing number suggests an absolute gain. Samsung 5.5, LG 5.8, Maytag 6.0: the race pushes total drum volume upward because total volume is the number on the spec sheet, and the consumer compares numbers. Larger numbers win.

The European approach offers a contrast. European washing machines are rated in kilograms — a "9 kg" washer holds 9 kilograms of dry laundry. The metric is imperfect: fabric density varies, a load of denim weighs differently from a load of synthetics. But the kilogram rating measures what the consumer puts into the machine, not what the machine contains. The cubic-foot rating measures the drum's volume. One metric faces the laundry. The other faces the drum. The consumer knows how many towels and sheets they have. The consumer does not know how many cubic feet those towels and sheets occupy.

The overpacking completes the cycle. The consumer who reads 5.0 cubic feet and fills the drum accordingly gets a comforter that is wet on the outside and dry in the middle — a load that exceeded the capacity the machine was never designed to hold at full rated volume. The machine washed the perimeter. The spec sheet described the container. The consumer read the contents. The number was precise, standardized, reproducible, and irrelevant to the question the consumer was trying to answer, which was not "how large is the drum" but "how much laundry can I wash."
