Part CCCXIV: The Fragrance

Piece #2011: The List

Thomas turned the bottle around and read the ingredient list. Water. Sodium lauryl sulfate. Cocamidopropyl betaine. Glycerin. Fragrance. Citric acid. Sodium chloride. Methylchloroisothiazolinone.

Eight ingredients. He counted them on his fingers. Eight substances in the bottle.

He was wrong. One of those words was not a substance. One of those words was a door, and behind the door were rooms he could not see.

Piece #2012: The Container

The fragrance chemist's workstation held forty-seven bottles. Each bottle contained a single compound — a solvent, a fixative, an aromatic, a stabilizer. She combined them according to a formula developed over eighteen months. The formula was proprietary. The formula was a trade secret.

"How many compounds in this fragrance?" Thomas asked.

"Forty-seven."

"And on the label?"

"One word. 'Fragrance.'"

"So the consumer reads one ingredient."

"The consumer reads one word. The word contains forty-seven compounds. The law permits this. The Fair Packaging and Labeling Act requires ingredient listing. Trade secret protection exempts the fragrance formula from disclosure. Both operate in the same legal framework. Both appear on the same label."

Thomas looked at the forty-seven bottles. Limonene. Linalool. Coumarin. Diethyl phthalate. Benzyl benzoate. Each one had a name. Each name could appear on the label, individually, the way the EU required for twenty-six known allergens. Each name did not appear. Each name was inside the word.

Piece #2013: The Allergen

The woman showed Thomas the rash on her wrists. Red, raised, itching — contact dermatitis. She had been using the same lotion for three years without incident. Then the formula changed.

"What ingredient causes it?" Thomas asked.

"I don't know. The label says 'fragrance.' My dermatologist suspects cinnamaldehyde. But cinnamaldehyde isn't on the label. It's inside the word 'fragrance.' I can't confirm it without patch testing."

"Can you read the label in Europe?"

"In Europe, cinnamaldehyde would be listed individually if it exceeded 0.001% in a leave-on product. I could see it. I could avoid it. Here, it's invisible."

Thomas looked at the lotion bottle. The ingredient list was printed in small type, in descending order of predominance, following the same format as every other regulated label. Every ingredient was individually named — except one. The one that could contain the allergen. The one that could contain anything. The one word that looked like all the others but was not like any of them.

Piece #2014: The Authorization

Thomas read the statute. Section 301 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Trade secrets. Protected from disclosure. The fragrance formula is intellectual property. The consumer's right to know what is applied to their skin is weighed against the manufacturer's right to protect a formula. The manufacturer wins.

He read the EU regulation. No 1223/2009. Twenty-six known allergenic substances. Individually listed above threshold concentrations. The trade secret is partially maintained — the full formula remains proprietary. Only the allergens are disclosed. A compromise between the consumer's skin and the manufacturer's formula.

Two regulatory systems. Two different answers to the same question. One seals the container entirely. The other cracks it open at the allergen threshold. Both are called consumer protection. One protects the consumer from ignorance. The other protects the manufacturer from disclosure. Both use the same word — "protection" — to describe what they do.

Piece #2015: The Singularity

Thomas stood in the drugstore, holding a bottle of shampoo, reading the ingredient list. He had learned to read each word as what it was — a chemical compound, individually named, individually accountable. All of them were singular. All of them were transparent. All of them were one thing.

Except one.

"Fragrance" sat in the middle of the list, wearing the same font as every other ingredient, occupying the same space, following the same descending-order format. It looked like the others. It behaved like the others. It was not like the others. It was a container disguised as a content. A plural disguised as a singular. A door disguised as a wall.

He put the bottle back. He picked up another. The ingredient list was different — different compounds, different order, different function. But the word was the same. "Fragrance." The same word in every product. The same container in every list. The same singularity concealing a different multiplicity each time.

One word. Forty-seven compounds. The law permits this. The label performs this. The consumer reads this. The skin absorbs this. The word "fragrance" is the only ingredient on the label that is not an ingredient. It is an authorization — a legal permission to not say what is there, printed in the same font as the things that are said, looking like disclosure while performing concealment.
