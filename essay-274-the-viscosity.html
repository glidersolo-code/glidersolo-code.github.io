The Viscosity

In 1911 the Society of Automotive Engineers created a numbered system to classify motor oil by viscosity: SAE 10, 20, 30, 40, 50. The numbers were grades, not measurements — bins into which oils fell depending on their flow characteristics. By 1926 the system had expanded to six grades. By 1933, the W grades appeared: 10W and 20W, designating oils tested at sub-zero temperatures for cold-weather starting. The W stood for Winter. It has been misread as Weight for ninety years.

The multigrade oil changed everything about the system except the label. Before the 1940s, drivers changed oil with the seasons — SAE 10 for winter, SAE 30 for summer. The development of viscosity index improvers — long-chain polymer additives that coil tightly when cold and expand when hot — allowed a single oil to span two grades. A 5W-30 is a base oil thin enough to pass the Cold Cranking Simulator at minus thirty degrees Celsius, chemically modified to achieve a kinematic viscosity between 9.3 and 12.5 centistokes at one hundred degrees Celsius. The two numbers are not a range. They are two tests, at two temperatures, compressed into a single string by a hyphen that represents a chemical achievement.

The grade is a bin, not a point. SAE 30 spans 9.3 to 12.5 centistokes — a 34 percent range. Two oils can share the grade while differing substantially in flow characteristics. The consumer who reads "5W-30" as a specification is reading a classification. The distinction matters: a specification defines a value; a classification defines a neighborhood.

The grade also omits what happens between the two test temperatures. Cold cranking at minus thirty. Kinematic viscosity at one hundred. Nothing in the label describes behavior at twenty or fifty or eighty degrees — the temperatures where most driving occurs. The grade illuminates the endpoints and shadows the middle, like a bridge that names its two shores and says nothing about the span.

Shear stability is absent from the bottle. The polymer chains that expand to create the "30" behavior can break under the sustained stress inside bearings and cam followers. An oil that enters the engine as 5W-30 can shear down to 5W-20 over the course of an oil change interval. The High Temperature High Shear test — ASTM D4683, measured at 150 degrees Celsius and one million reciprocal seconds — was added to SAE J300 to capture this failure mode. But HTHS viscosity appears only in the technical data sheet. The consumer bottle carries the virgin grade, the oil as it was when it entered the bottle, not as it will be when it needs replacing.

Behind the grade sits the API service category — the actual performance specification. API SP, introduced in 2020, defines wear protection, deposit control, oxidation resistance, and emissions compatibility. The category supersedes SN, SM, SL, SJ, the alphabet working backwards through decades of increasing demands. A 5W-30 meeting only SJ is a different product from a 5W-30 meeting SP, despite the identical grade. The grade sorts the consumer's attention. The category sorts the engine's requirements. One is printed large. The other is printed small. The consumer who matches by grade alone has matched viscosity to the shelf. The consumer who matches by category has matched chemistry to the engine. The font size on the bottle reflects the mismatch: the less important specification is more visible, and the more important specification fits in the fine print.
