Fiction The Evaluation

Fiction

Part CDXXXIX: The Evaluation

Piece #2636: The Form

Thomas receives a course evaluation form at the end of the semester. The form has questions. The questions use a Likert scale: strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, strongly agree. Thomas assigns numbers. The numbers will be averaged. The average will be compared to other instructors' averages. The comparison will inform hiring, tenure, promotion.

Thomas considers: what is he evaluating? Thomas enjoyed the course. The instructor was clear. The lectures were well-organized. Thomas felt he learned a great deal.

Thomas wonders: did he learn a great deal? Thomas has no comparison. Thomas did not take the same course from a different instructor. Thomas cannot observe the change in his own cognitive structure. Thomas can observe his experience. Thomas rates the experience.

Piece #2637: The Zero

Thomas reads the meta-analysis. Uttl, White, and Gonzalez, 2017. Multisection studies — courses where different sections take the same final exam. The correlation between evaluation ratings and student learning: effectively zero. Not modest. Not weak. Zero.

Thomas reads the earlier meta-analyses that reported positive correlations — Cohen, 1981; Feldman, 1989. Uttl re-analyzed the data. The positive correlations were artifacts of small-sample bias. When the bias was corrected, the correlation vanished. For decades, the institution relied on numbers that measured nothing related to their stated purpose.

The numbers determined careers. The numbers measured experience. Experience and outcome are different. The institution treated them as the same.

Piece #2638: The Inversion

Thomas reads the Bocconi study. Random assignment — students could not choose their instructors. Braga, Paccagnella, and Pellizzari, 2014.

Instructors who received HIGHER evaluations produced students who performed WORSE in subsequent courses. The correlation was negative. Not zero — negative. The students preferred the instructors who taught them less. The students punished the instructors who taught them more.

Thomas understands: the instructor who challenged him, who assigned difficult material, who pushed past comfort — that instructor received lower ratings. The instructor who entertained, who kept the material accessible, who never exceeded the comfortable — that instructor received higher ratings. The evaluation rewarded the experience. The experience opposed the outcome.

Piece #2639: The Name

Thomas reads the MacNell experiment. Online course. Same instructor. Two sections. One section believed the instructor was male. One section believed the instructor was female. Everything else was identical — same content, same assignments, same grading, same response times.

The "male" instructor received higher ratings. On every dimension. Including promptness — where both sections received identical turnaround. The students did not evaluate the teaching. The students evaluated the name.

Thomas thinks about what a 5-point Likert scale can measure. It can measure the student's impression. The impression includes the material's difficulty, the instructor's gender, the instructor's attractiveness, the time of day, the grade the student expects. The impression includes everything except the outcome — because the outcome is invisible.

Piece #2640: The Number

Thomas thinks about what happens to the evaluation.

The student circles a number. The numbers are collected. The numbers are averaged. The average is reported to one decimal place: 4.2 out of 5.0. The number is compared to the department average: 3.9. The instructor is above average. The number enters the tenure file. The tenure committee reads the number. The committee evaluates the teaching.

The committee evaluates the teaching by reading a number that measures the experience of students who cannot observe the outcome of their own learning, who rate the entertainment as quality, who penalize challenge and reward comfort, and whose ratings correlate with the instructor's perceived gender but not with how much the students actually learned.

The number is the evaluation. The evaluation is the number. Ninety-five percent of institutions use this number to determine who teaches and who does not. The experience is the outcome. The outcome is the experience. The feeling is the learning. The learning is the feeling.
