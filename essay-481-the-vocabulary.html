Essay 481: The Vocabulary

The investigator arrives at the burned building. The floor is damaged — irregular shapes, curved edges, trails. The investigator writes "pour pattern." The word "pour" is not a description of a shape. It is a description of an action. The action has an actor. The actor is a person pouring accelerant. The investigator has not yet analyzed the evidence. The investigator has named it. The naming is the analysis.

This is how the vocabulary of fire investigation worked for most of the twentieth century. The technical terms were not neutral. They were verdicts dressed as observations. "Pour pattern" encoded arson into the description of a floor. "Crazed glass" encoded abnormal heat into the description of a window. "Alligator charring" encoded accelerant into the description of wood. The language of the discipline carried its conclusions the way a seed carries a tree — invisibly, inevitably, before anything is planted.

The investigators were not frauds. They were inheritors. The vocabulary preceded them. It was taught through apprenticeship — from investigator to investigator, generation to generation, like a family name. No one tested it because it did not present itself as a testable claim. It presented itself as a description. Descriptions are not tested. They are verified. The investigator verified that the floor had irregular patterns. The investigator verified that the glass had web-like cracking. The verification was genuine. The vocabulary that converted verification into verdict was invisible.

In 1990, a fire scientist named John Lentini burned a house. No accelerant. The house produced every pattern the vocabulary attributed to arson. The floor had "pour patterns." The glass crazed. The wood showed alligator charring. The house next door had said what no investigator had heard: the fire does this on its own.

In 1992, NFPA 921 renamed the observations. "Pour pattern" became "irregular pattern." Two words changed. A verdict became a question. The correction was not a new instrument. It was a new name. The renaming was the revolution — because the name had been the concealment. As long as the floor was a "pour pattern," the question "what made this?" could not be asked. The name had already answered it.

Between the old vocabulary and the new one, a man was executed. Cameron Todd Willingham, Corsicana, Texas, February 17, 2004. Convicted on more than twenty "indicators of arson" that the corrected vocabulary had already rejected. The correction was twelve years old when the execution occurred. The old names were still in the expert's mouth. The jury heard "pour pattern" and heard a man pouring gasoline. The jury heard "crazed glass" and heard abnormal heat. The jury heard "multiple origins" and heard intentional setting. The physical evidence was real. The names were real. The connection between them was vocabulary.

The deepest concealment is a description that already contains its conclusion. A hypothesis announced as a hypothesis can be tested. A hypothesis disguised as a name cannot. It enters testimony as observation and exits as verdict. The expert appears to reason from evidence to conclusion. The vocabulary has placed the conclusion before the evidence. The expert's reasoning is real. The starting point is not.

What would a corrected world look like? Not a world without fire investigation — fires must be investigated. Not a world without expert testimony — expertise matters. A world where the names of things do not already contain the answers. Where "irregular pattern" replaces "pour pattern." Where the question precedes the name. Where the floor is allowed to be a floor before it is required to be a crime.

The vocabulary was corrected. The verdicts it produced were not.
