Vignette

The Container

Chen is staring at a photograph of a container port when Maren comes in.

"What are you looking at?"

"A port. Shanghai. It handled forty-seven point three million TEU last year."

"TEU?"

"Twenty-foot equivalent unit. One standard shipping container. Twenty feet long, eight feet wide, eight feet six inches tall."

"Forty-seven million containers."

"Forty-seven million crane-lifts. Some of those containers are empty. Roughly one in five. They're being shipped back to Asia because the US imports more boxes than it exports. The empty box counts the same as the full box."

Maren sits down. "Why would you count an empty box?"

"Because the counting unit is the box, not what's inside it. A container of soybeans worth ten thousand dollars and a container of smartphones worth five million dollars also count the same. One TEU each."

"That's absurd."

"It's the format. Malcolm McLean, 1956. Before him, loading a ship took five days and cost five dollars eighty-three per ton. After him, sixteen cents. He didn't speed up the loading. He eliminated it. The goods enter the box at the factory and exit the box at the warehouse. The ship is incidental."

"So the number measures — what?"

"Crane-lifts. Touches. How many times a sealed box was moved from one vehicle to another. A container from Shenzhen to Rotterdam that transships through Singapore gets counted three or four times. Singapore's ranking as the world's second-busiest port substantially reflects that it's in the right place, not that it's producing things."

"It's a logistics measurement pretending to be a trade measurement."

"Yes. And there's something else." Chen pulls up a second photograph — a highway overpass. "This bridge clearance was set to accommodate the height of a truck carrying a container. Railroad tunnels were bored for double-stacking containers. Warehouse loading docks were raised to match the floor of the trailer carrying the container. Port cranes were built to lift these exact dimensions."

"The world was built around the box."

"And once it was, the box looked inevitable. As though twenty feet had always been the natural length for moving things. The dimensions were originally a compromise — American highway limits, European rail gauges, existing ship holds. The compromise became the environment. The environment naturalized the compromise."

Maren is quiet for a moment.

"Thirty-five thousand longshoremen worked the docks of New York in 1954," Chen says. "By 1980, fewer than five thousand. The labor didn't relocate. It ceased to exist. Before the box, you could stand on a dock and watch men carry sacks, roll barrels, hoist crates. After the box, a crane lifts a sealed container from ship to chassis. A truck drives away. What's inside — its weight, its value, its origin — is invisible."

"The box counts the box."

"The box counts the box."
