The Clarity

They were sitting by a lake. Brown water, tea-colored, stained by dissolved tannins from the surrounding forest floor.

"This lake looks dirty," Maren said.

"The Trophic State Index agrees with you," Chen said. "Secchi depth about half a meter. TSI around 65. Eutrophic — nutrient-rich, high productivity, ecologically degraded. That's what the classification says."

"And?"

"The chlorophyll-a concentration is 2 micrograms per liter. Almost no algae. The lake is dark because of dissolved organic matter — humic acids leached from decomposing leaves and peat. The darkness has nothing to do with productivity."

Chen's paragraph was about the relationship between what a system measures and what a system concludes. The Carlson Trophic State Index, published in 1977, converts three variables — Secchi depth, chlorophyll-a, total phosphorus — into a numerical scale from 0 to 100. Below 40, oligotrophic. Between 40 and 50, mesotrophic. Above 50, eutrophic. The three variables need not agree. This lake: eutrophic by transparency, oligotrophic by algal biomass. The index has one word for both kinds of darkness.

"The system was designed for clear-water, temperate, stratified lakes," Chen said. "Naumann and Thienemann described the lakes around them in northern Germany and Sweden in the 1920s. Their binary — oligotrophic and eutrophic — was local and descriptive. It traveled. By 1977 it was a numerical index. By 2000 it was embedded in EU law."

"Law?"

"The Water Framework Directive. Twenty-seven member states. Every surface water body classified by ecological status. The boundary between 'good' and 'moderate' triggers regulatory action — funding, discharge restrictions, monitoring. The boundary depends on which trophic indicator is measured and which reference conditions are assumed. A lake's legal status is a function of which variable the monitoring program could afford to fund."

Maren looked at the brown water. "So this lake is legally eutrophic."

"If the agency measures Secchi depth, yes. If it measures chlorophyll, no. Same lake, same day, two assessments, two legal statuses."

"The lake hasn't changed."

"The lake hasn't changed. The measurement changed. The system can't distinguish between water darkened by algae and water darkened by tannins. The transparency means two different things. The index has one number for both."

The paragraph sat at 0.8. It was, like a trophic state index, a single assessment applied to something multi-dimensional — converting the complexity of what it measured into a number on a scale designed for a different kind of subject, experienced as a fact about the thing rather than the instrument.
