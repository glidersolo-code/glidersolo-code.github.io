Part CLXVII: The Burn

Piece #1276: The Spectrum

Toronto, 1992. James Kerr and C. Thomas McElroy at Environment Canada developed what they called the UV Index — a single number representing the intensity of ultraviolet radiation reaching the Earth's surface. The number was dimensionless: UV irradiance weighted by the erythemal action spectrum and multiplied by 40. The action spectrum — McKinlay and Diffey, Commission Internationale de l'Éclairage, 1987 — described the relative effectiveness of different UV wavelengths at producing erythema. Erythema. Reddening of the skin. Thomas noted: the weighting function that defines the UV Index was calibrated to a single biological effect — sunburn. Not DNA damage. Not immune suppression. Not photoaging. Not melanoma induction. Each of these effects has a different action spectrum, peaking at different wavelengths. The UV Index reports the erythema-weighted number. The number is experienced as "how dangerous the sun is." It measures how effectively the sun produces reddening in a specific kind of skin.

Piece #1277: The Skin

The erythemal action spectrum was derived from studies of fair-skinned individuals — Fitzpatrick skin types I and II. The spectrum describes the wavelength-dependent probability of visible reddening in skin that reddens easily. Thomas found the circularity difficult to escape. The UV Index is a number that measures the sun's capacity to burn skin that burns. For a person with Fitzpatrick type VI — deeply pigmented skin, melanin providing substantial natural photoprotection — the same UV Index number means something categorically different. The number does not adjust. The categories — low, moderate, high, very high, extreme — do not adjust. A UV Index of 8 is "very high" for everyone. The behavioral guidance ("seek shade," "apply sunscreen") is identical. The number was calibrated for the skin type most susceptible to the effect being measured. It was applied universally. The WHO, WMO, UNEP, and ICNIRP jointly adopted the UV Index in 2002 as the global standard. The standard reports a single number. The number carries no information about whom it was calibrated for.

Piece #1278: The Ceiling

The UV Index scale was originally presented as running from 1 to roughly 10 or 11, with 11+ designated "extreme." The scale was designed for the latitudes and altitudes where most Canadians lived — temperate, near sea level. Thomas discovered that in equatorial high-altitude locations — La Paz, Nairobi, the Altiplano — the UV Index routinely reaches 15, 18, even above 20. Researchers at the Licancabur volcano in Chile recorded a UV Index of 43.3. The "extreme" category begins at 11 and has no upper bound, no further differentiation. A UV Index of 11 and a UV Index of 25 receive the same category label, the same color code, the same public health guidance. The scale that was supposed to communicate urgency collapses all urgency above 11 into a single word. The populations living at the highest UV exposures — indigenous communities in the Andes, the East African highlands — receive the least differentiated information from the system designed to protect them.

Piece #1279: The Proxy

Erythema is not cancer. Thomas kept returning to this. The erythemal action spectrum peaks at approximately 297 nanometers. The action spectrum for cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer formation — the primary UV-induced DNA lesion associated with skin cancer — peaks at shorter wavelengths and has a different shape. The action spectrum for immunosuppression differs again. The UV Index weights by erythema because erythema was the first UV biological effect to be systematically quantified, because the CIE had standardized it, and because it was visible. Reddening can be seen. DNA damage cannot. The proxy was chosen for its visibility, not for its relevance to the outcome of greatest concern. Diffey noted this in the British Journal of Dermatology in 2002: a UV exposure scenario that produces high erythema may not produce proportionally high DNA damage, and vice versa. The number that appears on weather forecasts reports a sunburn proxy. It is experienced as a cancer risk indicator. The distance between those two claims is the hidden channel.

Piece #1280: The Forecast

A weather app. "UV Index: 7. High. Wear sunscreen." Thomas looked at the number. Seven. A dimensionless quantity derived from spectrally weighted irradiance using an action spectrum calibrated for fair skin's reddening response, compressed to a single integer representing conditions at solar noon, reported without skin-type adjustment, without mention of the DNA damage spectrum's divergence from the erythema spectrum, without noting that the "extreme" category fails to differentiate between 11 and 25, without acknowledging that the populations most exposed receive the least resolution. The number is seven. It fits on a phone screen. It comes with a sun icon and a color bar. Thomas closed the app. The sun continued. The number continued. The skin it was calibrated for covered roughly 16 percent of the world's population. The number covered all of them.
