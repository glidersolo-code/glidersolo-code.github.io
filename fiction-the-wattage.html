Part CCXXIX: The Wattage

Piece #1586: The Input

Thomas buys a microwave oven. The box says 1100 watts. The competitor says 1000. He buys the 1100 because more watts means faster cooking, hotter food, better performance. But the 1100 watts is the input power — what the magnetron draws from the wall outlet. The cooking power — the microwave energy actually delivered to the food — is approximately 60 to 65 percent of the input. The "1100-watt" microwave cooks at roughly 700 watts. The "1000-watt" competitor, if its efficiency is slightly higher, might cook at 680. The difference the consumer paid for — 100 watts of cooking advantage — may be 20 watts. Or zero. Or negative. The number that determined the purchase measures the wrong thing.

Piece #1587: The Standard

IEC 60705 — the international standard for testing household microwave ovens — defines output power by a water-heating test. One liter of water at a controlled starting temperature, heated for a specified time, temperature rise measured. Output power calculated from the energy transferred to the water. This is the cooking power — the number that predicts how fast food heats. Some manufacturers label this number. Others label the input power. Some label a "rated" wattage that is neither input nor output but a nominal figure derived from the magnetron's specification. The consumer has no way to know which wattage the box describes. The number appears without a label distinguishing input from output. 1100 watts could mean any of three things.

Piece #1588: The Recipe

The frozen dinner says "Microwave on high for 3 minutes (1100W)." The consumer has a "1100-watt" microwave — but the microwave's 1100 watts is input power, and the recipe's 1100 watts assumes output power. The consumer follows the instruction exactly and the food is undercooked by 30 to 40 percent. The recipe and the appliance use the same number to mean different things. No convention establishes which wattage recipes reference. Most recipe developers test on output-rated microwaves. Most consumer microwaves are marketed by input. The gap between the recipe's assumption and the appliance's label is systematic, consistent, and invisible.

Piece #1589: The Power Level

The microwave has ten power levels. "Power level 5" does not mean half the wattage. It means the magnetron cycles on and off — running at full power for half the time and producing zero power for the other half. Average power is halved. Peak power remains constant. The food receives alternating bursts of full energy and periods of nothing. At low power levels, the duty cycle creates hot spots and cold spots more pronounced than at full power, because the off-periods allow thermal diffusion to redistribute heat while the on-periods deposit it unevenly. The consumer reads "50% power" as a gentle, even heat. The appliance delivers intermittent full-intensity pulses. Inverter microwaves (Panasonic, 1988) actually reduce magnetron output, delivering true variable power — but the power-level display is identical. "Power level 5" on a cycling microwave and "Power level 5" on an inverter microwave produce different thermal profiles from the same label.

Piece #1590: The Interior

The label says 1.6 cubic feet interior capacity. The food sits on a turntable. The turntable diameter determines the maximum dish size. A 1.6-cubic-foot cavity with a 13.5-inch turntable accommodates most dinner plates. But the wattage-per-cubic-foot ratio — which determines how quickly the oven heats a given volume of food — is invisible. A 700-watt output microwave with a 1.6-cubic-foot cavity cooks more slowly than a 700-watt output microwave with a 1.1-cubic-foot cavity, because the same energy fills a larger space. The consumer compares watts and cubic feet separately. The ratio that determines performance — watts per unit volume — appears nowhere.
