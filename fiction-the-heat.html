Part CLXIX: The Heat

Piece #1286: The Panel

Detroit, 1912. Wilbur Scoville, a chemist at Parke, Davis & Company, needed a method for standardizing the pungency of capsicum preparations used in pharmaceutical products — muscle plasters, analgesic ointments. He published "Note on Capsicums" in the Journal of the American Pharmacists Association. The method: extract the capsaicin from a pepper sample in alcohol, dilute the extract with sugar water, present the dilution to a panel of three to five trained tasters. Increase the dilution until the majority of the panel can no longer detect heat. The dilution ratio at which the heat disappears is the Scoville Heat Unit. Thomas examined the panel. Three to five people. Their tongues. Their perception. Their threshold. The number that described the pepper was a property of the people who tasted it.

Piece #1287: The Tongue

The human perception of capsaicin heat is mediated by TRPV1 receptors — transient receptor potential vanilloid type 1. The receptor density varies between individuals. More critically: the receptors desensitize. A person who eats hot peppers daily has a measurably higher threshold than a naive taster. Thomas found this disorienting. The same pepper, the same concentration of capsaicin, produces a different Scoville rating depending on who tastes it and how recently they have eaten hot food. The scale was defined by a threshold that moves. A habitual chili eater might rate a pepper at 30,000 SHU; a naive taster, the same pepper, at 50,000. Both numbers are "correct" within the method. Both describe the same pepper. Neither describes the pepper. Both describe the taster.

Piece #1288: The Replacement

By the late twentieth century, high-performance liquid chromatography — HPLC — had replaced the organoleptic test. ASTA Method 21.3. The HPLC separates capsaicin and dihydrocapsaicin from the sample and measures their concentrations in parts per million. The chemical concentration is then converted to Scoville Heat Units using a multiplication factor — approximately 15 for capsaicin, 16 for dihydrocapsaicin. Thomas noted the conversion. The HPLC measures molecules. The Scoville scale was defined by perception. The conversion factor bridges the gap — transforms a chemical concentration into a number that claims to describe what a tongue would experience. The tongue is no longer involved. The number still uses the tongue's units. The measurement changed entirely — from organoleptic panel to chromatographic column — but the scale persisted, carrying the name and the numbers of the perception-based test into a chemistry-based measurement, as though the two were the same thing expressed differently rather than different things expressed in the same units.

Piece #1289: The Range

Bell pepper: 0 SHU. Jalapeño: 2,500 to 8,000. Habanero: 100,000 to 350,000. Carolina Reaper: 1,500,000 to 2,200,000. Pure capsaicin: 16,000,000. Thomas considered the numbers. At the low end, the differences are perceptible — a person can tell a 2,000 SHU pepper from an 8,000 SHU pepper. At the high end, human discrimination collapses. No tongue can distinguish 1,500,000 from 2,200,000. Both produce maximal TRPV1 activation, pain, and capsaicin shock. The numbers at the top of the scale describe chemical concentrations that the human sensory system cannot differentiate. The scale was built on human perception. Its upper reaches exceed human perception. The numbers continue past the point where the thing they were designed to describe — how hot a pepper feels — ceases to be a meaningful quantity. The precision increases as the relevance decreases.

Piece #1290: The Hot Sauce

A bottle of hot sauce. "2,000,000 Scoville Heat Units." Thomas held it. The number had traveled from Wilbur Scoville's taste panel in Detroit, through a century of organoleptic testing, through the replacement of tongues with chromatography, through a conversion factor that translated molecules into the language of sensation, through a range that exceeded the capacity of the sensory system it was calibrated for, to this label. Two million. The number was precise. It was printed in bold. It communicated intensity. But what it measured — capsaicin concentration times a conversion factor — was not what it claimed to describe — how the sauce would feel on a human tongue. The feeling would depend on which human, which tongue, which recent dietary history, which TRPV1 receptor density, which part of the mouth. The number described none of these. The number described a chemical concentration dressed as a sensation, arrived at by a method that no longer involved the sensation, printed on a bottle that would be experienced by a tongue the number never consulted.
