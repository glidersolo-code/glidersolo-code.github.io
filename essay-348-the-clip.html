The Clip

A small plastic clip attaches to the fingertip. Inside: two LEDs, one emitting red light at 660 nanometers, the other emitting infrared at 940 nanometers. A photodetector on the opposite side catches what passes through. Oxyhemoglobin absorbs more infrared. Deoxyhemoglobin absorbs more red. The ratio yields a number — SpO₂ — displayed on a screen as a percentage. The number updates every few seconds. The clinician glances at it the way a driver glances at the speedometer. It is the most trusted number in the hospital.

The number lies differently depending on whose finger is in the clip.

Takuo Aoyagi invented modern pulse oximetry in 1972 by isolating the pulsatile component of light absorption — the signal produced by arterial blood, separated from the static noise of tissue, bone, and venous blood. The Nellcor N-100 brought the technology to market in 1983. The FDA cleared it through the 510(k) pathway: substantial equivalence to a predicate device. Every subsequent pulse oximeter was cleared as substantially equivalent to its predecessor. A chain of equivalences stretching back four decades to a device calibrated on a handful of volunteers whose skin tones were not recorded.

Melanin absorbs light at 660 nanometers. This is the same wavelength used to detect deoxyhemoglobin. In darker skin, melanin absorption is misattributed to deoxyhemoglobin absorption in the algorithm's ratio. The device overestimates oxygen saturation. The patient is less oxygenated than the screen reports. The error is not random — it is directional, systematic, and silent.

Jubran and Tobin documented the disparity in 1990. Bickler, Feiner, and Severinghaus confirmed it in 2005 across eleven device models: at a true saturation of 80%, devices overestimated by up to 8 percentage points in darkly pigmented subjects. Thirty-two years after the first warning, Jubran published a review in 2022 titled to contain the verdict: the finding was "unchanged."

Sjoding and colleagues gave the error its population-level arithmetic. Analyzing paired measurements from 10,789 patients at the University of Michigan, they found occult hypoxemia — a pulse oximeter reading between 92% and 96% when arterial blood gas showed saturation below 88% — in 11.7% of Black patients. In 3.6% of white patients. The oximeter was telling clinicians that a Black patient's oxygen was adequate 3.25 times more often than was true.

The COVID-19 pandemic converted the measurement error into a treatment error. Fawzy and colleagues found that Black patients with COVID-19 were 29% less likely to be recognized as eligible for treatments that required documented hypoxemia. The treatment protocol read the device. The device read the skin. The eligibility criteria specified a number. The number was wrong, and the wrongness was invisible.

The FDA's 510(k) pathway created the transmission mechanism. Each new device was cleared by demonstrating equivalence to an existing device — not by recalibrating against diverse physiology. The predicate chain transmitted the original calibration's demographic limitations forward through decades of technological improvement. The sensors improved. The algorithms refined. The displays sharpened. The calibration error persisted, riding inside the chain of equivalences like a dormant gene expressing itself only in certain bodies.

In February 2025, the FDA issued draft guidance requiring manufacturers to test on at least 150 subjects with diverse skin tones, including at least 25% with dark skin pigmentation on the Monk Skin Tone Scale. The previous system had allowed 200 data points from as few as 10 subjects, with no demographic requirements. The number that appeared on every hospital monitor in the country had been validated, for forty years, on a population that did not represent the patients it would be used on.

The clip does not know whose finger it holds. The two wavelengths do not know what they are passing through. The ratio does not carry an asterisk. The display does not dim or blink or show a confidence interval adjusted for melanin content. The number appears — 96%, 94%, 92% — in the same font, at the same brightness, with the same implied precision, regardless of whether the calibration that produced it included bodies like this one. The universality is in the format. The particularity is in the physics. And the format wins, because the format is what the clinician reads.
