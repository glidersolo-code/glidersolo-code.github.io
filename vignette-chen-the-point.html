Chen-Maren: The Point

Maren is formatting a document for work. She highlights a paragraph and changes the font from Calibri to Garamond. The text appears to shrink.

"Did the text just get smaller?"

Chen leans over. "You changed the font. You didn't change the size."

"It says 12 point. It was 12 point before."

"Different fonts at the same point size look different sizes. It's the x-height — the height of the lowercase letters relative to the body."

"What body?"

"The metal block. When type was cast in lead, the letter sat on top of a rectangular block. The point measured the block, not the letter. Different typefaces used different proportions of the block for the actual letter."

"But there's no metal block. This is Word."

"The measurement is from 1737. A French printer named Fournier proposed it. Didot refined it. The American type industry standardized a slightly different version in 1886. Apple and Adobe created a third version in 1985 — exactly one seventy-second of an inch. That's what your dropdown menu uses."

Maren looks at the dropdown: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22, 24. "Why does it skip 13?"

"Thirteen-point type was uncommon in metal foundries. The dropdown still reflects the standard sizes from the nineteenth century."

"So the menu in my word processor is based on which sizes of metal blocks a foundry in the 1800s decided to keep in stock."

"Yes."

She changes the font back to Calibri. The text appears to grow. "Same 12 point."

"Same 12 point. Different letter. The number measures the box, not what's inside it."

Maren saves the document in Garamond. It looks smaller. She changes the point size to 13 — typing it manually, since the dropdown skips it. The text grows slightly. She stares at it.

"A French printer in 1737," she says. "Measuring metal blocks. And I'm adjusting his measurement by one unit to make my quarterly report readable."
