The Spray

The box says "3,000 PSI." The cleaning power is PSI times GPM.

Pressure alone is half the equation. A 3,000 PSI machine at 2.0 gallons per minute produces 6,000 cleaning units. A 2,000 PSI machine at 3.0 GPM produces the same 6,000 cleaning units. The consumer who chooses the 3,000 PSI machine for its larger headline number has selected an equivalent machine. PSI determines how deep the water penetrates a surface. GPM determines how fast the water flushes loosened debris away. For a driveway — a large, flat surface with moderate embedded grime — flow rate may matter more than pressure, because the task is flushing rather than cutting. The box prints PSI in large type. GPM, if present, appears in the specifications panel on the side or back.

The nozzle tip redistributes the headline number. Five tips ship with most consumer machines: 0-degree pencil stream, 15-degree, 25-degree, 40-degree fan, and low-pressure soap. The 40-degree tip — recommended for general surface cleaning — spreads the stream across a wide angle, reducing the pressure per unit area at the surface proportionally. The consumer operating with the recommended tip is cleaning at a fraction of the pump's rated PSI at any given point on the surface. The "3,000 PSI" describes the pump's output. The nozzle decides how that output is distributed. The box printed the pump. The consumer uses the nozzle.

Distance adds the third reduction. The manual recommends 6 to 8 inches for embedded stains. The consumer, cautious about damage, holds the wand at 18 inches. Pressure drops with distance as the stream disperses. At double the recommended distance, effective impact pressure falls to approximately one-quarter. The driveway still cleans — multiple passes at reduced pressure accumulate the same result — but the cleaning is not happening at 3,000 PSI. The 3,000 was measured at the pump, not at the surface, not through the nozzle, not at the consumer's distance.

"Max PSI" versus "rated PSI" is the fourth variable. Some manufacturers advertise peak pressure at zero flow or at the unloader's highest setting — a number 10 to 20 percent above sustained operating pressure. A "3,100 max PSI" machine and a "2,800 rated PSI" machine may contain the same pump. No regulation standardizes which number appears on the box. The consumer comparing shelf cards is comparing different labeling conventions, not different machines.

A number that describes the pump, not the nozzle. That measures output, not surface impact. That may be max rather than rated. That omits the flow rate that determines half the cleaning equation. The consumer, comparing PSI in the aisle, compares half of one variable out of four.
