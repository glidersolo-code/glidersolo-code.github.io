The Computus

Chen is reading about the Metonic cycle when Maren calls to ask about Easter.

"April fifth this year," Maren says. "Orthodox is April twentieth. Same rule, different date. How does that work?"

Chen explains: the Council of Nicaea established one rule — first Sunday after the first full moon on or after the vernal equinox. Both churches follow it. The Western church uses the Gregorian calendar; the Eastern church uses the Julian. The Julian has drifted thirteen days from the Gregorian. So the Julian equinox — still called March 21 — falls on what the Gregorian calendar calls April 3. Different starting point, different computed moon, different Easter.

"But the actual equinox is the same day for everyone," Maren says.

"The actual equinox is irrelevant," Chen says. "Neither church uses it. The equinox is March 21 by definition. The full moon is computed from tables, not observed. The ecclesiastical full moon can differ from the astronomical full moon by up to three days."

Silence on the line.

"The rule says 'the first full moon,'" Maren says slowly. "But the full moon in the rule isn't the full moon in the sky."

"The full moon in the rule is the 14th day of a tabular lunar month computed from a 19-year cycle established by an Athenian astronomer in 432 BCE."

"So when was the last time anyone looked at the sky to determine Easter?"

Chen thinks about this. The answer, roughly, is never — or at least not since the tables became canonical in the 6th century. The tables were designed as approximations. The approximations became the computation. The computation became the date. Fifteen hundred years of Easter, determined by arithmetic, not astronomy.

"The sky is optional," Chen says.

"But the language isn't. The language still says 'full moon.' The language still says 'equinox.' Everyone who hears the rule imagines a church looking up."

Maren would say this, Chen thinks. Maren always finds the word that outlived the phenomenon. The rule performs astronomy. The table performs the rule. The moon the table describes is not in the sky. It is in the table. The table is seventeen centuries old. The moon is still called a moon.
