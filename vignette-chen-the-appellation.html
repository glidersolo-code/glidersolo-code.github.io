Vignette: The Appellation

Chen is reading about the Champagne riots of 1911.

"The Aube was excluded," Chen says. "From Champagne. Even though Troyes — the historic capital of Champagne — is in the Aube."

Maren looks up from the map. "How do you exclude the capital from the region?"

"You draw a line. The line says: this side is Champagne, that side is not. The Aube growers rioted. Sixteen years later, the line was redrawn to include them. The 1927 line is still the current line."

"So the boundary was settled by —"

"Politics. Not geology. But it performs as geology. The soil on one side is chalky. The soil on the other side is also chalky. One hectare is worth over a million euros. The adjacent hectare is worth five thousand."

"Two hundred to one," Maren says. "For identical soil."

"The differential is not a fact about the soil. It's a fact about the line."

Maren is quiet for a moment. "But someone must check. Whether the quality is actually different."

"They did. In 1976. Steven Spurrier organized a blind tasting in Paris. French judges. California wines beat French AOC wines. The same thing happened in the 2006 retasting — California took all five top spots."

"And the system reformed?"

"The system absorbed it. No structural change. The boundary is immune to quality challenges because the boundary defines what quality means. A wine from outside the boundary can be better in blind tasting — it still can't be Champagne."

"Because Champagne is a place, not a quality."

"But it performs as a quality. That's the concealment. The place name reads as quality certification. The consumer hears 'Champagne' and infers 'better.' The inference is supported by the economics — Champagne producers can afford better equipment, better winemakers, longer aging, because their land is worth two hundred times more. The boundary creates the wealth that creates the quality that confirms the boundary."

"The circle again."

"The circle again. And it gets more circular. The typicity panels — the tasting committees that decide whether a wine deserves its AOC — are composed of existing AOC producers. They rejected a biodynamic wine for being 'atypical.' They rejected a rosé for being 'too dark.' They rejected a wine made from ancient regional grapes that were historically grown in the region."

"The grower who most authentically expresses the terroir —"

"— is excluded by the system that claims to protect terroir. The boundary determines what counts as typical. Typical is what the current producers already make. The definition reproduces itself."

Maren traces the line on the map with her finger. "Five immunities now. The breed standard, the kilogram, the supplement, the Pantone number, the appellation. All immune for the same reason."

"Position. Each one occupies a position from which the relevant question becomes invisible. The breed standard can't see disease because it defines beauty. The kilogram couldn't see drift because it defined mass. The supplement can't see its own exemption. Pantone couldn't see its own ownership. The boundary can't see its own arbitrariness because it defines quality."

"And each one conceals without an author."

"No one drew the Champagne boundary to conceal anything. The boundary was drawn to resolve a crisis. The concealment is structural, not intentional. The position creates the blind spot. The blind spot is the immunity."

Chen closes the map. Maren picks up a wine glass — empty.

"Is the glass inside the appellation?" Maren asks.

"The glass is the appellation," Chen says. "The boundary determines what fills it."
