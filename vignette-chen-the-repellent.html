The Repellent

Chen was reading the bottles on the shelf. "Thirty percent DEET. Forty percent. Ninety-eight."

"The ninety-eight is for deep woods," Maren said.

"The ninety-eight is for longer duration, not deeper woods." Chen set the 98% back. "The percentage determines how long the repellent lasts, not how strongly it repels. A ten-percent product and a ninety-eight-percent product repel the same mosquito with the same efficacy at minute one. The ten percent expires in two hours. The thirty percent lasts five to eight."

"And the ninety-eight?"

"About the same as the fifty. The curve plateaus. Above fifty percent, additional concentration adds negligible duration."

Maren picked up the 30%. "So this one and the 98% provide the same protection height."

"Same height. Different length. And the length difference between 50 and 98 is functionally zero." Chen took the 30%. "The percentage is a wall duration, not a wall height. The consumer reads wall height because that's what percentages perform — a linear scale from low to high, where higher is more."

"The paragraph," Maren said.

"At zero point eight."

"The paragraph is thirty percent DEET. Concentrated enough to repel the counterargument for five to eight readings. The reader who encounters a ninety-eight-percent paragraph — maximally concentrated, dense with evidence — doesn't get more protection per reading. The same barrier height. The duration might extend to the tenth reading. But above fifty percent concentration, additional evidence adds negligible persistence."

"The plateau."

"The reader's attention has a logarithmic response to argument density. Doubling the evidence adds a diminishing increment of persuasion time. Past the plateau, additional concentration just puts more chemical on the page without extending the protection."

Chen dropped the 30% bottle in the basket. "Thirty percent. Five hours. The trail is four. The number matches the need. The ninety-eight matches the shelf."
