The Grade

Rudolf Flesch, a writing consultant with a doctorate from Columbia, published The Art of Readable Writing in 1948. His readability formula took two inputs: average sentence length (words per sentence) and average word length (syllables per word). Two coefficients, a constant, and the formula produced a number — the Flesch Reading Ease Score. In 1975, J. Peter Kincaid and colleagues at the Naval Training Analysis Center, under contract from the US Navy, reworked Flesch's formula to output a grade level rather than an abstract score. The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level: 0.39 times average sentence length plus 11.8 times average syllables per word minus 15.59. The output is formatted as a school grade — "8.2" means eighth grade, second month.

The grade level implies a reader. It names a student in a specific year of school. The formula has no information about any reader. It counts syllables and sentence boundaries in the text. The output is formatted as a statement about the audience — who can read this — but it is a computation about the text's surface features, and the computation knows nothing about what makes a text difficult to understand. It cannot detect conceptual density. It cannot detect required background knowledge. It cannot detect logical complexity, irony, ambiguity, structural difficulty, or genre conventions. It counts word length and sentence length. "See Spot run" scores at first grade. A contract clause with only monosyllabic words — "Each side shall have the right to end this deal at will" — also scores low. The contract clause requires legal knowledge. The grade level is the same. The comprehensibility is not.

Hemingway scores low. The Old Man and the Sea — short sentences, common words, few syllables — scores at approximately fourth grade on the Flesch-Kincaid scale. The novel is a meditation on endurance, mortality, and the relationship between a man and the natural world. It requires emotional maturity, life experience, and the ability to read past the surface of simple sentences into the psychological architecture beneath them. A fourth grader can decode every word. The formula measures decoding. Readability requires more than decoding.

The Plain Writing Act of 2010 required US federal agencies to write in "plain language." Compliance is measured, in practice, by readability scores. The Affordable Care Act required health insurance documents to meet readability thresholds. Tax forms, medical consent forms, prescription labels — all subjected to formulas as regulatory compliance tools. The formula, designed to describe text difficulty, became a legal compliance metric. The obligation to communicate clearly was reduced to a number. The number could be achieved by shortening sentences and substituting shorter words without changing the conceptual content, producing documents that pass the readability threshold while remaining incomprehensible to the audience whose comprehension the threshold was meant to ensure.

Multiple readability formulas exist — Flesch Reading Ease, Dale-Chall, Gunning Fog, Coleman-Liau, SMOG, Automated Readability Index — all built from the same two dimensions: sentence length and word complexity. All produce a single number. All claim to describe how readable a text is. None can detect the difference between a tax form and a children's story if both use short sentences and common words. Readability is a multi-dimensional property of the interaction between a text and a reader — background knowledge, conceptual density, structural complexity, cultural familiarity, genre conventions, motivation. The formulas measure two surface features of the text alone and report the result as a statement about the reader, formatted as a grade level that names a specific school year as though the formula had tested a student instead of counted syllables.
