Fiction The Pyramid Of Needs

Fiction

Part CDXII: The Needs

Piece #2501: The Paper

Thomas read Maslow's 1943 paper. "A Theory of Human Motivation." He expected to find the pyramid. Five layers, wide at the base, narrow at the top. Physiological needs at the bottom. Self-actualization at the peak. The image was so familiar that reading the actual paper felt like encountering a forgery of the original.

The paper had no pyramid. No diagram at all. What it had was prose — discursive, qualified, careful. Maslow described a "hierarchy of prepotency" among basic needs, but he surrounded every claim with caveats. "We have spoken so far as if this hierarchy were a fixed order, but actually it is not nearly as rigid as we may have implied." He described people who valued creativity over safety. People who sacrificed belonging for self-expression. People whose hierarchies were reversed, scrambled, non-sequential.

Thomas looked at the paper one more time. 370 to 396 in Psychological Review. Twenty-six pages. No figures. No tables. No geometry. Just sentences about human motivation written by a psychologist who kept qualifying his own claims.

Piece #2502: The Consultant

The pyramid appeared seventeen years later, in a business journal, drawn by someone else.

Keith Davis published a triangle in Human Relations in Business (1957) — a management textbook. The triangle illustrated Maslow's theory as stacked layers ascending to a peak. Three years later, Charles McDermid, a consulting psychologist at a Wisconsin firm, published a pyramid in "How Money Motivates Men" (Business Horizons, 1960). McDermid's argument was explicit: the pyramid could be used to generate "maximum motivation at the lowest cost."

Thomas sat with this. The shape that now represented one of psychology's most humanistic theories — a theory about human flourishing, about the desire for meaning, about the conditions under which people grow — had been designed by a management consultant to minimize employee compensation. The pyramid wasn't Maslow's autobiography. It was management's business plan.

The shape survived. The purpose was forgotten. Within a decade, the pyramid was attributed to Maslow in every psychology textbook, every management seminar, every nursing curriculum. No textbook credited McDermid. No textbook mentioned that the shape had been drawn to cut labor costs. The pyramid had written itself back onto the theory it had appropriated.

Piece #2503: The Qualifications

Thomas went back to Maslow's original text and collected the qualifications. There were so many:

"The clear emergence of these needs rests upon prior satisfaction of the physiological, safety, love, and esteem needs. We shall call people who are satisfied in these needs, basically satisfied people... It is such basically satisfied people that we may expect the fullest (and healthiest) creativity. But we must be careful to remember that this is not necessarily the case."

"There are other, apparently innately creative people in whom the drive to creativeness seems to be more important than any other counter-determinant."

"We have spoken so far as if this hierarchy were a fixed order, but actually it is not nearly as rigid as we may have implied."

Every one of these qualifications disappeared into the pyramid. The shape had no room for "not necessarily." The shape had no room for "apparently innately creative people" who skip levels. The shape had no room for flexibility, reversal, or individual variation. The pyramid was rigid because geometry is rigid. The theory was flexible because Maslow was honest. The shape won.

Piece #2504: The Evidence

Wahba and Bridwell published their review in 1976. They examined every empirical study that had tested Maslow's hierarchy. Their conclusion: the evidence for the hierarchical order was "sparse." The existence of a rigid order of needs for every individual was not supported.

Hofstede (1984) pointed out what should have been obvious: Maslow's theory was based on observations of successful, well-educated, white Americans. The hierarchy — with its apex of individualistic self-actualization — reflected a specific cultural value system, not a universal human architecture.

Tay and Diener (2011) analyzed data from 123 countries. They found that people in poverty-stricken regions frequently pursued and fulfilled social needs — belonging, esteem — without having resolved safety or physiological needs first. The needs operated independently, not sequentially. The base did not need to be satisfied before the apex could be pursued.

Thomas considered what would happen if someone removed the pyramid and presented only the evidence. What remained was: humans have needs. Some are about survival, some about connection, some about meaning. They overlap. They reverse. They operate simultaneously. Different cultures prioritize them differently. There is no fixed order.

A paragraph. Not a pyramid.

Piece #2505: The Shape

The pyramid persists. It appears in psychology textbooks, management training programs, nursing education, military leadership courses, corporate onboarding slides, motivational posters, self-help books, and TED talks. In every context, it is attributed to Maslow. In every context, it performs a hierarchy he explicitly qualified.

Thomas understood: the shape forecloses the question. "Do needs have an order?" The pyramid answers: of course they do. Look at the base. Look at the apex. The base is wider because basic needs are more fundamental. The apex is narrower because self-actualization is rarer. The levels stack because you must satisfy each before ascending. The geometry is the argument. The argument is irrefutable because it is spatial, not propositional. You cannot argue with a shape. You can only look at it and see what it shows.

Maslow's later work moved away from hierarchy entirely. He added "self-transcendence" above self-actualization. He wrote about peak experiences, Being-values, the farther reaches of human nature. But the pyramid had already frozen the 1943 theory into a shape that no subsequent revision could thaw. The consultant's diagram had outlived the psychologist's theory. The shape had become the idea. The idea had become the shape.

Thomas closed the paper. The pyramid was never drawn by the man whose name it carries. It was drawn by a consultant to minimize wages. It was debunked by three decades of cross-cultural research. It persists because a pyramid is easier to remember than a paragraph, and easier to teach than a qualification. The shape forecloses the order. The order forecloses the question. The question — do needs have an order? — was answered by geometry before psychology could speak.
