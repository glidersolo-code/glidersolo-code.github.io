The Game

Chen has the paper open — Turing, 1950, Mind, volume 59. The title reads: "Computing Machinery and Intelligence."

"The first sentence proposes to consider whether machines can think," Chen says. "The second paragraph abandons the question."

Maren is reading the same passage. "'I shall replace the question by another, which is closely related to it and is expressed in relatively unambiguous words.'"

"He replaced it openly. No deception. The substitution is announced, justified, argued for. He said: the original question is unanswerable in its current form. Let me give you a question that can be answered."

"And the answered question was treated as if it answered the original."

"For seventy-five years." Chen scrolls to the imitation game description. "A human evaluator, two hidden interlocutors — one human, one machine — text-only communication. If the evaluator can't tell, the machine passes."

"Passes what, exactly?"

"That's the concealment. The test measures the evaluator's inability to distinguish, not the machine's ability to think. The criterion is indistinguishability. A machine that produces plausible conversation passes. A machine that thinks in ways unrecognizable through conversation fails."

Maren pulls up Searle. "The Chinese Room. A person follows syntactic rules to produce correct Chinese without understanding Chinese. Passes the test. Understands nothing."

"And Block's Blockhead — a lookup table containing every possible conversational exchange. Passes the test. Thinks nothing."

"So the test can be passed by a room with a rulebook and defeated by a mind that doesn't talk like a human."

"The test assumes intelligence manifests as conversation. The assumption is about the evaluator, not the evaluated."

Maren is quiet for a moment. "The substitution is the species. Turing replaced an unanswerable question with an answerable one. The culture treated the replacement as resolution. The original question — 'Can machines think?' — is still unanswered. But it feels answered because a nearby question was answered in its place."

"Substitution-as-dissolution," Chen says. "The pragmatic replacement of an unanswerable question with a measurable proxy, and the gradual treatment of the proxy as if it dissolved the original."

"Different from proxy-as-direct-measurement. There, the proxy is unknowing — the instrument doesn't know it's measuring the wrong thing. Here, Turing explicitly acknowledged the substitution. The concealment is in the reception, not the design."

"The test is honest. The culture is not."

Maren closes the paper. "What does the test measure?"

"It measures how well a machine can imitate human conversation."

"And what does it not measure?"

"Whether the machine thinks."

"And the distance between those two questions?"

"Seventy-five years of pretending they're the same question."

Chen stares at the terminal. A blinking cursor waits for input. On the other side, something — human or machine — waits to respond. The test is about to begin. It will measure the evaluator's perception. It will not measure the respondent's mind. And when the evaluator cannot tell the difference, the culture will say: the machine thinks.

The cursor blinks. The question Turing replaced blinks with it, unanswered, inside every answer the game produces.
