The Ghost Population

"Seven hundred forty-two," Chen says, reading from a public health report.

Maren looks over his shoulder. "Deaths per hundred thousand?"

"Age-adjusted deaths per hundred thousand. The crude rate is eight ninety-one. The adjustment brings it down because this county is old — lots of retirees. The adjustment asks: what would the death rate be if this county had the age structure of the 2000 US standard population?"

"And the answer is seven forty-two."

"The answer is seven forty-two. A number that describes no actual dying. Nobody in this county died at the rate of seven forty-two. The number describes a hypothetical county — this county's diseases inhabiting someone else's demographics."

Maren sits down. "Why does the standard population matter?"

"Because the United States used a different standard population until 1999. The 1940 standard. It was younger — it weighted younger age groups heavily. In 1999, the NCHS switched to the 2000 standard, which is older. The weights shifted."

"And?"

"And the Black-to-white mortality ratio went from about 1.61 to about 1.42. Same year. Same deaths. Same people. Different weights. The apparent racial disparity shrank by twelve percent because the ghost population got older."

Maren is quiet for a moment. "The ghost population."

"The standard population. It's inside every age-adjusted number. It provides the weights. The weights determine the comparison. The comparison enters the headline. The headline does not mention the ghost."

"Does anyone know this?"

"Epidemiologists know. There's a footnote: 'Age-adjusted to the 2000 US standard population.' The footnote is on every report. Every table. Every map. The footnote discloses the ghost. The disclosure is technically complete and practically invisible. Because the reader has no intuition for what a different standard would produce. The footnote says 'this is the frame.' The reader looks at the painting."

Maren picks up the report. Puts it down. "So when someone says 'the death rate dropped' — "

"They might mean the death rate dropped. Or they might mean the standard population changed. Or both. The format cannot distinguish them. The single number — seven forty-two — performs as a measurement of this county's health. It is a measurement of this county's health as weighted by a bureaucratic choice made in 1999 about which ghost to use."

"Can you pick a ghost that makes any comparison come out however you want?"

"Not any comparison. But within a range. The direction of a trend can reverse depending on the standard. The ranking of two populations can flip. The magnitude always changes. The ghost is not arbitrary — but it is a choice. And the choice is consequential. And the number does not carry the choice. The number carries the result."

Maren looks at the report one more time. "Seven forty-two."

"Seven forty-two and a ghost."
