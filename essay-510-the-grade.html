The Grade

The letter in the window is an A. The customer walks in. The letter performed its function before the customer reached the door. The decision was made at the window, not at the kitchen.

An A in New York means 0 to 13 violation points. An A in Los Angeles means 90 to 100 on a descending scale. The letters are the same. The numbers behind them are not commensurable. In Los Angeles, two major critical violations — enough to trigger a closure order — can still leave a restaurant at 92 points. The 92 becomes an A. The A goes in the window. The critical violations go in the file.

The file is public. Fewer than ten percent of consumers consult it. The letter replaces the file the way a headline replaces an article — not by restricting access but by satisfying the need the access was meant to serve.

In Tennessee, inspectors applying identical checklists to the same categories of violation produced mean scores ranging from 69 to 92. A 23-point spread. The difference between an A and a C is smaller than the difference between inspectors. The letter is presented as a property of the restaurant. The variance belongs to the person holding the clipboard.

Restaurants that later experienced confirmed foodborne outbreaks scored 81.2 on average. Restaurants without outbreaks scored comparably. The twelve most commonly cited violations were non-critical: dirty ceilings, missing signage, improperly stored cleaning supplies. The violations that cause illness — holding temperatures, cross-contamination, inadequate handwashing — are harder to see and easier to miss. The checklist weighs what is surveyable. The letter reports the survey. The survey measures the visible, not the dangerous.

The format attributes to the restaurant what belongs to the encounter between the restaurant and the inspector on that particular Tuesday. The encounter varies. The attribution does not. The A stays in the window until the next inspection, which might be in six months or might be in eighteen. The letter performs stability. The conditions it summarized were a snapshot.

Ninety-one percent of New York residents approve of the grading system. Eighty-eight percent say the letter influences where they eat. The approval is not for the inspection. The approval is for the letter. The letter is the product. The inspection is the process. The product has replaced the process in public regard the way a diploma replaces the education — not by lying about the education but by being more portable, more displayable, more legible at a glance.

The concealment is not that the report is hidden. The report is online, searchable, free. The concealment is that the letter is sufficient. The format performs attribution: this restaurant is an A. The encounter that produced the A — the inspector's thresholds, the day's conditions, the weighting of visible over critical — is behind the letter. The letter is in the window. The encounter is in the file. The window faces the street. The file faces no one.
