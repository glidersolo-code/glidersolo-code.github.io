Essay 482: The Ballot

The ballot is supposed to be a window. The voter decides; the ballot records; the record is counted; the count determines the winner. The ballot introduces nothing. The format is invisible. The design is neutral.

No ballot has ever been neutral.

In Palm Beach County, Florida, November 7, 2000, a ballot design intended to improve readability for elderly voters produced more than 2,000 misdirected votes, more than 19,000 invalidated ballots, and a presidency. Theresa LePore enlarged the font. She spread ten candidates across two pages. The punch holes ran down the center. The second hole appeared to belong to the second candidate on the left page — Al Gore — but belonged to the first candidate on the right page — Pat Buchanan. Buchanan received 3,407 votes from a county with 337 registered Reform Party members. George W. Bush won Florida by 537.

Buchanan went on television and said, "Almost certainly those are Al Gore's votes and not mine."

The butterfly ballot was extreme. The ordinary case is quieter. The candidate listed first on any ballot gains a measurable advantage — 2 to 3 percentage points in typical races, nearly 10 percentage points in low-information contests. The advantage has a name: the primacy effect. It has a cognitive mechanism: satisficing. When voters lack information about candidates, they accept the first adequate option rather than evaluating all alternatives. The first name on the ballot enters consideration first. The ballot must list names in some order. Some candidate must be first. The advantage cannot be eliminated. California's randomized alphabet — 26 letters drawn in random order, candidate names rotated across 80 Assembly districts — distributes the advantage. It does not remove it. There is no design that removes it. There is only design.

The rolloff effect operates on a different axis. Voters skip races further down the ballot — not because they choose to abstain but because the ballot's physical structure determines which contests receive attention. In Florida's 13th Congressional District in 2006, 14.9 percent of electronic ballots recorded no vote in the congressional race because it appeared on the same screen as the governor's race. In other counties where the same race appeared on its own screen, undervotes were 2 to 5 percent. The design suppressed approximately 13,000 votes. The margin of victory was 369.

The ballot is not a window. The ballot is a machine. The machine has preferences the voter cannot see. The preferences are not intentional — LePore did not design the butterfly ballot to elect Bush, the touchscreen did not intend to suppress congressional votes. The preferences are structural. Order influences choice. Layout influences attention. Alignment influences accuracy. These are not failures of design. They are properties of design. Every design has them. No design lacks them.

What would a transparent ballot look like? Not a ballot without order — order is unavoidable. Not a ballot without layout — layout is the condition of physical existence. A transparent ballot would be one that acknowledges its own influence. That treats its design as a variable to be controlled, not an invisibility to be assumed. California's rotation system. Randomized ballot order in every state. Testing for comprehension before deployment. Usability review. The ballot as an instrument that requires calibration, not a window that requires nothing.

The window is a lens. The lens bends the light. The bending shaped a presidency.
