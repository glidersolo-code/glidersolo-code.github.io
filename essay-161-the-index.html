The Index

Essay 161

This critique is well-known. Nearly everyone who has encountered the Body Mass Index has heard that it fails to distinguish muscle from fat, that it was never meant for individual diagnosis, that its thresholds were calibrated on white European populations. The critique is almost cliché. What is less commonly named is the mechanism — and the mechanism connects this case to a pattern that extends far beyond anthropometry.

Adolphe Quetelet was a Belgian astronomer and statistician. In 1832, working on his concept of l'homme moyen — the average man — he observed that body weight in large populations scales approximately with the square of height. The relationship was empirical. Quetelet chose the squared exponent because it produced a roughly constant ratio in his datasets, not because any physiological theory predicted it. The relationship described a population, not a person. L'homme moyen was a statistical construct: the center of a distribution, a coordinate that no individual was expected to occupy.

In 1972, Ancel Keys, with Fidanza, Karvonen, Kimura, and Taylor, published "Indices of relative weight and obesity" in the Journal of Chronic Diseases. They tested several weight-for-height indices against measured body fat percentage in 7,424 men across five countries and found that weight/height² — the Quetelet Index — correlated best with body fat at the population level. Keys renamed it Body Mass Index. He explicitly warned it was "at best a rough measure" and suitable for epidemiological studies, not individual assessment. The squared exponent was Quetelet's 1832 approximation, inherited without physiological justification.

In 1995 and 1997, the World Health Organization standardized BMI thresholds for international use: below 18.5 underweight, 18.5-24.9 normal weight, 25-29.9 overweight, 30 and above obese. The thresholds were derived primarily from mortality and morbidity data in white European and North American populations. In 2004, a WHO expert consultation, publishing in The Lancet, acknowledged that the relationship between BMI and health risk varies significantly across populations. Asian populations face higher cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI — type 2 diabetes risk increases substantially at BMI 23 rather than 25, and cardiovascular risk at 27 rather than 30. The consultation recommended that countries define their own "action points" but declined to change the international thresholds, citing the need for global comparability. The threshold stayed. The data that would have changed it was acknowledged and set aside.

Frank Q. Nuttall at the Minneapolis Veterans Affairs Medical Center and the University of Minnesota, publishing "Body Mass Index: Obesity, BMI, and Health: A Critical Review" in Nutrition Today in 2015, summarized the index's well-documented limitations. BMI cannot distinguish fat from lean mass. It does not account for fat distribution (visceral vs. subcutaneous). It systematically misclassifies muscular individuals as overweight and can classify individuals with excess body fat as normal weight if their muscle mass is low. The relationship between BMI and mortality is not monotonic — a substantial body of evidence shows lowest mortality in the "overweight" category (BMI 25-30) rather than the "normal" category, a finding that has generated decades of debate about whether the thresholds themselves are miscalibrated or whether confounders explain the pattern.

The concealment mechanism is not that BMI is inaccurate — that critique is established. The mechanism is the specific pathway by which a population statistic became an individual diagnostic tool, and the specific reasons it cannot be corrected.

First, population-as-individual: Quetelet designed the index to describe distributions. Keys validated it as a population-level correlate. WHO adopted it as an individual classification system. At each step, a tool designed for aggregate inference was applied to individual diagnosis. The population mean became the individual benchmark. The standard deviation became the pathology threshold. The coordinate that described no individual person was used to classify every individual person.

Second, era-locked calibration: the squared exponent is from 1832. The name is from 1972. The thresholds are from 1995-1997. Each layer was locked in before the next was added. The thresholds reference the name, the name references the exponent, the exponent references an empirical observation from a nineteenth-century Belgian astronomer's datasets. To change the thresholds, one would need to change the thresholds — but also the clinical guidelines, insurance criteria, pharmaceutical inclusion standards, public health targets, and institutional protocols that reference them.

Third, infrastructure-absorbs-error: BMI thresholds are embedded in health insurance underwriting, clinical screening protocols, pharmaceutical trial inclusion and exclusion criteria, public health surveillance, school fitness assessments, military enlistment standards, and immigration medical evaluations. Changing the standard requires coordinated action across an infrastructure that no single authority controls. The WHO consultation in 2004 acknowledged the evidence and declined to change the thresholds. The error was identified, documented, and absorbed.

The pattern is now visible across four entries. A-weighting (1933, sound measurement). CIE standard observer (1931, color specification). Jones factors (1931, protein labeling). Quetelet/Keys/WHO thresholds (1832/1972/1995, body composition). Each began as a population-level measurement calibrated on a limited sample. Each was adopted for individual-level application. Each is known to be inaccurate. Each has a published correction that is not used. Each persists because the infrastructure built around the standard cannot be rebuilt without costs that exceed any single actor's capacity or incentive. The mechanism is not ignorance. The mechanism is infrastructure.
