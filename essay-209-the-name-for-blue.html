The Name for Blue

In Russian, light blue and dark blue are different colors. Not different shades — different colors, as different as red and orange are in English. Голубой (goluboy) is the light blue of the sky on a clear day. Синий (siniy) is the dark blue of the deep sea. A Russian speaker asked to sort blue chips into two piles does it faster than an English speaker, and the speed difference disappears when the Russian speaker counts aloud while sorting. The verbal interference removes the advantage. The linguistic category is not a label applied after the perception. It is part of the perception.

In 1969, Brent Berlin and Paul Kay proposed that this should not be possible. Basic Color Terms: Their Universality and Evolution surveyed twenty languages and argued that all human languages carve up color space in the same way, in the same order. First a language gets terms for dark and light. Then red. Then yellow or green. Then both. Then blue. Then brown. Then the rest. The sequence was evolutionary, and it was universal. The biology of color vision — three types of cone cells, opponent-processing channels — determines which categories perception can distinguish, and languages discover these categories in a fixed order. English, with eleven basic terms, had reached the final stage. Languages with two terms were at the beginning. The difference was developmental. The hierarchy was implicit. It was also wrong.

Debi Roberson and colleagues studied the Berinmo of Papua New Guinea in 2000. The Berinmo have five basic color terms. Their term "nol" covers what English speakers call dark green and blue. Their term "wor" covers what English speakers call light green, yellow, and parts of orange. The category boundaries are not English boundaries shifted a few nanometers. They are different boundaries — drawn through different parts of color space, grouping together what English separates and separating what English groups. And the Berinmo show categorical perception effects at their boundaries. Colors that cross a Berinmo boundary are easier for Berinmo speakers to tell apart than colors equally far apart in physical wavelength that fall within a single Berinmo category. The perception follows the language. The English speaker at the same task shows categorical perception at English boundaries. Both speakers see the same light. They do not see the same colors.

The Himba of Namibia have separate terms for shades of green that English collapses into one word. Himba speakers discriminate between these greens with ease. English speakers find the discrimination difficult. Meanwhile, the blue-green distinction that English speakers find obvious — the distinction Berlin and Kay treated as a perceptual universal — gives Himba speakers no advantage. The universal boundary is not universal. It is English.

Berlin and Kay's methodology reinforced their conclusions. They used Munsell color chips — a standardized set of samples manufactured in the United States, arranged in a grid of hue and lightness. They asked speakers to name each chip and point to the "best example" of each color name. The Munsell grid presupposed the dimensions it investigated. The chips sampled color space at intervals determined by American perceptual standards. The best-example task assumed that each color term has a focal center — a "best red," a "best blue" — and that these focal centers are universal. The methodology found what it assumed.

The deeper claim is not about color. It is about the relationship between perception and language. Berlin and Kay assumed the direction was one-way: perception determines language. Languages discover the categories that the eye provides. The evidence from Berinmo, Himba, and Russian speakers shows the direction is bidirectional. Perception constrains language — three cone types set outer limits on what can be distinguished. But language also constrains perception — the categories available in a speaker's language affect which distinctions are perceptually salient. The sky is blue in English and the sea is blue in English. In Russian, they are different colors. Neither language is wrong. Each language is an instrument that measures some differences and ignores others. The instrument shapes the measurement. The measurement is experienced as the world.
