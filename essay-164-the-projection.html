The Projection

Essay 164

Gerardus Mercator published his world map in 1569 — eighteen printed sheets totaling 202 by 124 centimeters, titled Nova et Aucta Orbis Terrae Descriptio ad Usum Navigantium Emendate Accommodata: new and more complete representation of the terrestrial globe properly adapted for use in navigation. The title specifies the purpose. The projection was designed so that a rhumb line — a path of constant compass bearing across the globe — plots as a straight line on the map. A navigator could draw a line between two ports, read the angle with a protractor, set the compass, and sail. The mathematics required to achieve this — a conformal cylindrical projection in which the vertical scale at any latitude equals the horizontal scale, and both scale as the secant of the latitude — produced a specific and unavoidable trade: angles are preserved exactly, and area is distorted systematically. The distortion increases toward the poles without bound. On the Mercator projection, Greenland appears roughly the same size as Africa. The actual ratio is approximately 1 to 14. Africa is 30.4 million square kilometers; Greenland is 2.2 million.

This is not a flaw. It is a theorem. No flat map of a sphere preserves all metric properties simultaneously. Carl Friedrich Gauss proved in his Theorema Egregium (1827) that surfaces of different curvature cannot be mapped to each other without distortion of some metric properties. Every flat map of a sphere must sacrifice something. The Mercator projection sacrifices area for angles. The Lambert equal-area projection sacrifices angles for area. The Robinson projection compromises on both, sacrificing mathematical exactness in any single property for an aesthetically balanced appearance. John P. Snyder's Map Projections — A Working Manual (USGS Professional Paper 1395, 1987) catalogs hundreds of projections, each preserving a different property set and distorting a different set. The choice of projection is a choice of which distortions to accept. The choice is always made.

The Mercator projection declined for general cartographic use throughout the twentieth century. By the 1970s, professional cartographic organizations recommended against using it for world maps. Arno Peters's 1973 revival of the Gall-Peters equal-area projection provoked what Mark Monmonier, in Rhumb Lines and Map Wars: A Social History of the Mercator Projection (University of Chicago Press, 2004), calls "the map wars" — a decades-long debate about whether area-preserving or angle-preserving projections better serve public understanding of the world. The American Cartographic Association issued resolutions against the use of rectangular projections for world maps. The Mercator was not banned, but it was professionally disfavored.

Then the web happened. In 2005, Google Maps launched using the Web Mercator projection — later codified as EPSG:3857. The choice was computational, not cartographic. The Mercator projection maps the globe into a square (or near-square, truncating the poles at approximately 85° latitude). Squares tile efficiently: each zoom level subdivides each tile into four equal sub-tiles, and the conformal property ensures that features at tile edges align correctly across zoom levels. The mathematics that made Mercator useful for 16th-century rhumb lines — the angle-preserving property — is the same mathematics that makes it useful for 21st-century web tile rendering. Sarah Battersby, Michael Finn, E. Lynn Usery, and Kristina Yamamoto at the USGS documented the implications in Cartographica (49(2):85-101, 2014): Web Mercator is not technically conformal (it uses spherical rather than ellipsoidal formulas), introduces measurable positional errors at large scales, and propagates the well-known area distortions into every web map viewed by billions of users daily. The distortions are documented. The documentation changes nothing. Google Maps, OpenStreetMap, Bing Maps, Mapbox, and essentially every web mapping service uses Web Mercator because the tiling infrastructure is built on it.

Three concealment mechanisms operate here. Selective-preservation: the Mercator projection preserves one metric property (angles) exactly, at the cost of systematically distorting another (area). The preservation is by design. The distortion is by necessity — Gauss's theorem guarantees it. But the user encounters the map as a map, not as a mathematical transformation with specified trade-offs. The selectivity of what is preserved — angles yes, area no — is invisible to the user who sees only a representation of the world. The user absorbs the area distortion as geography. The mental model of continental sizes that most humans carry was formed by Mercator projections, and the distortion lives in the model. This is distinct from proxy-as-direct-measurement (where one quantity stands in for another) and from binary-reification (where a binary result becomes a taxonomy). Selective-preservation is about a transformation that preserves some properties exactly at the cost of distorting others, used as though it preserves all properties. Era-locked calibration: the 1569 projection, designed for Age of Exploration maritime navigation, persists in digital mapping 457 years later. The purpose changed — from rhumb lines to web tiles — but the projection remained because the mathematical property that serves the new purpose (conformal → tiling efficiency) is the same property that served the old one (conformal → straight rhumb lines). The calibration era (16th-century navigation) is irrelevant to the current application (web mapping). Infrastructure-absorbs-error: every web mapping library (Leaflet, OpenLayers, Mapbox GL, Google Maps JavaScript API), every tile server (OpenStreetMap tile servers, Mapbox, Google Cloud), every cached tile set, and every application that consumes map tiles is built on Web Mercator. Alternative projections for web mapping exist — Adaptive Composite Map Projections, for instance, proposed by Bernhard Jenny at Oregon State (published in IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 2012). They are not adopted because the tile infrastructure cannot be rebuilt without coordinated action across every mapping service, every client library, and every cached tile set on the internet.

Mercator titled his map "for use in navigation." The title was a scope statement. The scope was ignored. The map designed for plotting compass courses was hung on classroom walls, printed in atlases, and rendered on screens as though it were a faithful image of the world. The projection is not hidden. The selectivity of what it preserves is hidden. And the selectivity shapes the mental model: equatorial regions appear smaller than they are, polar regions appear larger, and the distortion enters the understanding of billions of people as geography rather than as the mathematical consequence of a 16th-century navigator's need for straight rhumb lines.
