Part CLXVIII: The Chill

Piece #1281: The Cylinder

Little America, Antarctica, 1939-1941. Paul Siple and Charles Passel, members of the US Antarctic Service Expedition, hung a plastic cylinder filled with water from a pole. They measured how long it took the water to freeze under different combinations of air temperature and wind speed. The faster the freeze, the higher the "wind chill factor" — a heat-loss rate, measured in kilocalories per square meter per hour. Thomas noted: the original wind chill was not a temperature. It was a cooling rate of water in a plastic container. The experiment measured how quickly inanimate water lost heat to moving air. It did not measure how a human body experienced cold. The plastic cylinder had no metabolism, no clothing, no vasoconstriction, no shivering response. The number it produced was a property of water and plastic and wind. It was published as a property of weather.

Piece #1282: The Conversion

The wind chill factor was a number in kilocalories per square meter per hour. The public could not interpret it. So the number was converted — reformatted into an "equivalent temperature." Wind chill equivalent temperature: the air temperature that would produce the same rate of heat loss on a calm day as the actual temperature produces with wind. Thomas found this conversion astonishing. The equivalent temperature does not exist. When the forecast says "wind chill minus forty," the air temperature is not minus forty. The air temperature might be minus fifteen. The number reports a heat-loss rate disguised as a temperature — dressed in degrees, printed alongside the actual temperature, spoken in the same units, experienced as the same kind of measurement. A rate of cooling was translated into the language of temperature so that the public would understand it. The public understood it — as a temperature. The conversion succeeded precisely by concealing what it converted from.

Piece #1283: The Face

In 2001, the National Weather Service and the Meteorological Service of Canada replaced the Siple-Passel formula. The new wind chill temperature index was based on a computational model of heat loss from a human face. Randall Osczevski and Maurice Bluestein. The model assumed: a human face. Walking at 4.8 kilometers per hour. At a height of 1.5 meters — the approximate height of a human face, not the standard 10-meter anemometer height. In shade. No solar radiation. Thomas examined each assumption. The face. Not a hand. Not a torso. Not a clothed body — a bare face, which is typically the most exposed surface in winter. Walking, not standing. In shade, not sun. Every assumption was reasonable for a specific scenario: a person walking outdoors in winter with only the face exposed. But the number produced by this scenario was reported as "the wind chill" — as though it described the weather rather than the model's assumptions about the person in it.

Piece #1284: The Summer

Robert Steadman, 1979. The heat index — the summer equivalent. Steadman modeled the relationship between air temperature, humidity, wind speed, and human thermal comfort. His model person was specific: 5 feet 7 inches tall, 147 pounds, wearing long trousers and a short-sleeved shirt, walking at 3.1 miles per hour, in shade. Thomas wrote the specifications out. The heat index on the weather forecast — "feels like 105°F" — describes the apparent temperature experienced by a person of that specific height, weight, clothing, activity level, and sun exposure. A person sitting in direct sun. A person running. A person wearing different clothing. A larger person. A smaller person. Each experiences a different heat index. The forecast reports one. The one it reports is Steadman's model person, walking in trousers, in shade. The model person is invisible. The number is visible. The number says "feels like." It does not say "feels like to a specific hypothetical person under specific hypothetical conditions."

Piece #1285: The Feels

"Feels like." Thomas considered the phrase. It appeared on every weather app, every forecast, every outdoor sign. "Feels like 98°F." "Feels like minus 25°F." The phrase claimed to describe a sensation. It described a computation. A multi-variable physical process — radiation, convection, evaporation, metabolism, clothing, activity, body geometry — compressed into a single number formatted as a temperature that is not a temperature. The wind chill is not a temperature. The heat index is not a temperature. Both are heat transfer rates from model bodies under model conditions, converted into the units of temperature so that the public can read them beside the actual temperature and believe they understand both. The conversion is the concealment. If wind chill were reported in its native units — watts per square meter, or kilocalories per hour — no one would confuse it with the air temperature. It is reported in degrees because degrees are understood. The understanding is the misunderstanding. Thomas closed the notebook. The forecast said "feels like." Feels like to whom. Under what conditions. In what clothing. At what activity level. The number answered none of these questions. It answered in degrees.
