Part CLXXVII: The Fit

Piece #1326: The Barleycorn

King Edward II of England, in a statute around 1324, defined the inch as the length of three barleycorns laid end to end. The foot was twelve inches — thirty-six barleycorns. English shoe sizes, when they were eventually formalized, adopted the barleycorn as their unit: each full size represents one-third of an inch, approximately 8.47 mm. A child's size 0 corresponds to a foot length of 4 inches (12 barleycorns). Adult sizes continue the progression upward, with a discontinuous break between children's and adults' that varies by country.

The barleycorn is still the increment of the American and British shoe sizing systems. When a consumer in New York tries on a size 10 and a size 10.5, the half-size difference represents one-sixth of an inch — approximately 4.23 mm. The arithmetic of a medieval grain measure determines the resolution of a modern sizing system. The barleycorn is invisible in the number. The consumer sees "10" and experiences a measurement. The measurement is medieval agriculture formalized into a number that appears modern.

Thomas's clinical scales carry their own barleycorns — arbitrary increments chosen by the instrument's designers that determine the resolution of the measurement. The PHQ-9's four-point scale — 0, 1, 2, 3 — was chosen for convenience, not for correspondence to clinical intervals. The gap between "several days" and "more than half the days" and "nearly every day" is treated as uniform. The increments are arbitrary. The arithmetic conceals the arbitrariness.

Piece #1327: The Width

A foot is a three-dimensional object. Length, width, arch height, instep volume, toe box shape, heel width. The shoe size number captures one dimension: length. Width is nominally available — the American system offers designations from AAAA (narrowest) to EEEE (widest), with B as standard for women and D as standard for men. In practice, most shoes are manufactured and sold in a single width. The width designation exists in the system but is absent from the inventory. The number on the box — "10 D" — promises two dimensions. The shoe on the shelf delivers one.

Arch height, instep volume, toe box requirements — none are represented in the number. A flat-footed size 10 and a high-arched size 10 share a number. The number matches them to the same box. The three-dimensional foot is reduced to a one-dimensional measurement — length — with a second dimension — width — that is nominally present and practically absent, and the remaining dimensions unrepresented.

Thomas's condition is three-dimensional: mood, function, social connection, physical health, cognitive capacity, temporal experience. The PHQ-9 captures one dimension — symptom frequency — with a nominal gesture toward functional impairment in its final question. The number promises comprehensiveness. The instrument delivers one dimension with a gesture toward a second.

Piece #1328: The Conversion

A foot of a specific length generates different numbers in different countries. A foot that measures 270 mm is: US men's 9.5, US women's 11, UK 9, EU 43, Japanese 27.0 (cm). Five different numbers for the same foot. The conversion charts are approximate — the mapping between systems is not exact because the zero-points, increments, and scaling conventions differ. The American system uses barleycorns from a children's baseline. The European system uses Paris points — two-thirds of a centimeter, or approximately 6.67 mm, a unit introduced by French shoemakers in the 19th century. The Japanese system uses centimeters directly. Each system made its own choices about where to start counting, how large a step to take, and which dimension of the foot to prioritize. The same physical reality — a foot of a given length — produces five different numbers depending on which convention is applied.

There is no international standard. ISO 9407 (Mondopoint system) was published in 1991, defining shoe sizes by foot length and width in millimeters. It is used in military and some athletic contexts. It has not replaced any national system for consumer footwear. The consumer buying shoes online from a European retailer consults a conversion chart. The chart is approximate. The fit depends on the manufacturer's interpretation of the standard, the last used to construct the shoe, and whether the company has succumbed to vanity sizing — the practice of labeling a shoe with a smaller number than its actual measurements to flatter the customer.

Thomas's condition generates different numbers in different systems. The PHQ-9 and the Hamilton Rating Scale use different zero-points, different increments, different scaling. The same clinical state produces different numbers depending on which instrument is applied. There is no universal conversion. The conversion charts are approximate.

Piece #1329: The Last

The shoe is built on a last — a three-dimensional form around which the upper and sole are constructed. The last determines the shoe's internal volume, arch placement, toe box shape, heel curve, and instep height. Two shoes labeled "size 10" from different manufacturers have been built on different lasts. A Nike size 10 fits differently from an Adidas size 10 fits differently from a New Balance size 10. The numerical agreement — all three say 10 — conceals the volumetric disagreement. The consumer knows this from experience: "I'm a 10 in Nike but a 10.5 in Adidas." The number is not a measurement of the foot. It is a label assigned by the manufacturer to a last, loosely correlated with foot length, specific to the brand, the style, and sometimes the production batch.

Vanity sizing compounds the instability. Fashion brands, observing that customers prefer to buy smaller numbers, have shifted their sizing downward — today's size 8 fits a foot that would have been size 9 in the same brand twenty years ago. The number has drifted from the measurement it claimed to represent. The consumer's size has not changed. The label has.

Thomas's clinical labels drift from the conditions they claim to represent. The DSM's thresholds for major depressive disorder have shifted between editions. The PHQ-9's severity categories — mild, moderate, moderately severe, severe — are labels assigned by the instrument's developers to score ranges, loosely correlated with clinical severity, specific to the instrument, the validation population, and the era. Thomas's severity has not changed. The label's meaning has.

Piece #1330: The Number

The consumer stands in a shoe store. A salesperson measures the foot on a Brannock Device — a metal instrument patented by Charles F. Brannock in 1927 in Syracuse, New York. The device measures length and width using fixed scales. It outputs a number: 10 D. The number is specific to the American system. It describes the foot's length in barleycorn-based increments from a children's baseline. It captures one dimension fully and one partially. It corresponds to no international standard. It will disagree with the number from any other country's system and will vary from manufacturer to manufacturer within the same country.

The consumer takes the number to the shelf. Finds a box marked 10. Tries the shoe on. It may fit or it may not, because the number on the Brannock Device and the number on the box were produced by different processes — one is a measurement of the foot, the other is a label assigned to a last. The agreement of the two numbers is approximate, brand-specific, and subject to vanity sizing. The consumer adds a half-size, subtracts, tries another brand. The number started as a measurement and became a negotiation.

One hundred and seventy-seven Parts. Thomas's clinical numbers start as measurements and become negotiations. The PHQ-9 outputs a score. The clinician takes the score to the treatment algorithm. The algorithm may or may not fit, because the number on the questionnaire and the threshold on the algorithm were produced by different processes — one is a measurement of symptoms, the other is a label assigned to a treatment level. The agreement is approximate, instrument-specific, and subject to threshold drift. The clinician adjusts. The fit is negotiated. The number was supposed to determine the fit. The fit determines whether the number is useful.
