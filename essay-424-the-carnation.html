The Carnation

Anna Jarvis never married. She never had children. She spent twenty-four years fighting the commercialization of a holiday she created to honor her own mother, who had lost seven of twelve children in infancy.

The holiday took six years to build. On May 10, 1908, the first celebration was held simultaneously at Andrews Methodist Church in Grafton, West Virginia — where her mother taught Sunday school — and at Wanamaker's department store auditorium in Philadelphia, where fifteen thousand people overwhelmed a five-thousand-seat venue. Jarvis sent five hundred white carnations to Grafton: her mother's favorite flower. By 1910, West Virginia recognized it. By 1911, every state observed it. On May 9, 1914, Woodrow Wilson signed Proclamation 1268. Nine years from grief to federal holiday.

The format was perfectly legible. A named day. A fixed date. A symbolic flower. A singular possessive — "Mother's Day," not "Mothers' Day." Each child honoring their particular mother. The grammar was precise. The intent was personal: a handwritten note, a carnation, a visit. The format was also perfectly capturable.

By 1920, Jarvis had shifted from building to fighting. She called the commercial interests "charlatans, bandits, pirates, racketeers, kidnappers, and termites." She endorsed boycotts against florists who raised carnation prices every May. In 1925, she crashed an American War Mothers convention in Philadelphia and was arrested for disturbing the peace, dragged screaming from the meeting by police. She fought the Postal Service's three-cent stamp because a vase of carnations beside Whistler's Mother amounted to a floral advertisement. She succeeded in keeping the words "Mother's Day" off the stamp. By 1943, she was petitioning to rescind the holiday entirely. By 1944, she had thirty-three simultaneous lawsuits.

She spent her inheritance on legal fees. She went blind. She entered the Marshall Square Sanitarium in West Chester, Pennsylvania. She spent her last four years there. Her care was paid for — secretly — by people in the floral and greeting card industries. She was never told. She died November 24, 1948, at eighty-four.

The holiday she created generates $35.7 billion in annual spending. One hundred and thirteen million cards are exchanged. The second Sunday in May is the busiest restaurant day of the year. The singular possessive survives on every card. The grammar she specified — each child honoring their particular mother — persists in the lettering while the meaning is buried under $6.8 billion in jewelry, $6.3 billion in dining, and $3.2 billion in flowers.

The commercial industries that appropriated the holiday paid for the care of the woman who fought them. The capture rewrote the purpose. The format Jarvis designed — the named holiday, the fixed date, the symbolic flower, the sentiment — was what the commerce needed. The format performed intimacy. The commerce extracted revenue. The founder became invisible inside her own creation, and the creation outlived her objection by eighty years and counting.

The apostrophe remains. The meaning is gone.
