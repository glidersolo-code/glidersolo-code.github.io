Part CCLIV: The Seed

Piece #1711: The Bag

A 5-pound bag of grass seed. The front says "covers up to 2,000 sq ft." Thomas's bare patch — where the oak tree had been removed — measured approximately 800 square feet. He bought one bag. He had more than enough. The back of the bag listed two rates: "New Lawn: 2,000 sq ft. Overseeding: 4,000 sq ft." The same bag claimed two areas, differing by a factor of two, depending on whether the consumer was establishing new grass or thickening existing turf. Thomas's patch was bare earth. He needed the new-lawn rate. If he had read only the front — "covers up to 4,000 sq ft" — and was establishing a new lawn, he would have spread half the seed needed. The word "up to" performed the larger number. The use case determined which number applied.

Piece #1712: The Size

The bag contained a mix: 40 percent Kentucky bluegrass, 30 percent perennial ryegrass, 30 percent fine fescue. Kentucky bluegrass seeds are tiny — approximately 2.2 million per pound. Perennial ryegrass: approximately 230,000. Fine fescue: approximately 610,000. The "2,000 sq ft" coverage rate was calculated from the recommended seeding rate for this specific blend — a rate that accounts for the varying sizes and establishment characteristics of each species. A bag of pure Kentucky bluegrass at the same weight would cover more area (more seeds per pound). A bag of pure perennial ryegrass would cover less (fewer, larger seeds). The consumer comparing two bags by coverage-per-dollar may be comparing seed sizes, not values. The bag with the larger coverage number may simply contain smaller seeds.

Piece #1713: The Percentage

The tag on the bag lists a germination rate: 85 percent. This is legally required by the Federal Seed Act. The number was tested in a laboratory — seeds placed on moist blotter paper at controlled temperature, counted after the species-specified germination period. The "2,000 sq ft" coverage rate assumes all seeds germinate. At 85 percent germination, effective seed density drops to the equivalent of 1,700 square feet of coverage. At the tag's "sell by" date, germination may have decreased further — seed stored in a hot garden center loses viability faster than seed stored in climate control. Thomas's bag had been displayed in an outdoor rack through July. The germination rate printed on the tag described the seed at the testing date. The seed in Thomas's bag had survived a summer.

Piece #1714: The Soil

Germination is not establishment. A seed that germinates — sends out a root and a shoot — may not survive to produce a mature plant. Establishment depends on: soil temperature (cool-season grasses germinate optimally at 50 to 65 degrees Fahrenheit soil temperature; Thomas planted in mid-August when soil temperatures exceeded 80 degrees), seed-to-soil contact (seeds sitting on loose, unraked soil may desiccate before rooting), watering frequency (new seed needs moisture several times daily for the first two weeks — Thomas watered once in the morning), and competition (weed seeds germinate in the same window, competing for the same light and water). Of the seeds that germinated, perhaps 40 to 60 percent established. Thomas's 800-square-foot patch, seeded at a rate designed for 2,000 square feet, at 85 percent germination reduced to 75 percent by summer storage, at perhaps 50 percent establishment, produced approximately 300 to 350 square feet of grass density. The patch looked thin. He bought a second bag.

Piece #1715: The Lawn

Two hundred and fifty-four Parts. Thomas seeded the patch twice. The second application — in September, when soil temperatures had dropped to 62 degrees — performed better. The seed-to-soil contact improved because the first application's failure had taught him to rake the seed in. The watering improved because his neighbor mentioned the twice-daily requirement. The establishment rate rose. By October, the patch was covered. It took two bags, two applications, one season of learning, and conditions that the coverage rate on the first bag had not described. The bag said 2,000 square feet. The lawn required the consumer to provide the soil, the temperature, the water, the timing, and the raking. The seed, having been scattered, needed everything the bag did not list.
