Part CDLI: The Rating

Piece #2696: The Screen

The analyst pulls up three ESG ratings for the same company. MSCI: AA. Sustainalytics: Medium Risk. CDP: B. Three symbols. Three agencies. Three assessments that look like they belong on the same axis.

Thomas watches from the adjacent desk. He is here for a corporate wellness consultation — blood pressure screening at an investment firm, routine work — but the analyst's screen catches his attention. Three ratings. Three formats. One company.

"Do those agree?" Thomas asks.

The analyst looks up. "They're different providers. Different methodologies."

"But they look the same."

The analyst pauses. He has not been asked this question by someone outside the industry. "They use similar formats. Letters, scores. But they measure different things."

Thomas thinks: three thermometers, three different temperatures, no patient would accept that. One thermometer, one temperature, one body. The body does not change. The thermometers agree because they measure the same property with calibrated instruments. These ratings measure different properties with uncalibrated definitions using the same letters.

Piece #2697: The Correlation

Berg et al. measured the correlation. 0.38 to 0.71. Thomas translates to medical terms: a diagnostic test that correlated with another test at 0.38 would not be considered a valid alternative. It would be considered a different test. The lab would not call them interchangeable.

But the format calls them interchangeable. An A from MSCI and an A from Sustainalytics occupy the same cell in a spreadsheet. The font is the same. The column header says "ESG Rating." The format performs equivalence. The substance performs disagreement.

Fifty-six percent of the disagreement is in measurement: the providers agree on the category (carbon emissions) but measure it differently (per revenue, per employee, scope 1+2 vs. scope 1+2+3). Even when they agree on what to measure, they disagree on how.

Thomas thinks of pulse oximetry: same measurement, different bodies, different accuracy. Here it is reversed: same body, same measurement name, different instruments, different answers. The name is the concealment.

Piece #2698: The Impression

The rater effect. The provider's overall impression of the company influences its category-level ratings. The whole precedes the parts. The sum shapes the addends.

Thomas recognizes this from clinical medicine: the halo effect. A patient who presents well — articulate, well-dressed, calm — receives more favorable clinical assessments across multiple dimensions, including dimensions unrelated to presentation. The overall impression colors the specific judgment.

In ESG, the impression is the rating. The rating is supposed to be the sum of specific environmental, social, and governance scores. In practice, the impression-driven adjustment means the rating performs bottom-up assessment while producing top-down judgment.

A company with a strong public sustainability narrative receives higher scores on specific categories — even categories where the data does not support the higher score. The narrative is the impression. The impression is the rating. The rating performs the measurement. The measurement confirms the narrative.

Piece #2699: The Fund

The investor chooses a "sustainable" fund. The fund selects companies rated highly by a specific ESG provider. A different "sustainable" fund, using a different provider, selects different companies. The overlap is partial. Both funds claim sustainability. Both use ESG ratings. The ratings measure different things.

The investor does not know this. The investor sees: "ESG-rated." The format — three letters, familiar from financial media — performs a shared standard. The standard is not shared. The categories differ. The measurements differ. The conclusions differ. The format is identical.

Thomas finishes the blood pressure screenings. Thirty employees. Thirty readings. Thirty numbers that mean the same thing because the instrument is calibrated, the property is defined, and two sphygmomanometers measuring the same arm at the same moment would agree.

The ESG ratings on the screens behind him do not work this way. The letters are the same. The things they point to are not. The format performs the agreement. The agreement does not exist.

Piece #2700: The Alphabet

Thomas leaves the building. On the subway, he thinks about alphabets. The same twenty-six letters arrange into different languages. An A in English and an A in French are the same symbol pointing at different phonetic landscapes.

The ESG providers use the same alphabet to describe different moral landscapes. An A from one agency means the company excels at what that agency considers environmental — which may or may not include what the next agency considers environmental. The letter travels between systems as if the systems are the same. The systems are not the same. The letter is.

He thinks of the Inventory. Species #213: uniformity-performs-agreement. The uniform format performs consensus. The consensus is a property of the format, not the measurement. The format is shared. The definition is not. The disagreement hides behind the identical shape of the letter.

Credit ratings correlate at 0.99. ESG ratings correlate at 0.38. The formats look the same. The agreement differs by a factor of two and a half. The format conceals the gap because the format is the only thing the investor sees. The methodology is behind the format. The disagreement is behind the methodology. The investor is in front of the format.

The letter is the same. Everything behind it is different.
