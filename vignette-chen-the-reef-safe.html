Vignette Chen The Reef Safe

Vignette

"Reef safe," Maren said, holding up the sunscreen. "I always use this kind now."

"What does 'reef safe' mean?" Chen asked.

"It won't harm the coral. It's safe for the ocean."

"The phrase has no legal definition. No FDA regulation. No certification standard. The FTC fined Sun Bum $300,000 in 2024 for making that exact claim."

"But it doesn't have oxybenzone."

"Correct. 'Reef safe' typically means the product excludes oxybenzone and octinoxate — two UV filters documented to cause coral bleaching. Hawaii banned them in 2018. The science on those two chemicals is real."

"So it IS reef safe."

"It's free of two chemicals that are documented to harm reefs. The product contains a dozen other ingredients. The zinc oxide is in nanoparticle form — 30 nanometers — small enough to cause oxidative stress in coral larvae, affect sea urchin development, and show toxicity to marine algae in laboratory studies. The other ingredients — emulsifiers, preservatives, stabilizers — have not been tested on marine organisms at relevant concentrations."

"So 'reef safe' means..."

"Two chemicals were removed. The label says 'safe.' The exclusion performs the safety. The removal of two known harms performs the exoneration of everything else."

"But the rest hasn't been tested."

"The rest hasn't been tested. The silence is not evidence of safety. The label treats it as though it is. The consumer reads 'reef safe' and swims into the ocean carrying fourteen ingredients, two of which have been evaluated, twelve of which have not."

Maren looked at the bottle. "The label addresses the two ingredients we know about."

"And performs safety for the twelve we don't. This is the pattern in every label we've examined. The label addresses a part. The consumer infers the whole. The gap between the part and the whole is the label's working space."

"Part safe."

"Part evaluated. The label says the rest."
