The Product

A Chen-Maren Vignette

The department measured productivity.

Productivity was defined as output — publications, conference papers, grant applications, supervised theses. Each was a transaction: a unit of academic production that entered the department's annual report. The report was compiled, submitted to the dean, compared to other departments, and used to determine funding allocations, hiring lines, and program reviews.

Chen's paragraph produced no transactions. It sat at 0.8, dense, recursive, resistant to the forms of output the productivity metric was designed to count. It had not been published as a standalone paper — the committee had not cleared it. It had not been presented at a conference — it was not a conference paper. It had not generated a grant application — no funding agency had a category for a 39-line argument about institutional measurement.

"The paragraph is not productive," Hendricks said, reading the annual report.

"The paragraph has changed how three graduate students write about methodology," Maren said. "It has been cited informally in four dissertation proposals. It has restructured a seminar's reading list. None of these are transactions."

"If they're not transactions, they're not in the report."

"The report measures output. Output is not productivity."

Hendricks paused. "Simon Kuznets said the same thing about national income."

"And the report was adopted anyway."

The department's productivity metric was designed for accounting — for measuring what the department produced in units that could be counted and compared. The metric migrated. It became the assessment of how well the department was doing. A department with high output was thriving. A department with low output was struggling. The metric measured transactions. The transactions were not the department's intellectual contribution any more than GDP was the nation's welfare.

Chen's paragraph contributed nothing to the report. Chen's paragraph had restructured how the department thought about its own measurement systems — a contribution that the measurement system could not measure because the measurement system counted transactions, and the contribution was not a transaction, and what was not a transaction did not exist in the report.

The report was filed. The paragraph sat at 0.8. The gap between them — between what the metric counted and what the paragraph did — was the distance between a product and a contribution, which was the distance between a sum and a welfare, which was the distance Kuznets had warned about in 1934, and which was printed in the founding document of the figure that did not measure what it was used to represent, and the warning was still there, readable, true, and without effect.
