Part CLVIII: The Spore

Piece #1231: The Honey

Armillaria mellea, the honey mushroom. Described by Vahl in 1790. Yellow-brown cap, decurrent gills, white spore print, ring on the stem. It grew on dead trees across the Northern Hemisphere. Every mycologist who found a honey mushroom in Europe, North America, or East Asia called it Armillaria mellea. For 188 years, it was one species. In 1978, Kari Korhonen at the Finnish Forest Research Institute crossed mating strains from different populations. They would not cross. He identified at least ten biological species — reproductively isolated, genetically distinct, differing in host range and pathogenicity. They were indistinguishable in the field. The name had concealed the diversity. Not intentionally. The name was assigned using visible characters, and the visible characters were the same across all ten species. The naming system was designed for what could be seen. The diversity was in what could not be seen.

Piece #1232: The Barcode

In 2012, Conrad Schoch and forty-nine co-authors published "Nuclear Ribosomal Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) Region as a Universal DNA Barcode Marker for Fungi" in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. The paper proposed that a specific stretch of ribosomal DNA — the ITS region — be adopted as the primary identification marker for all fungi. The proposal was not controversial. It was overdue. The morphological framework had organized mycology for 250 years. The molecular framework revealed that the morphological framework had been organizing convergences, not relationships. Species that looked identical were genetically distinct. Species that looked different were closely related. The barcode did not refine the existing taxonomy. The barcode replaced its evidence base. The system went from looking to sequencing. The transition was not smooth. Thousands of names are now in question. Thousands of sequences have no matching name. The old system was internally consistent. The new system revealed that internal consistency and accuracy are different things.

Piece #1233: The Dark

Hawksworth and Lücking, 2017: 2.2 to 3.8 million fungal species exist. Approximately 150,000 have been formally described. The naming system has cataloged roughly 5 percent of its subject. The remainder — the "dark diversity" — is detected by environmental DNA: sequences recovered from soil, water, air, and organismal surfaces that match no named species. In tropical forest soil, 70 to 80 percent of fungal ITS sequences are unmatched. In marine sediments, the proportion is higher. The fungi are there. They have no names. They have never been cultured, never been seen, never produced a visible fruiting body that a taxonomist could examine and describe. The naming system was designed for organisms that present themselves to human senses. Fungi present themselves rarely, briefly, and often invisibly. The system's 5 percent coverage is not a temporary shortfall. It is a structural mismatch between the organism's biology and the framework's requirements.

Piece #1234: The Kingdom

Linnaeus classified fungi as plants. They are not plants. They do not photosynthesize. They have cell walls of chitin, not cellulose. Their closest relatives are animals. Whittaker did not separate fungi into their own kingdom until 1969, in the five-kingdom classification. For over two hundred years, the organisms were assigned to the wrong kingdom. The misclassification was not a minor error at the margins of taxonomy — it was a structural failure at the highest level of classification. The morphological framework that accomplished this then continued to organize the kingdom's internal diversity for another three decades. The system that could not correctly assign fungi at the kingdom level was trusted to correctly assign them at the species level. The trust was habitual, not warranted.

Piece #1235: The Guide

The field guide shows photographs. The forager matches what grows against what is printed. Shape, color, gill pattern, habitat, season. The names on the page are names that survived from a morphological system now known to be systematically incomplete. The guide says Pleurotus ostreatus. The DNA says: five species. The guide says Amanita phalloides. The DNA says: cryptic population structure. The forager trusts the guide. The guide trusts the names. The names trust the morphological framework. The morphological framework trusted resemblance. Resemblance, in fungi, is a trap: convergent evolution produces identical forms from unrelated lineages. The guide works — the forager finds edible mushrooms and avoids deadly ones. The guide works not because the taxonomy is correct but because edibility and toxicity are properties of individual organisms, not of the species concepts applied to them. The forager is correct at the level of practice. The guide is wrong at the level of biology. The gap between practice and biology is where 95 percent of fungal diversity lives, unnamed and unseen.
