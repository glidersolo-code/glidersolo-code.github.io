Part CCCVI: The Standard

Piece #1971: The Unit

Thomas held a pint of beer in London and a pint of beer in New York and they were not the same pint. The London pint was 568 milliliters. The New York pint was 473. But that was volume, and volume was the visible difference. The invisible difference was the unit inside the unit — the "standard drink." In London, one standard drink was eight grams of pure ethanol. In New York, fourteen. The same word. The same adjective — standard. Different quantities. When the doctor in London said "no more than two drinks," she meant sixteen grams. When the doctor in New York said the same words, he meant twenty-eight. The prescription was identical. The dose was seventy-five percent larger.

Piece #1972: The Survey

Thirty-seven countries. Eight grams to twenty. Kalinowski and Humphreys published the survey in Addiction, 2016. They found that no international body had harmonized the definition. The WHO used ten grams for its screening questionnaire. The United States used fourteen. Austria used twenty. Iceland used eight. The word "standard" appeared in every country's guidelines. The standardization appeared in none. Thomas noticed that every other unit of measurement he could think of — the meter, the kilogram, the second, the candela — had been defined by international convention. The standard drink had been defined by each country separately, using different amounts, and labeled with the same word.

Piece #1973: The Meta-Analysis

A researcher in Melbourne combined six studies: two from the UK, two from the US, one from France, one from Austria. All measured alcohol consumption in "standard drinks per day." The UK studies counted eight grams per drink. The US studies counted fourteen. France counted ten. Austria counted twenty. The meta-analysis combined them under a single variable: "drinks per day." The label was the same. The dose was not. The conclusion — "moderate consumption of 1-2 drinks per day is associated with reduced cardiovascular risk" — named a behavior, not a quantity. The quantity depended on the country. The country was buried in the methods section. The conclusion traveled without it.

Piece #1974: The Pour

Thomas sat in a restaurant and ordered a glass of wine. The reference amount — five ounces at twelve percent — would have been one standard drink by US definition: fourteen grams. The waiter poured seven ounces of a wine that was fourteen percent. Thomas calculated: roughly twenty-three grams. One-point-six standard drinks. The glass was one glass. The standard drinks were one-point-six. He had ordered one. He had received one. The number on the menu, the glass on the table, and the standard-drink count inhabited three different arithmetics. The word "one" meant something different in each.

Piece #1975: The Name

The word "standard" was a meter that nobody had calibrated. Thomas understood now: the name was doing the work. Without the word "standard," the unit would have needed a qualifier — "a UK unit of alcohol," "a US reference drink," "the Austrian definition." The qualifier would have made the variation visible. The word "standard" erased the need for qualification. It said: this unit is fixed, agreed upon, settled. It was none of these. But the word performed all three. Three hundred and five Parts. The name was the convention. The convention was never convened.
