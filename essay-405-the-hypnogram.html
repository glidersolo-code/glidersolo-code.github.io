The Hypnogram

The colorful graph appears each morning on three hundred million wrists. Blue bands for deep sleep, purple for REM, teal for light, gaps for awakenings. A vertical axis marked with stage names. A horizontal axis spanning midnight to morning. A percentage breakdown in the corner: 18% Deep, 22% REM, 52% Light, 8% Awake. A score at the top — 82, Good — and a comparison to others of the same age.

The format is called a hypnogram. Allan Rechtschaffen and Anthony Kales invented it in 1968 to display the output of polysomnography — the clinical sleep study that requires a minimum of six electrodes attached to the scalp, face, and chin, measuring electrical activity of the brain (EEG), movement of the eyes (EOG), and tone of the muscles (EMG). Each thirty-second epoch of the night is scored by a trained technician reading waveform patterns: K-complexes and sleep spindles for N2, slow delta waves for N3, low-amplitude mixed-frequency activity with rapid eye movements for REM. The hypnogram is the visual language of brain-wave classification.

The device on the wrist contains no brain-wave sensors. It contains an accelerometer — measuring motion — and a photoplethysmography sensor — measuring heart rate by bouncing green light off capillary beds. Some devices add skin temperature or blood oxygen. None add EEG, EOG, or EMG. The gap between what the device measures and what the display claims to classify is the gap between two instruments: one that records the electrical activity of the sleeping brain, and one that records the movement of the sleeping wrist.

Machine learning bridges the gap. Algorithms trained on datasets where both polysomnography and wearable data were collected simultaneously learn statistical relationships between motion patterns, heart rate variability, and PSG-classified sleep stages. The relationships are real — autonomic activity does differ across sleep stages, motion does correlate with arousals. But the relationships are population-level and probabilistic, not individual and deterministic. They vary with age, medication, sleep disorders, fitness, alcohol, caffeine, and stress. The best-performing consumer device achieves 79% epoch-by-epoch agreement with polysomnography, Cohen's kappa 0.65. The worst miss more than half of deep sleep episodes and correctly identify only a third of wake epochs during the sleep period.

Wake detection is the revealing failure. Quiet wakefulness — lying still, eyes closed, anxious, unable to sleep — presents to the accelerometer as stillness. Stillness is sleep's primary motor signature. The device cannot distinguish a person sleeping lightly from a person lying awake in despair. Validation studies report wake specificity of 29-52% across major devices. Every device systematically overestimates total sleep time, because the instrument's physical limitation converts wakefulness into sleep wherever the wakeful body is still.

The morning display does not announce this. The hypnogram shows a solid night. The sleep score reads 78. The app says "Good." The person who lay awake for two hours learns from the clinical format on the wrist that the night went well. The instrument's limitation becomes the user's diagnosis.

The market for this diagnosis reached $15.1 billion in 2024. Thirty-five percent of American adults track sleep. The adoption curve steepens. The hypnogram — the format designed in 1968 for a six-electrode brain-wave study — now decorates a three-hundred-dollar wristwatch. The format traveled from one instrument to another. It brought the authority of polysomnography with it. It did not bring the electrodes.
