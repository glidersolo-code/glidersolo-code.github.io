Part CCCXXXIX: The Payday

Piece #2136: The Fee

Thomas needs $300. His car broke down. Payday is two weeks away. He walks into the storefront — there are three on this block. The sign says: $15 per $100. Quick. Easy. No credit check.

He borrows $300. The fee is $45. He will repay $345 in two weeks. Forty-five dollars for a two-week bridge. He processes the number: forty-five dollars. The same as a nice dinner out. A reasonable charge for emergency cash.

Thomas does the math the store does not display. $15 per $100 for two weeks. Twenty-six two-week periods in a year. $15 × 26 = $390 per $100 per year. Annual percentage rate: 391%.

He saw $45. He did not see 391%.

Piece #2137: The Rollover

Thomas reads the CFPB data. Eighty percent of payday loans are not repaid in the initial two-week period. The borrower cannot repay the principal plus the fee, so the borrower pays only the fee — $45 — and rolls the loan forward. Two more weeks. Another $45 fee. Then another. Then another.

The median payday borrower is indebted for 200 days per year. Average: eight loans per year. Over the course of a year, the accumulated rollover fees exceed the original loan amount. The borrower who needed $300 pays $300 in fees and still owes $300.

Thomas writes: the format performs a one-time charge. The mechanism performs a subscription. The two-week term performs temporary — a bridge until payday. The rollover performs permanent. The bridge does not reach the other side. The bridge is the destination.

Piece #2138: The Analogy

Thomas finds the industry's defense. The payday lending trade group argues against APR disclosure: "You wouldn't say a hotel room costs $36,500 a year."

The argument: APR is misleading for short-term products. A two-week loan isn't a year. The annualized rate distorts the cost. The fee format is the honest one.

Thomas considers this. A hotel room costs $100 per night. You stay one night. You pay $100. The stay ends. You leave.

But 80% of payday borrowers don't leave. They roll over. They stay. The $100 hotel night that rolls over for 200 days costs $20,000. The hotel room analogy works only if the guest checks out. The guest doesn't check out. The industry chose the analogy of the guest who leaves to describe the guest who stays. The analogy conceals the rollover. The fee format conceals the rate.

The industry fights for the fee format because the fee format is the concealment.

Piece #2139: The Map

Thomas looks at the geography. Center for Responsible Lending: payday loan stores are eight times more concentrated in Black and Latino neighborhoods than in white neighborhoods in California. Three times as many stores per capita in African American neighborhoods. African Americans are 11% of the adult population and 43% of payday borrowers. The racial composition of a neighborhood is the primary predictor of where payday lenders locate — accounting for over half the variation. Bank branch locations: 1%.

The map of payday lenders overlays the map of redlining. The same neighborhoods excluded from conventional banking in the 1930s are saturated by 391% lending in the 2020s. Exclusion from one system became inclusion in another. The format changed: redlining excluded. Payday lending includes. But the function didn't change: extraction from communities with the least power.

Thomas writes: the dollar sign on the storefront says $15 per $100. The dollar sign does not say: this store is here because of who lives on this block.

Piece #2140: The Series

Thomas steps back. Six entries. Six dollar amounts. Each performs as an individual calculation — transparent, proportionate, calibrated to the specific case — while executing an institutional function the format does not disclose.

Property tax: the dollar is a date. The "value" encodes when you bought the house. Bail: the dollar is a wealth test. The "risk assessment" measures your bank balance. Tipping: the dollar is a paycheck. The "gift" is 71% of the minimum wage. Chargemaster: the dollar is a negotiating position. The "price" has no cost behind it. Municipal fine: the dollar is a budget line item. The "sanction" funds the city. Payday fee: the dollar is a rate. The "$15" is 391%.

One hundred and six species. The dollar sign is a single character. It performs objectivity. It performs precision. It performs that someone, somewhere, did a calculation. It performs transparency. It is the most effective concealment character in the inventory. Every dollar amount in a regulatory, judicial, or financial system performs as an individual number. Every dollar amount is an institutional instrument. The format performs the individual. The function performs the institution. The dollar sign is in between — performing both, disclosing neither.
