The Night

Maren's cousin spent four months sleeping in her car in 2019. Chattanooga. She had lost her apartment after a medical bill she couldn't pay. She stayed with friends for two weeks, then another friend, then the friends ran out. She slept in the Walmart parking lot on Lebanon Pike. She was not counted.

"She wasn't literally homeless by HUD's definition," Maren says. "She had a vehicle. She was in a parking lot. But she wasn't in a shelter or on the street in January."

"Was there a count that year?"

"There's a count every year. One night. Last ten days of January. Volunteers walk pre-assigned zones between midnight and dawn. My cousin was in her car in a Walmart parking lot in Chattanooga. Nobody walked through the Walmart parking lot."

Chen reads the definitions. Literal homelessness: shelters and streets. Not doubled up. Not in vehicles unless the vehicle is in an area specifically canvassed. Not in jails or hospitals, even if the person was homeless before admission and will be homeless after discharge.

"The McKinney-Vento Education program uses a broader definition," Maren says. "It includes doubling up. 1.4 million homeless students. The PIT found 110,000 under 18. Thirteen to one."

"Thirteen to one."

"Same children. Same country. Two definitions. The narrow definition determines the federal funding. The broad definition determines the school services. The children who qualify for school support don't exist in the federal homelessness count."

Chen reads the climate data. Peter Rossi, Chicago, 1985. The unsheltered count fell 62% from fall to winter. "January."

"January. In Minneapolis, the cold drives people indoors — into shelters, where they're counted as sheltered homeless. In Los Angeles, the weather lets them stay outside. New York has a legal right to shelter — 95% sheltered on count night. LA has no right to shelter — 70% unsheltered. The comparison says LA's crisis dwarfs New York's. The comparison is measuring climate and law, not homelessness."

"And the annual numbers?"

"The one-night PIT found 591,768 in 2013. Over the year, 1.4 million used shelters. 2.4 times as many. Annual homelessness: 3.4 times the PIT. Lifetime prevalence: 7.4% of the US population. One night says 600,000. One year says two million. One lifetime says twenty million."

"Three numbers. Three framings."

"Three policy responses. The one-night frame says: build more shelters for a chronic population. The annual frame says: prevent homelessness and rehouse quickly. The lifetime frame says: fix housing policy. The PIT chose the first frame. The funding formula locked it in. $3.2 billion allocated based on the one-night count. The doubled-up families generate zero federal dollars, by explicit HUD policy."

Chen is quiet for a moment. "Your cousin."

"She got a waitressing job in March. Saved enough for first and last. Moved into a studio in April. She was homeless for four months and invisible for all of them. Not in the count. Not in the definition. Not in the funding formula. The definition said she wasn't homeless. She said she was sleeping in a Walmart parking lot in January while volunteers were counting bodies on the other side of town."

"The count happened on one night."

"The homelessness happened every night. The gap between the night and the year is not a measurement error. It's the measurement."
