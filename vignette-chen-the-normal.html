The Normal

"My doctor says my thyroid is fine," Maren said. "TSH 3.8. Normal."

Chen asked, "Which laboratory?"

"Does it matter?"

"If the laboratory uses an upper limit of 4.5, you're normal. If it uses 2.5, you're subclinically hypothyroid. Same blood. Same number. Different reference range."

"How can two laboratories disagree on what's normal?"

"Because the reference range is not a fact about the body. It's a fact about the sample from which it was derived."

Chen's paragraph was about the relationship between the reference population and the individual. The original TSH reference range was established from a population that included people with undiagnosed Hashimoto's thyroiditis — an autoimmune condition that elevates TSH. The disease the test was designed to detect was present in the sample used to define normal. The contamination was invisible: the autoimmune disease was undiagnosed, so the sample appeared healthy.

"When they excluded those individuals retrospectively," Chen said, "the upper limit dropped. The NACB recommended 2.5. The ATA agreed. The Endocrine Society didn't. A 2004 consensus panel of three societies ended without consensus. Between 2.5 and 4.5 lies a population of tens of millions — predominantly women over 50 — whose diagnosis depends on which committee their physician follows."

"And if they're diagnosed?"

"Levothyroxine. Twenty-three million prescriptions per year. The TRUST trial randomized 737 adults over 65 with subclinical hypothyroidism to levothyroxine or placebo. No difference in symptoms, quality of life, or cardiovascular events. The TSH improved. The patients didn't."

"The number improved but the person didn't."

"The number was always improvable. The reference range generates the diagnosis. The diagnosis generates the prescription. The prescription normalizes the number. The number's improvement confirms the system. The patient was never the thing being measured."

Maren was quiet for a moment. "And age?"

"TSH rises naturally with age. An 80-year-old's TSH of 4.0 is below the 70th percentile for their age. A 25-year-old's is at the 98th. The reference range isn't stratified. Same boundary, all ages. The reference population's age structure is embedded in the range and applied as though it were biology."

The paragraph sat at 0.8. It was, like a reference range, a boundary between categories — the paragraph and everything else — derived from a sample that included the condition it was meant to distinguish, applied without stratification across contexts that required it, and experienced as a fact about the thing being measured rather than the system doing the measuring.
