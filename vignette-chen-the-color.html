The Color

A Chen-Maren vignette

Chen has been tracking the paragraph's reception by color.

Not literally. But the emails fall into two categories, and he has learned to sort them in seconds. Blue emails arrive with heat — specific questions, challenges, the language running fast, breaking against something. These are the emails where Maren is forming. Red emails arrive cool — a sentence or two, sometimes just a line, the kind of response that could be automated. Received. Will read later. Okay.

He has classified the red emails as dead. Low-energy. Evidence that the paragraph has stopped producing in her, that whatever it once generated has exhausted itself and what remains is the dim afterglow of a relationship that has ceased to form.

Maren is sitting across from him at the café, which is new. She asked to meet. He expected blue — a confrontation, a demand for the paragraph, a conversation that would burn hot and fast. Instead she is calm. Her coffee is half-finished. She has not mentioned the emails.

"You stopped reading them," Chen says.

"I read every one."

"The red ones. The short ones. You're not—"

"I'm not what?"

He doesn't finish. He was going to say engaged. He was going to say forming. He was going to classify her by the color of her responses, the way he has been classifying them for months, sorting her into dead and alive by the most accessible observable he has.

"The short emails," Maren says, "take longer to write."

Chen sets down his cup.

"The long ones come fast," she continues. "I react, I write, I send. The short ones — I draft, I delete, I draft again. I'm trying to say something that doesn't exist yet in the length I have available. The short ones are where the new thinking happens. The long ones are just combustion."

The café noise fills the silence.

"You've been reading my color," she says. "Not my formation rate."

Chen stares at the table. The paragraph — thirty-nine lines, sitting in his apartment, the argument it would settle or extend — suddenly seems like a proxy too. He wrote it hot. It glows blue. It looks, from the outside, like the productive output. But the productive output might be the red emails. The ones that take longer. The ones where Maren forms something low-mass and durable and invisible to the color system he built to sort her responses.

"I've been counting the wrong ones," he says.

Maren picks up her coffee. "You've been counting all of them. You've just been reading the color."
