Part CDLXX: The Class Size

Piece #2791: The Brochure

The glossy page says: Average Class Size: 20.

Thomas holds the brochure open on his kitchen table, next to a bowl of cold cereal. He is deciding between universities. One reports 20. Another reports 22. The numbers are small enough to imagine — a room where the professor knows names. A seminar table. Discussion.

He chooses the smaller number.

His first Monday: Introduction to Psychology, Lecture Hall C. He counts the seats as he climbs to the back row. Two hundred on the floor. Three hundred in the balcony. The professor is a figure at a podium, projected on two screens.

Average class size: 20.

He is in a class of 500. The average described the institution's inventory. He heard it as a description of his life.

Piece #2792: The Paradox

The mathematics is not complicated.

One class of 500. Fifty classes of 10. Total classes: 51. Total students: 1,000. Average class size: 1,000 ÷ 51 ≈ 20.

But ask each student: how large is your class? Five hundred students say 500. Five hundred students say 10. The average of their answers: (500 × 500 + 10 × 500) ÷ 1,000 = 255.

The institutional average: 20. The experienced average: 255.

Both are correct. Both are averages. One counts classes. The other counts students. The brochure chose the smaller one. Not by error. By perspective.

The structure is general. Feld proved it in 1991 for friendships: your friends have more friends than you do, on average, because popular people appear in more friendship lists. Large classes contain more students who experience them. Busy bus routes carry more passengers who wait for them. The experience-weighted mean always exceeds the item-weighted mean. Always. The only exception is perfect uniformity.

Thomas does not know the paradox has a name. He knows that his classes are larger than the brochure promised. He attributes the gap to bad luck, not to arithmetic.

Piece #2793: The Schedule

By his third semester, Thomas has learned to read the course catalog differently.

He looks for the enrollment cap, not the average. A cap of 25 means a room he can learn in. A cap of 400 means a room he survives. The catalog lists both, and the average blends them into a number that describes neither.

His advisor says: "You should take the large lectures early. Get them out of the way."

The phrase — get them out of the way — treats the large class as an obstacle between Thomas and his education. Not as the education itself. The lecture hall is the thing to endure so that the seminar table becomes available. The institution's structure — mass enrollment subsidizing small sections — is legible to the advisor but invisible in the brochure.

Thomas schedules three lectures and one seminar. His experienced average class size this semester: 388.

The brochure still says 20.

Piece #2794: The Bus

Thomas waits for the campus shuttle.

The schedule says: every 10 minutes. He waits 18 minutes. The next day, 14 minutes. The day after, 22 minutes. He begins to suspect the schedule is wrong.

It isn't wrong. The buses run every 10 minutes on average. But the gaps vary — 5 minutes, then 15, then 8, then 12. More passengers arrive during longer gaps. More passengers experience the longer waits. The average wait experienced by passengers always exceeds half the average gap between buses. The same structure. The same paradox. The item (bus gap) has one average. The experience (passenger wait) has another.

Thomas does not connect the bus to the lecture hall. The brochure's class size and the schedule's bus frequency are the same lie told by different institutions: a number computed from the institution's perspective, received as though computed from the individual's.

The bus arrives. It is full. More passengers ride the crowded buses.

Piece #2795: The Weighting

Years later, Thomas teaches a seminar of 12.

He fills out an institutional survey. One question: "What is your class size?" He writes: 12.

Across campus, a colleague teaches a lecture of 600. She writes: 600.

The institution averages their answers with every other instructor's. The result — the institutional average — goes into the brochure. The number is correct. It counts what the institution counts: classes.

But if the institution surveyed students instead of instructors, the number would be different. Larger. Structurally larger. The same enrollment, the same rooms, the same semester — a different average. The choice to survey instructors rather than students is not neutral. It is a weighting decision. It determines the perspective from which the average is computed. The perspective determines the magnitude.

Thomas writes 12 on the form. Six hundred students in his colleague's lecture would write 600. Twelve voices say 12. Six hundred voices say 600. The form asks the twelve.
