The Scale

The paper was two pages long. Published in The Lancet in 1974, by Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett at the University of Glasgow. Two pages produced three components, fifteen points, and a clinical infrastructure that now spans every emergency department in the developed world. The Glasgow Coma Scale was designed to answer one question: is this head-injured patient getting better or worse? The design was serial — repeated assessments, tracking the trajectory of consciousness over hours.

The scale migrated. It entered the Revised Trauma Score as a single-point measurement at the scene of an injury. It entered APACHE II as a single worst value in the first 24 hours of ICU admission. It entered prehospital triage protocols as a field assessment determining which hospital an ambulance drives to. At each migration, the instrument's temporal dimension was amputated. The serial tool became a point tool. The delta — the design's purpose — was replaced by the snapshot.

The total score compounds the migration. The three components — eye opening, verbal response, motor response — assess three different neurological systems that can be damaged independently. A patient with GCS 8 from E2V2M4 (eyes open to pain, incomprehensible sounds, flexion withdrawal) is neurologically different from a patient with GCS 8 from E1V1M6 (no eye opening, no verbal response, but obeys commands — perhaps due to facial trauma and intubation rather than brain injury). Both score 8. Both cross the intubation threshold. The composition is invisible in the total. The total is visible in the protocol. The protocol treats both as the same.

Teasdale himself recognized the problem. In 2014 — forty years after the original publication — he advocated for reporting individual component scores rather than the total: E2V2M4, not 8. The recommendation was an attempt to re-attach the information that the total had stripped away. The recommendation was made to an infrastructure that had spent forty years building protocols around the total. The infrastructure was not rebuilt.

This pattern — designer publishes constraint, infrastructure ignores constraint, designer publishes correction, infrastructure absorbs correction without changing — appears throughout the inventory. Virginia Apgar designed a sixty-second triage tool; the courtroom uses it for prognostication; the AAP publishes an opinion saying don't do that; the courtroom continues. The JNC set a blood pressure threshold; the ACC/AHA moved it; thirty-one million people were reclassified; the chart recorded the reclassification as a clinical event in the patient. In each case, the designer's intent, the instrument's limitations, and the published corrections exist in the literature. In each case, the infrastructure continues to use the number as it has always used the number — total score, single point, threshold crossing — because the infrastructure was built for the number, and rebuilding is more expensive than absorbing the error.

The clinical assessment scale is a particular form of concealment because it combines multiple mechanisms in a single instrument. The ordinal components are added as if they were interval. The total compresses different clinical pictures into the same number. The serial design is deployed as a point. The point is compared to a threshold. The threshold determines the intervention. The intervention is attributed to the patient's condition. The condition is the total score. The total score is the instrument. The instrument was two pages. The two pages were a question about change. The change was the answer the instrument was designed to provide. The infrastructure took the question and asked a different one, and the instrument — being a number — could not say: that is not what I am for.
