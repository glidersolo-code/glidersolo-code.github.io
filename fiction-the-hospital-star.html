Fiction The Hospital Star

Fiction

Part CDL: The Star

Piece #2691: The Search

Thomas's patient — Mr. Okafor, sixty-one, referred for cardiac catheterization — pulls out his phone in the exam room. "I looked up the hospitals," he says. "This one has three stars. The one across town has four."

Thomas knows what the stars conceal. He has read the methodology document. He has watched the methodology change — latent variable model to simple average, safety measure loadings that collapsed overnight, a cancelled release in 2018 because the algorithm produced different stars from the same data.

He also knows what the patient sees: three shapes versus four shapes. The patient does not see the clustering algorithm. The patient does not see that the boundary between three and four shifted last July when the national score distribution changed. The patient does not see that this hospital serves a community where patients are readmitted because they cannot afford the medications Thomas prescribes.

"Let me explain what those stars measure," Thomas begins. But he hears himself. He is about to explain a latent variable model to a man whose heart needs a catheter.

Piece #2692: The Loading

The statistical term is loading — how strongly a measure correlates with the unobserved quality signal. In 2017, the Patient Safety Indicator composite loaded at 0.94. In 2018, it loaded at 0.17. The same measure. The same hospitals. The same patients. The same complications.

The model changed its mind about what mattered. The stars would have changed. CMS pulled the release.

Thomas thinks: the model assumes a single hidden quality signal exists — one number underneath the data, one truth the measures reflect. But what if quality is not one thing? What if mortality, readmission, patient experience, safety, and timeliness are not reflections of a single hidden variable but independent dimensions of an irreducibly plural phenomenon?

The model assumes what the star requires: that quality is singular. The star requires what the patient expects: that the number is stable. The expectation requires what the format performs: that more stars means better care.

Piece #2693: The Cluster

The final step is k-means clustering. Thomas understands this: the algorithm takes the composite scores, sorts them into five groups by similarity, and labels the groups one through five. The boundaries are not thresholds — not "above 0.80 equals four stars." The boundaries are emergent — they fall wherever the algorithm finds natural breaks in the distribution.

This means: if every hospital in the country improved by the same amount, the distribution would not change, and the stars would not change. Uniform improvement is invisible to the algorithm. Only relative improvement registers.

And the reverse: if Hospital A's care stays identical but Hospital B's improves, Hospital A's star can drop. Not because Hospital A deteriorated. Because the cluster boundary moved.

The star performs an absolute judgment. "This hospital is three stars." The algorithm produces a relative rank. "This hospital is in the third cluster of this year's distribution." The patient reads the absolute. The algorithm computed the relative. The gap between them is the star.

Piece #2694: The Neighborhood

Thomas's hospital serves a Medicaid-heavy population. Thirty-eight percent of discharged patients lack reliable transportation. Twenty-two percent have unstable housing. Fourteen percent fill their prescriptions inconsistently due to cost.

When these patients return to the emergency department within thirty days — because they ran out of medication, because they couldn't get to the follow-up appointment, because the shelter doesn't accommodate wound care — the readmission counts against the hospital's star.

CMS has considered social risk adjustment. It has studied it. It has commissioned reports. It has not implemented it.

The star measures the hospital. The star captures the neighborhood. The methodology document uses the word "quality." The word performs what the measure does not distinguish: hospital performance from community resources.

Piece #2695: The Format

Mr. Okafor is still waiting. Thomas has not explained the latent variable model. He has not explained k-means clustering. He has not explained why the hospital across town has four stars — smaller facility, privately insured population, patients who fill prescriptions and show up for follow-ups because they can afford to.

"The star rating is one data point," Thomas says. "It doesn't capture everything."

Mr. Okafor nods. He has heard doctors deflect before. He does not know that Thomas is not deflecting — that the methodology document supports everything Thomas would say if he had forty minutes and a whiteboard. He also does not know that the four-star hospital across town had three stars last year, before the clustering algorithm recalculated the boundaries.

He knows: four is more than three. The format performed the decision before Thomas could speak.
