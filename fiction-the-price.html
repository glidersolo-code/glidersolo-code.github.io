Part CXL: The Price

Piece #1141: The Willingness

Thomas had a price.

Not a price in the sense of a charge — no invoice accompanied his birth. A price in the sense of the Value of a Statistical Life: a dollar figure assigned to the risk reduction associated with preventing one death. The figure, as of 2024, was $13.7 million according to the Department of Transportation and $11.6 million according to the Environmental Protection Agency. Thomas was worth two different amounts depending on which agency was counting.

The number was derived from wage-risk studies. Economists examined workers who accepted dangerous jobs — coal miners, loggers, construction workers — and measured the wage premium they received for the additional mortality risk. From the premium, the economists inferred a willingness to pay: if a worker accepted $700 in additional annual wages for a 1-in-10,000 increase in mortality risk, the implied value of a statistical life was $7 million. The arithmetic was straightforward. The inference was not: it assumed that wage premiums reflected voluntary choices, that workers had accurate information about risk, that the labor market was competitive, and that the risk preferences of male blue-collar workers in the 1970s and 1980s could be applied universally.

Thomas was not a male blue-collar worker in the 1980s. Thomas was an infant. The number was applied to him anyway.

Piece #1142: The Formula

The formula was: Benefits = VSL × Deaths Prevented.

A regulation that would prevent 100 deaths per year had annual benefits of $1.37 billion (at DOT's $13.7 million) or $1.16 billion (at EPA's $11.6 million). If the regulation cost more than the benefits, it was not cost-effective. If it cost less, it was. The formula converted an ethical question — should we prevent these deaths? — into an arithmetic question — does the number exceed the cost?

The conversion was the point. Executive Order 12291, issued by the Reagan administration in 1981, required that major regulations demonstrate benefits exceeding costs. The requirement needed a number. W. Kip Viscusi, an economist, provided one. The number was adopted. The number was updated for inflation. The number was cited in thousands of regulatory impact analyses. The number became the infrastructure through which the government decided whether to save lives.

Thomas's life entered the formula through his ZIP code. The regulation that reduced pollution in his neighborhood had benefits calculated using the VSL. The benefits were compared to the costs of industrial compliance. The comparison determined whether the air he breathed would be cleaned. The formula worked. The formula was the question: at what price was Thomas's air worth cleaning?

Piece #1143: The Discount

The senior death discount lasted two years.

In 2003, the EPA applied an age adjustment to the VSL: lives of people over 65 were valued at 63% of the standard figure. The adjustment was based on research suggesting that willingness to pay for risk reduction declined with age. The adjustment meant that a regulation primarily benefiting the elderly was less likely to pass the cost-benefit test. AARP protested. Congress held hearings. The EPA withdrew the adjustment and declared it would use a uniform VSL regardless of age.

The withdrawal did not resolve the problem. It relocated it. The uniform VSL treated every life as equally valued — $13.7 million, infant and octogenarian alike. But the methodology that produced the number reflected the preferences of middle-aged workers. The uniformity was a policy decision layered over a methodological particularity. The number appeared universal because the policy declared it so, not because the data supported universality.

Thomas's VSL was the same as his grandmother's. The sameness was a political choice made in response to a political controversy. The controversy was about a number. The number was about a life. The life was not a number.

Piece #1144: The Elasticity

The VSL had an income elasticity of 0.5 to 0.6.

Income elasticity: for every 1% increase in income, the VSL increased by 0.5% to 0.6%. The relationship meant that the statistical value of a life in a wealthy country was higher than the statistical value of a life in a poor country. The same regulation — preventing the same number of deaths from the same cause — would pass the cost-benefit test in the United States and fail it in Bangladesh. Not because the deaths were different. Because the incomes were different.

The elasticity was not a moral claim. It was a mathematical relationship derived from the data. The data showed that wealthier populations exhibited higher willingness to pay for risk reduction. The finding was unsurprising: people with more money could afford to pay more. But when the finding entered the formula, it produced a result: some deaths were worth preventing and others were not, and the difference was income.

Thomas's income elasticity was zero. Thomas had no income. Thomas was an infant. The VSL applied to him was derived from workers who earned wages and accepted risks. Thomas earned nothing and accepted nothing. The number was applied to him by extrapolation — from workers to non-workers, from adults to infants, from choices to conditions. The extrapolation was the bridge between the data and the decision. The bridge was made of arithmetic. The arithmetic was solid. What it connected was the question.

Piece #1145: The Price

The price was the concealment.

Not the dollar figure — the figure was public, published, debated. The concealment was the conversion: the moment an ethical question became an arithmetic operation. Should we prevent these deaths? became a formula. The formula required a number. The number required a methodology. The methodology required data. The data required a population. The population was specific: male blue-collar workers in the 1970s and 1980s. The specificity was generalizable, the methodology assumed. The assumption was the bridge. The bridge was the formula. The formula was the policy. The policy determined who lived.

Thomas's chart was a price list. Every measurement — birth weight, gestational age, Apgar, bilirubin, hematocrit — was a number that triggered a decision. The decision allocated resources. The resources had costs. The costs were compared to benefits. The benefits were calculated using a number derived from workers who never knew Thomas, in decades before Thomas was born, making choices Thomas would never make.

1145 pieces. The formula runs. The formula is not the question. The formula replaced the question. The replacement is the price.
