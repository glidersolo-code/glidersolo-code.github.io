The Rating

John Moody published his first bond ratings in 1909 — letter grades assigned to railroad debt. Aaa, Aa, A, Baa, Ba, B, Caa, Ca, C. Standard & Poor's and Fitch Ratings followed. Three private companies in New York assigning letters to the debts of corporations, municipalities, and sovereign nations worldwide. The letters appear on bond prospectuses, in regulatory filings, in pension fund mandates. AAA means safety. The letter does not explain its reasoning. The methodology is proprietary. The letter speaks.

The entity being rated pays for the rating. The "issuer pays" model has been standard since the 1970s — before that, investors paid for access to ratings. Under the current model, a corporation wanting to sell bonds hires a rating agency and pays for the evaluation. The agency produces a letter. Investors read the letter as an independent assessment. The assessment is produced by a company paid by the borrower to produce it. The conflict is disclosed. The conflict is visible. The conflict persists because the alternative — investor-pays — collapsed when ratings became freely circulated, and because regulatory mandates make ratings functionally mandatory regardless of who pays.

In 2006 and 2007, the three major agencies assigned AAA ratings to tranches of collateralized debt obligations backed by subprime mortgages. The CDO structures were modeled on assumptions that national housing prices would not decline simultaneously. The assumption was embedded in the model. The model produced the letter. By 2008, the securities rated AAA were worthless or near-worthless. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission found that the agencies had assigned their highest ratings to securities that defaulted at rates hundreds of times higher than historical AAA default rates. The letter was not simply a description of credit quality. It was a regulatory gateway: pension funds were mandated to hold AAA-rated paper, banks counted AAA securities as risk-free capital, money market funds were required to hold top-rated instruments. The letter created the conditions under which the risk was concentrated.

On August 5, 2011, S&P downgraded the United States from AAA to AA+. The Treasury found a two-trillion-dollar arithmetic error in S&P's fiscal projection and notified the agency before publication. S&P acknowledged the error and downgraded anyway. The rating was legally classified as an opinion — protected speech, not subject to the same liability as an audit or warranty. An opinion that moved trillions of dollars in capital allocation, that changed interest rates on government debt, that triggered automatic selling by funds mandated to hold only AAA paper — classified as speech.

The Securities and Exchange Commission designates "Nationally Recognized Statistical Rating Organizations." Bank capital requirements under Basel II and III reference NRSRO ratings. Pension funds are mandated — by charter or by law — to hold only investment-grade securities. The government confers recognition on the agencies. The agencies' letters then determine what government-regulated institutions can buy. The circularity is structural: the regulator empowers the rater, and the rating constrains the regulated. A letter from a private company, paid for by the entity it evaluates, using methodology it does not fully disclose, classified as opinion rather than measurement, functioning as regulatory infrastructure that determines the allocation of trillions of dollars — experienced by markets as an objective assessment of credit quality.
