Part CCCXLVI: The Policy

Piece #2171: The Document

Thomas reads a privacy policy.

Not skims. Not scrolls past. Reads. Word by word. The way he reads everything else — contracts, menus, parking signs, the fine print on receipts. He starts at the top. "We value your privacy." Four words that could mean anything. He continues.

The policy is 6,938 words. He knows this because he counted. At his reading speed — about 250 words per minute — it will take twenty-eight minutes. He has nowhere to be.

By paragraph three, he has encountered the word "may" eleven times. "We may collect." "We may share." "We may use." The word performs possibility. The function performs certainty. Everything behind "may" is everything the company does. The word exists not to describe a condition but to soften a declaration. "We collect" is a statement. "We may collect" is a courtesy.

He finishes in thirty-one minutes. He understood perhaps 60 percent. The parts he didn't understand involved phrases like "legitimate business interest" and "de-identified aggregate data" and "as permitted by applicable law." Each phrase pointed to a body of law he hadn't read, which pointed to regulations he didn't know existed, which pointed to enforcement actions he would never see.

He clicks "I agree."

The same click everyone else clicks. The difference is he read the document first. The sameness is the click. The reading changed nothing about what the click does. The click does not mean "I understand." The click means "I have been told."

Piece #2172: The Hours

Two hundred and forty-four hours.

Thomas calculates. If he reads every privacy policy for every website he visits in a year — 1,462 sites, average policy 2,514 words — it would take 244 hours. Seventy-six working days. Nearly four months of an eight-hour workday, reading nothing but privacy policies.

No one does this. The number is not an estimate of what people spend. It is an estimate of what the system assumes they spend. The legal framework — "notice and consent" — rests on the fiction that the notice has been given and the consent is informed. Two hundred and forty-four hours is the size of the fiction.

He thinks about what else takes 244 hours. Learning to play piano at a basic level. Running 24 marathons. Reading War and Peace four times. Reading the Bible cover to cover, twice.

The privacy policy framework asks for the equivalent of two Bible readings per year, distributed across 1,462 documents, each written at a college reading level for an audience that reads at eighth grade.

The framework does not expect compliance. The framework expects the gap. The gap IS the framework.

Piece #2173: The Firstborn

Thomas reads about the study.

Five hundred and forty-three university students. Communications students — people trained to read carefully. A fictitious social network called NameDrop. The privacy policy contained a clause requiring users to surrender their firstborn child to the company. Another clause said data would be shared with the NSA.

Ninety-eight percent agreed without raising concern.

He sits with this number. Ninety-eight percent. Not of random strangers. Of communications students at a university. People who study how information works. People who know what a privacy policy is. People who could, if they chose, read the document.

Seventy-four percent didn't read it at all. They chose "quick join." The remaining twenty-six percent scrolled through. Ninety-eight percent of all participants — readers and non-readers — agreed to give away their children.

The researchers called their paper "The Biggest Lie on the Internet."

Thomas thinks: the lie is not that people don't read. The lie is that the reading would matter. The firstborn clause is absurd. It would never be enforced. But the data-sharing clause — the one about the NSA — that one is not absurd. That one describes what actually happens. The absurd clause and the real clause sit in the same document, receive the same click, produce the same consent. The absurdity of one does not distinguish it from the reality of the other. The click treats them identically.

Piece #2174: The Banner

Thomas visits a European website. A banner appears. It covers the bottom third of the screen. It has two buttons: "Accept All" and "Manage Preferences."

He clicks "Manage Preferences." A new screen appears. Forty-seven categories of cookies. "Strictly Necessary." "Performance." "Functionality." "Targeting." "Social Media." Each category expandable. Each expansion revealing vendor names he has never heard of. Each vendor linkable to its own privacy policy.

He starts reading. Twenty minutes later he has reviewed eleven of forty-seven categories. He has toggled off thirty-two vendors. He is not sure what any of them do. The toggles perform control. The number of toggles performs transparency. Forty-seven categories of choice perform informed consent.

He learns that Europeans collectively spend 575 million hours per year clicking cookie banners. Fourteen billion euros in lost productivity. The equivalent of 287,500 full-time employees doing nothing but clicking consent buttons.

The regulation was designed to give users control. The regulation produced a compliance industry. The industry's primary output is fatigue. The fatigue's primary output is "Accept All."

Only 11.8 percent of consent platforms on the top 10,000 UK websites meet minimum legal requirements. The law requires consent. The industry produces clicks. The clicks perform consent. The law is satisfied.

Piece #2175: The Shield

Thomas finds the sentence he's been looking for. Not in a privacy policy. In a legal brief. A company's lawyer, defending against a data breach lawsuit:

"Plaintiff was provided with clear notice of our data practices and affirmatively consented by clicking 'I agree.'"

There it is. The privacy policy is not a disclosure to the user. The privacy policy is a defense against the user. The document does not say "we will protect your data." The document says "we told you what we do." The telling is the permission. The transparency is the shield.

Every element serves this function. The length — 6,938 words — is not a failure of editing. It is a legal necessity. Every practice must be mentioned. Every mention becomes a defense. The longer the policy, the more practices are disclosed. The more practices are disclosed, the more practices are immunized. Length is not the obstacle to reading. Length is the point.

The reading level — college, for an eighth-grade audience — is not a failure of plain language. The precision serves the same legal function. "We may share your information with third-party partners for purposes consistent with legitimate business interests as described herein" is not unclear because the drafter failed. It is unclear because clarity would specify what "legitimate business interests" means, and specification would constrain.

The click is not a failure of informed consent. The click is informed consent's replacement. The click says: I was told. The click does not say: I understood. The law does not require understanding. The law requires the click.

Thomas clicks "I agree."

The shield activates.
