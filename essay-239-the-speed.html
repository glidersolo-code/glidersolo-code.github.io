The Speed

Internet service providers advertise connection speeds in megabits per second. "Up to 1 Gbps." "Up to 300 Mbps." The qualifier "up to" means the maximum achievable under ideal conditions — a single device on Ethernet during off-peak hours with no congestion and optimal server performance. The FCC's 2023 Broadband Consumer Labels rule requires disclosure of "typical" speeds alongside advertised speeds. Typical download speeds are often 50 to 80 percent of the advertised maximum. A plan marketed as "up to 300 Mbps" may typically deliver 150-240 Mbps. The ceiling is the prominent number. The measurement is what the consumer experiences.

The advertised speed is download speed. Upload speed — data from the consumer to the internet — is typically much lower. On cable internet, a 300 Mbps download plan may offer 10-20 Mbps upload: a 15:1 ratio. The marketing emphasizes one direction. Video calls, cloud backups, file sharing, remote work — all depend on upload. The number marketed as "your internet speed" describes the direction that favors content consumption. The direction that favors the consumer's increasingly symmetric use is quieter on the specification sheet. Fiber-optic connections offer symmetric speeds, but cable and DSL — still the majority of American broadband — do not.

Speed is bandwidth — how much data per second. User experience depends on at least four dimensions: bandwidth, latency (delay before data begins arriving), jitter (variation in latency), and packet loss. A 300-Mbps connection with 200-millisecond latency stutters on a video call. A 10-Mbps connection with 20-millisecond latency is smooth. Bandwidth is thirty times higher on the connection that performs worse for the user's actual task. Gaming, video conferencing, real-time collaboration — all latency-sensitive — are served worse by the plan with the higher marketed number. The consumer choosing between ISPs based on advertised Mbps may select the plan with higher bandwidth and worse latency.

The FCC defines broadband through a threshold that determines federal funding eligibility. In 2010: 4 Mbps download, 1 Mbps upload. In 2015: 25/3 Mbps. In 2024: 100/20 Mbps. Each revision reclassified communities: areas "served" under the old definition became "unserved" under the new, making them eligible for infrastructure funding. Areas meeting the old threshold but below modern needs received no support because they remained "served." The threshold is a policy decision experienced as a measurement of service quality. A community with 25 Mbps was broadband-served in 2023 and unserved in 2024. The service did not change. The definition did. The definition determines which communities are visible as needing investment.

One megabit per second (Mbps) is one million bits. One megabyte per second (MBps) is eight million bits. ISPs advertise in megabits. File downloads display progress in megabytes. A consumer on a 100-Mbps plan sees a download rate of approximately 12.5 MBps and may conclude the connection underperforms by a factor of eight. The "M" in both abbreviations looks identical. The "b" and "B" carry an eightfold difference. The notation performs equivalence between units that differ by nearly an order of magnitude. A number marketed as speed, qualified by "up to," describing only download, measured in one dimension of a four-dimensional user experience, defined by a threshold that determines which communities receive infrastructure funding, expressed in units that consumers confuse with an eightfold-different unit — printed on advertisements, experienced as the quality of the connection.
