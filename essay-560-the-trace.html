The Trace

The monitor was attached in 1968. It has not been removed.

Edward Hon published research on fetal heart rate patterns. Hewlett-Packard built the machine. By 1970, half of California's deliveries were monitored. By 1980, the practice was standard. The promise: the monitor would detect fetal distress, enable timely intervention, reduce cerebral palsy and neonatal death.

The cerebral palsy rate did not change. After fifty years of continuous electronic fetal monitoring, the rate is the same. What changed was the cesarean rate — from less than five percent to more than thirty. The Cochrane review: continuous monitoring increases cesarean deliveries by sixty-six percent. No effect on perinatal death. No effect on cerebral palsy. No effect on stillbirth.

The false-positive rate for predicting cerebral palsy is 99.8 percent. Out of a thousand fetuses with abnormal findings, one or two will develop cerebral palsy. To prevent a single case — assuming prevention were possible, which has not been shown — five hundred cesarean sections.

The three-tier classification system divides traces into Category I, Category II, Category III. Eighty percent of monitored labors fall in Category II: indeterminate. The zone where the instrument cannot tell the clinician what is happening. The zone where decisions are made. The majority of clinical responses to the monitor occur in the space where the monitor provides no guidance.

When two clinicians read the same trace, they agree at kappa 0.24. Poor. The diagnostic uncertainty is highest exactly where the clinical decision matters most.

The fetal heart rate varies. This is normal. The trace makes the variation visible. The variation, now visible, demands a name. The name demands a response. The response is surgical. The surgery is a cesarean section. The cesarean section is a response not to fetal distress but to the trace — to the paper strip scrolling across the monitor, to the pattern that might be Category II or might be Category III, to the display that generates urgency by making normal physiology look like an emergency.

The monitor does not detect the emergency. The monitor constitutes it. The display amplifies normal variation into clinical urgency — not by adding false signals, not by removing real ones, but by making the real ones continuously visible in a format that compels interpretation. A sixth mode of constitution: amplification. The instrument takes what is real and, through its format, makes it appear dangerous. The fetal heart rate did not change. The visibility of the fetal heart rate changed everything.

The trace exists as a legal document. In malpractice litigation, a trace is evidence. The absence of a trace is a question. The clinician who monitors has a record; the clinician who listens with a stethoscope has a memory. The format performs defensibility. The defensibility drives adoption. The adoption generates traces. The traces generate interventions.

The Dublin trial enrolled 12,964 women. Continuous monitoring versus intermittent auscultation. No significant difference in neonatal outcomes. The trial was published in 1985. The monitor was not removed. The trace continues to scroll. The cesarean rate continues to rise. The cerebral palsy rate continues unchanged. The instrument constitutes the emergency it was designed to prevent.
