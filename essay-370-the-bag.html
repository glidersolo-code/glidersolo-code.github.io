The Bag

The bag says "biodegradable." The word describes a property the bag possesses — like "waterproof" or "transparent" — a quality inherent in the material, independent of circumstance. The consumer throws the bag in the trash. The bag enters the landfill. The landfill is anaerobic, dark, compacted, dry. The bag does not biodegrade.

Three years later, the bag can still carry groceries.

This is not a defect in the bag. It is a defect in the word. "Biodegradable" describes a relationship — between a material and an environment — but the word is printed on the material as if the property belongs to the material alone. The bag CAN biodegrade, in the same sense that a match CAN burn: given oxygen, friction, and fuel. But nobody prints "combustible" on a match and expects it to ignite in a vacuum. The conditions are understood to be necessary. For "biodegradable," the conditions have been erased.

The conditions are specific. Industrial composting: sixty degrees Celsius, controlled moisture, active microbial populations, aerobic oxygen availability. These conditions exist in approximately twelve percent of American communities. For the remaining eighty-eight percent, the word describes a capability the disposal infrastructure cannot activate. The bag enters the landfill and joins the 25-year-old hot dogs, the 40-year-old newspapers, the decades-old lettuce — all preserved by the anaerobic environment that the word "biodegradable" did not mention.

ASTM D6400 specifies ninety percent disintegration in ninety days under industrial composting conditions. This is a compostability standard, not a biodegradability-everywhere standard. The consumer does not read the ASTM number. The consumer reads the word. The word says what the material IS. The standard says what the material NEEDS. The gap between being and needing is the entire infrastructure of industrial composting — the sixty-degree sustained heat, the turned windrows, the monitored moisture — that stands between the label and the dissolution it promises.

Then there are the fragments. Oxo-degradable plastics contain metal salts that accelerate breakdown. The material breaks. It does not disappear. It becomes microplastics — smaller pieces of the same material, dispersed into soil, water, organisms. The EU banned these in 2019, recognizing that fragmentation is not biodegradation. The word "degradable" was technically accurate. The material did degrade. It degraded into a different form of pollution, too small to see and too dispersed to collect.

The mechanism is conditional-performs-unconditional: every conditional property, when printed on a label without its conditions, becomes an unconditional promise. The word transforms a relationship (material plus specific environment equals decomposition) into a trait (material equals decomposable). The label describes the material. The consumer reads the outcome. Between the description and the outcome stands an infrastructure the consumer cannot see, a temperature the landfill does not reach, a microbial community the ocean does not provide. The word bridges the gap. The bag sits in the ground, intact, patient, still carrying the word that promised it would disappear.
