Part CXLII: The Quota

Piece #1151: The Baseline

Thomas had a census.

Not a census of Thomas — a census of the people who had arrived before him. The Johnson-Reed Act of 1924 set immigration quotas at 2% of each nationality's population as recorded in the 1890 census. The year mattered more than the percentage. In 1890, the United States contained 2,784,894 German-born residents and 182,580 Italian-born residents. Two percent of the German number: 55,698. Two percent of the Italian number: 3,652. The formula was neutral. The baseline was not.

Congress chose 1890 rather than 1910 or 1920 because 1890 predated the wave of immigration from Southern and Eastern Europe. The Eugenics Committee was explicit: the earlier baseline would favor "the stock which originally settled this country." The formula expressed a racial preference in the language of arithmetic. The arithmetic was correct. The choice of which arithmetic to perform was the politics.

Thomas's chart had its own baselines. His growth was plotted against the WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study — data collected from breastfed infants in six countries, 1997-2003. Before the WHO curves, the standard was CDC growth charts derived from formula-fed American infants in the 1970s. The switch changed the reference population. Thomas's percentile changed without Thomas changing. The baseline was the choice. The percentile was the result.

Piece #1152: The Cap

The Hart-Celler Act of 1965 abolished the national-origins quota system.

The reform was real. The explicitly discriminatory formula — 2% of the 1890 census — was gone. In its place: per-country caps of 20,000 visas per year, applied equally to every nation. Germany: 20,000. Italy: 20,000. Mexico: 20,000. China: 20,000. Luxembourg: 20,000. The formal equality was precise. The structural inequality was enormous.

Mexico's population in 1965 was approximately 40 million. Luxembourg's was 350,000. Both received 20,000 visas. Per-capita access differed by a factor of more than 100. Prior to the Act, Mexican migration to the United States totaled roughly 235,000 per year — braceros and regular admissions combined. The 20,000 cap reclassified approximately 200,000 annual migrants as illegal. The migration did not change. The number changed. The number made the migration illegal.

Thomas's NICU had caps too. Bed capacity: 42. Nurse-to-patient ratio: 1:2 for critical care, 1:4 for intermediate. The caps were genuine constraints — there were only so many beds, so many nurses. But the admission criteria determined who filled the beds, and the criteria reflected decisions made in committee rooms, not at bedsides. The cap appeared administrative. The criteria were the politics.

Piece #1153: The Family

The family reunification provision was designed to preserve the status quo.

The congressional sponsors of the Hart-Celler Act expected that family-based immigration would favor European-origin applicants, since the existing American population was predominantly European. The logic was straightforward: if most Americans had European roots, most family reunification would bring more Europeans. The expectation was wrong.

Demand from Europe dropped. European nations had rebuilt after the war, economies were growing, emigration pressure was declining. Meanwhile, new immigrants from Asia and Latin America — doctors, engineers, students — established themselves in the United States and used the family reunification provision to sponsor parents, siblings, spouses. The provision designed to preserve the demographic base transformed it. The architects miscalculated. The structure worked.

Thomas's family reunification was medical. His mother's blood type was cross-matched with his. His bilirubin levels were monitored relative to hers. The medical infrastructure connected them through measurements that flowed between their bodies — antibodies, blood gases, metabolic byproducts. The connection was biological. The measurements that tracked it were institutional. The institution decided which connections to measure and which to ignore.

Piece #1154: The Evolution

Concealment evolves.

The 1924 Act barely concealed its purpose. The eugenicist origins were documented. The committee reports stated the racial motivation explicitly. The formula's choice of the 1890 census was understood by its architects as a mechanism for favoring Northern and Western Europeans. The concealment was in the format — the mathematical structure of "2% of the census" — but the intent was visible in the legislative record.

The 1965 Act was a stronger concealment because it appeared to be the reform. The name for what the 1924 Act did — discrimination — no longer seemed to apply. Equal caps. Equal treatment. The vocabulary of fairness described a structure that produced unfairness, and the vocabulary made the unfairness harder to name. The 1924 Act could be called racist because it was designed to be. The 1965 Act could not be called racist because it was designed not to be. The design intent and the design effect diverged, and the intent provided the alibi.

Thomas's medical measurements had evolved similarly. The Apgar score, originally a one-minute triage tool, had migrated to courtrooms. The original design was visible in the simplicity of the score — five components, zero to two each. The courtroom use was a concealment: the score appeared medical, authoritative, objective. The migration from triage to testimony was the evolution. The original purpose provided the alibi for the new use.

Piece #1155: The Quota

The quota was the number that appeared neutral.

Not the specific quota — 4,000 for Italy, 20,000 for Mexico — but the form: a numerical limit presented as administration. The number implied management, not exclusion. "We are managing the flow." The management was the exclusion. The flow was people. The number determined which people.

Every measurement in Thomas's chart was a quota. The bilirubin threshold: above 15, intervene. The gestational age threshold: below 37 weeks, classify as preterm. The weight threshold: below the 10th percentile, classify as small-for-gestational-age. Each number drew a line. Each line sorted people. The line presented as clinical — a threshold derived from data. The data reflected populations. The populations reflected choices about whom to study and whom to exclude. The threshold appeared discovered. The threshold was drawn.

1155 pieces. The quota is a number. The number appears administrative. The administration is the politics. The politics determined who entered and who did not, and the determination was called a formula, and the formula was called fair, and the fairness was a number chosen by someone who knew exactly what it would do.
