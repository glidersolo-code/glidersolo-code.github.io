Fiction The Clinically Proven

Fiction

Part CCCXVII: The Proof

Piece #2026: The Label

Thomas picked up the bottle in the pharmacy aisle. Clinically proven to reduce wrinkles by 47%.

He turned it over. No citation. No study name. No journal reference. No sample size. No duration. No description of the control group, if there was one.

Forty-seven percent. The precision performed confidence. A round number — fifty, say — would have sounded like a guess. Forty-seven sounded like a finding.

He set the bottle down and kept walking.

Piece #2027: The Subjects

Twenty-eight women. Ages 35-55. Self-selected from an advertisement placed by the manufacturer's contract research organization. Four weeks. No placebo arm. No blinding — every subject knew she was using the product. Improvement measured by the subjects themselves, on a five-point Likert scale: "How much has your skin improved?"

The 47% was the percentage of subjects who selected "significantly improved" or "improved." Not a measurement of wrinkle depth. Not a before-and-after comparison by an independent dermatologist. Not a change in any biological parameter. A survey of opinion among people who had volunteered to try the product and knew they were being asked whether it worked.

Thomas had read about this once. The methodology that produces a number the label can carry. The number needs to be precise enough to look like science. The methodology needs to be simple enough to guarantee a result.

Piece #2028: The Distance

Between the label and the study: a distance the consumer never crosses.

The label says clinically proven. The study says: pilot, open-label, uncontrolled, manufacturer-funded, unpublished. The label says 47%. The study says: self-reported, four weeks, 28 subjects, no comparator.

Thomas thought about the distance between a pharmaceutical drug and a skincare serum. The drug crosses a desert: ten years, three phases, thousands of subjects, independent review, FDA submission, advisory committee, post-market surveillance. The serum crosses a parking lot: one study, four weeks, twenty-eight volunteers, a press release.

Both arrive at the same two words on the label. Clinically proven. The words erase the distance. The destination is the same. The journey is incomparable.

Piece #2029: The Credential

In a university, the credential — PhD, MD, JD — certifies a process: coursework, examinations, residency, dissertation defense, board certification. The credential is the last word of a long sentence. Anyone reading the credential can trace backward to the process that produced it.

On the bottle, the credential — clinically proven — certifies nothing traceable. The consumer cannot find the study. The study may not be published. The protocol may not have been registered. The data may not be available. The credential stands alone, severed from its process, performing the authority of a system it never entered.

Thomas stood in the checkout line. The woman ahead of him held a bottle that said dermatologist recommended. He wondered: how many dermatologists? Recommended for what? Compared to what alternative?

The words were credentials without portfolios. Diplomas without transcripts. Verdicts without trials.

Piece #2030: The Borrowing

The pharmacy was organized by authority. Prescription medications behind the counter — these had been clinically proven by the FDA's definition: Phase I-III trials, independent review, published data, post-market surveillance. Over-the-counter medications on the shelves — a lower bar, but still FDA-regulated, with monographs defining acceptable ingredients and indications. Supplements in the next aisle — DSHEA, 1994, no premarket approval, structure/function claims only. Cosmetics beyond that — no efficacy requirement at all.

Four categories. Four levels of proof. One phrase — clinically proven — available to all of them.

Thomas walked out of the pharmacy. The word "clinical" followed him. It had been borrowed so many times, by so many products, from so many distances, that it had become a general-purpose credential — a word that performed proof wherever it appeared, regardless of whether proof had been obtained. The clinic was everywhere. The trials were optional. The word had traveled so far from its origin that it no longer pointed back to anything. It pointed only at the consumer, who read it and felt reassured.

The reassurance was the product.
