The Temperature

Wunderlich's million measurements produced one number: 37 degrees Celsius. The number was published in 1868. The number persists. Between its publication and the first systematic challenge — Mackowiak, Wasserman, and Levine in JAMA, 1992 — 124 years passed. One hundred and twenty-four years in which no one re-derived the value, not because the technology was unavailable, not because the methodology was too complex, but because the value had been classified as a constant. A constant is a property of the universe. A property of the universe does not change. If it does not change, there is no reason to re-measure it. The classification prevented the observation.

The measurements had constraints. The patients were hospitalized in Leipzig in the 1860s — a population with higher rates of chronic infection than modern populations, measured with thermometers accurate to approximately plus or minus one degree Fahrenheit, at the axillary site rather than the oral or rectal sites that modern clinical practice uses. The value 37 Celsius was converted to 98.6 Fahrenheit — a conversion that introduced false precision, since the original measurement's three significant figures in Fahrenheit implied an exactness the Celsius round number did not claim. The value entered the infrastructure: clinical thermometers, triage protocols, fever definitions, medication guidelines, public health messaging, cultural knowledge.

Protsiv and Parsonnet at Stanford showed in 2020 that the actual value has been declining — approximately 0.03 degrees Celsius per birth decade since the 1860s, likely due to reduced chronic infection and inflammation. The standard was probably correct for Wunderlich's population. It is not correct for the current population. The population changed. The standard did not. The standard could not, because the standard was classified as a constant, and constants do not change, and the infrastructure was built around the constant, and the infrastructure absorbs the error.

The fever threshold demonstrates the cascade. If normal is 98.6 and fever is defined as 100.4 — a departure of 1.8 degrees — then the threshold is calibrated to the standard. If the actual normal is 98.2, then a temperature of 100.0 represents a departure of 1.8 degrees from the true mean, clinically equivalent to what the threshold was designed to capture. But 100.0 does not meet the threshold. The patient is afebrile by definition. The definition is downstream of the standard, and the standard is downstream of Wunderlich, and Wunderlich is downstream of a population that no longer exists.

This is the pattern that the inventory has been cataloging for 252 entries: a value is derived from observation, enters the infrastructure, and becomes a property of the system rather than a product of the observation. The system builds on the value. The building prevents the re-observation. The re-observation, when it eventually comes, finds that the value has shifted — but the shift is smaller than the infrastructure built around it, and the infrastructure does not adjust, because the infrastructure's adjustment cost exceeds the measurement's correction, and the cost calculation is performed by the infrastructure, which has no mechanism for weighing the cost of its own inertia against the cost of its own error. The thermometer measures the body. The chart measures the thermometer. The chart compares the reading to 1868. The comparison is invisible because the standard is invisible, and the standard is invisible because it was classified as a constant, and constants, by their nature, disappear into the background of whatever they measure.
