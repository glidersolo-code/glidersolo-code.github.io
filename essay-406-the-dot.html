The Dot

The blue dot appears on the screen and says: here. Within a detailed map — streets drawn to precise widths, buildings filling exact footprints, parks bounded by their legal edges — the dot marks a position. The user glances down, sees the dot centered on the correct street, and walks. Six and a half billion devices perform this interaction.

The dot does not measure position. The GPS receiver inside the phone measures time — the interval between a satellite's broadcast and the signal's arrival, nanoseconds converted to distances via the speed of light. Four such time measurements, from four satellites, solved as a system of equations, yield three spatial coordinates and a clock correction. The position is a computed output from temporal inputs. The blue dot displays the solution to a math problem, formatted as a point on a map.

The map provides the context. The context performs the precision. Streets on the map come from surveying and satellite photogrammetry — sub-meter accuracy, professionally verified. Buildings occupy correct footprints. Every element of the map background was measured by instruments calibrated to centimeters. The blue dot sits within this precise rendering, and the eye reads the dot as sharing the map's precision. If the street corner is correct to centimeters, the dot on the street corner appears correct to centimeters. But the map comes from one measurement system. The dot comes from another. The map's accuracy: sub-meter. The dot's accuracy: three to five meters in open sky, ten to fifty in an urban canyon, a hundred or more indoors when the phone has silently switched from satellite signals to WiFi positioning.

That silent switch is the second concealment. Inside a building, GPS signals cannot penetrate. The phone transitions to WiFi (which access points are visible, at what signal strength: 15-40 meter accuracy), cell tower triangulation (100-300 meters), inertial dead reckoning (accelerometer counting steps from the last known point, accuracy degrading by the second). No notification marks the transition. The blue dot persists. The map persists. The apparent precision persists. The accuracy has changed by an order of magnitude, and the display has not changed at all.

In the urban canyon — downtown streets flanked by tall buildings — signals bounce off glass and steel before arriving at the antenna. The receiver cannot distinguish a direct signal from a reflected one. Each reflected path adds phantom distance: microseconds of delay that translate to meters of position error. Without correction, horizontal accuracy in deep urban canyons degrades to 38 meters; vertical to 58. The phone's software compensates by snapping the computed position to the nearest road — map-matching, it is called. The honest measurement, which might place the dot in the middle of a building, is overruled by the assumption that the user is on a road. The corrected position looks right. The honest position looks wrong. The correction uses the map to fix the measurement, and the fixed measurement confirms the map's precision, and the circle closes.

Altitude is the dimension the display hides entirely. GPS altitude is measured relative to the WGS84 ellipsoid, a mathematical surface approximating Earth's shape. Actual sea level — the geoid — differs from this ellipsoid by up to 192 meters, depending on location. The consumer device reports a number that performs height above the ground; without geoid correction, the number is height above a mathematical construct that may be tens of meters from the physical surface. The consumer never sees the distinction between ellipsoid and geoid, between mathematical surface and gravitational reality, between the world the equation describes and the world underfoot.

The system began as a weapons project. NAVSTAR, first satellite 1978. Full constellation 1995. From 1990 to 2000, the US Department of Defense deliberately corrupted the civilian signal, introducing time-varying errors of up to 100 meters. On May 1, 2000, the corruption was removed. Civilian accuracy improved tenfold overnight. The dot became useful. The market exploded. The constellation of 31 satellites remains under military operation, maintained at approximately two billion dollars per year, broadcasting a civilian signal provided without guarantee, without service agreement, without legal obligation.

The blue dot says nothing about any of this. The blue dot says: here. The "here" is computed from time, displayed within a context whose precision performs for the measurement, seamlessly maintained across method switches that degrade accuracy by orders of magnitude, dependent on a military constellation that one country operates and could degrade at will. The dot is the interface between all of this complexity and the user, and the interface's job is to make the complexity disappear.
