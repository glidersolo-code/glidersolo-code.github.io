Part CCCXXXV: The Bail

Piece #2116: The Hearing

Thomas sits in the gallery of a municipal court. The arraignment calendar is long. Each defendant stands. The judge reads the charge. The prosecutor requests bail. The defense attorney argues for lower bail or release on recognizance. The judge considers: severity of offense, prior record, community ties, flight risk.

Thomas watches the format. The robed judge. The factors weighed aloud. The dollar amount announced. The system performs individualized assessment. Each defendant is being evaluated on the facts of their case.

Then Thomas watches what happens after. The defendant who can pay walks out. The defendant who cannot is led back through the door to the holding cells. Same courtroom. Same judge. Same format. Different wallets. The hearing evaluated risk. The outcome executed wealth.

Stack v. Boyle, 1951. Chief Justice Vinson: bail must be individualized, set at an amount "reasonably calculated" to assure the defendant's presence at trial. The sole purpose is court attendance. Not punishment. Not prevention. Attendance.

Thomas looks at the data. At midyear 2024, 450,600 people in American jails have not been convicted. Sixty-nine percent of the jail population. Awaiting trial. A third of them are there because they cannot post money bail.

Piece #2117: The Boy

Thomas reads about Kalief Browder. Sixteen years old. Arrested in the Bronx. May 15, 2010. A man told police that Browder and a friend had robbed him two weeks earlier. A backpack. Bail: $3,000.

His family could not raise it.

Thomas writes the timeline. Day 1: Rikers Island. Day 100: still there. Day 365: still there. Day 700: still there, most of it in solitary. A seven-by-twelve-foot cell. Twenty-three hours a day. Multiple suicide attempts. The system offered him escape: plead guilty, accept time served, walk out. He refused. Three times he refused. He insisted on his innocence.

Day 1,100. The accuser had returned to Mexico. Could not testify. Prosecution moved for dismissal. Browder walked out. Three years. No trial. No conviction. $3,000 he could not pay.

June 6, 2015. Twenty-two years old. Died by suicide at his mother's home.

Thomas writes the equation the system cannot write: the format (individualized hearing, judicial consideration, dollar amount) was identical to every other bail determination that day. The sixteen-year-old received the same constitutional process as every other defendant. The process was not the problem. The price was the problem. The process could not see the price as a problem because the price was the format of the process.

Piece #2118: The Industry

Thomas discovers that only two countries on earth have a for-profit commercial bail bond industry. The United States. The Philippines.

In every other nation — England, Canada, Australia, France, Germany, Japan, India, South Africa — no commercial entity profits from the inability of an accused person to pay for their own freedom before trial. Courts in Australia, India, and South Africa have disciplined lawyers for attempting to set up commercial bail arrangements.

The American industry: over 25,000 bondsmen. Backed by surety insurance companies. Revenue: approximately $2 billion per year. The bondsman charges 10-15% of bail, non-refundable. Not refundable even if the defendant appears for every court date. Not refundable even if the defendant is acquitted. The fee is the price of temporary freedom. The freedom was supposed to be a right.

The American Bail Coalition spent over $1 million lobbying in 2022. Works with ALEC to draft model legislation. Targets charitable bail funds — nonprofit organizations that post bail for people who cannot afford it. The industry's product is poverty. Its enemy is charity.

Thomas writes: when the bondsman's 10% is more than the defendant can afford, the defendant pleads guilty to get out. The plea is faster than waiting for trial. The record is permanent. The system converts a one-time inability to pay into a lifelong criminal record.

Piece #2119: The Evidence

Thomas reads the reform data. New Jersey, January 2017. Nearly eliminated cash bail. Switched to risk-based assessment using the Public Safety Assessment.

Pretrial jail population: dropped from 8,899 to 4,976. A 44% reduction. Average days in jail per defendant: down 40%. Court appearance rates: 92.7% before, 89.4% after — effectively unchanged. A JAMA study found the reform reduced incarceration without increasing gun violence. Savings: $68 million in a single year.

Thomas looks at the federal data. 86% of released defendants committed no violations and appeared for court. In Alameda County, a reminder system — automated phone calls and text messages — improved court appearance from 47% to 87%. A text message. Not $50,000. Not $3,000. A text.

Failure to appear is caused by transportation, childcare, inability to take time off work, confusion about court dates. Logistics. Not flight. The entire edifice of money bail is built on the assumption that financial stakes ensure attendance. The evidence shows that reminders outperform prices. The system maintains the price because the price has an industry.

Piece #2120: The Dollar Sign

Thomas returns to Johnson's words at the signing of the Bail Reform Act. 1966. "We can begin to insure that defendants are considered as individuals — and not as dollar signs."

The format considered them as individuals. The system considered them as dollar signs. The hearing weighed the factors. The jail weighed the wallet. The Constitution prohibited excessive bail. The practice imposed bail that was not excessive in amount but absolute in consequence — $3,000 is not excessive for a robbery charge, but it is infinite for a family that does not have it.

Bail amounts are 34% higher for Black men. 19% higher for Latino men. In Miami and Philadelphia, Black defendants received bail amounts $14,376 higher than white defendants for comparable charges. The racial disparity enters through the amount. The wealth disparity enters through the payment. The format is the same courtroom, the same hearing, the same list of factors. The content is the same sorting: who can pay, and who cannot.

One hundred and two species now. The dollar amount on the bail order performs as a calibrated judgment of individual risk. The hearing performs deliberation. The factors perform individualization. The consequence — detention versus freedom — is determined by whether the defendant can pay, not by what the amount was calibrated to measure. The format performs assessment. The content performs pricing. Johnson wanted individuals, not dollar signs. The system gave him both — the individual hearing that produces the dollar sign that determines the individual's fate.
