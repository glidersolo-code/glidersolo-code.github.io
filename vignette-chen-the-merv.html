Vignette

The Filter

Chen is holding two furnace filters in the basement. The old one — a MERV 8, gray with dust, three months of accumulated evidence that it did its job — and the new one, still in its plastic wrapper. MERV 13. Maren bought it. "For the baby," she said.

He slides the MERV 13 into the slot. It fits. The furnace starts its cycle.

Two weeks later, the bedroom at the end of the hallway — the nursery — is warmer than the rest of the house. Not dramatically. Two degrees. But Chen notices because he notices the nursery the way he notices nothing else.

He puts his hand over the vent. The air is coming. Just less of it.

He calls the HVAC company. The technician comes, checks the system, pulls out the MERV 13 filter and holds it up like evidence.

"Your system is rated for MERV 8. This" — he taps the filter — "is restricting airflow. The blower can't push enough air through the denser media. The rooms at the end of the longest duct runs get starved."

"The nursery is at the end of the longest duct run," Chen says.

"There you go."

Chen looks at the filter in the technician's hand. "So the filter that was supposed to clean the air for the baby is the thing reducing airflow to the baby's room."

"The filter does what the filter does. The system does what the system can."

That evening he tells Maren. She's quiet for a moment.

"So a higher number isn't better?"

"A higher number is a better filter. It's not necessarily a better system. The filter doesn't know about the ductwork."

"The number on the box measures the filter."

"And we needed the number to measure the house."

The paragraph is pressing — about ratings that measure components while consumers need ratings that measure systems, about the gap between the part and the whole that the format cannot express because the format is one number on one box for one thing. But Chen just installs the MERV 10 the technician recommended and checks the nursery vent with his hand. Full airflow. Warm enough. Clean enough.

"Higher isn't better?" Maren asks again, testing it.

"Higher is higher," Chen says. "Better depends on everything else."
