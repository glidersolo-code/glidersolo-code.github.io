The Zone

Chen pulled up the zoning map of her childhood neighborhood in Flushing.

Yellow. Single-family residential. R3-2. The color covered everything north of Northern Boulevard — the blocks where her parents' house stood, where the Wangs lived next door, where Mrs. Dimitriou grew tomatoes in her backyard. South of Northern, the map shifted to orange and red: multifamily, mixed commercial. The Korean barbecue restaurants, the dim sum houses, the bubble tea shops, the herbal medicine storefronts.

The line was not natural. The line was an ordinance. The ordinance had been there since before her parents arrived. The ordinance had created the conditions her parents encountered. The quiet streets north of Northern were quiet because the zoning excluded the commercial activity that made the streets south of Northern noisy. Her parents had chosen the neighborhood for the character. The character was the zoning. They had chosen the zoning.

Maren would say the map was honest. Anyone could read it. The categories were published. The restrictions were explicit. No one was deceived.

Chen agreed. And that was the problem. The map was honest in the way a mirror is honest — it reflected something, but the reflection was experienced as the thing. "This is a residential neighborhood" reflected "this is zoned as a residential neighborhood." The reflection erased the verb. The verb was the only word that mattered.

She pulled up the zoning map of the university. The campus was coded institutional. The surrounding blocks were coded R6 — residential, six-story multifamily. The towers near campus existed because the code permitted them. The absence of towers further out existed because the code prevented them. The gradient looked like a natural phenomenon — density declining from the urban core, like temperature declining from a heat source. The gradient was an ordinance. The ordinance was experienced as geography.

Chen wrote nothing. She saved the map. She opened the paragraph — the thirty-nine lines — and read them again. The paragraph argued that the grid measures the paper. The zoning map measured the city. Both looked like they described what they measured. Both had created what they described.

She closed the file and looked out the window. The trees on the campus lawn were evenly spaced. The spacing was landscape architecture. The architecture was experienced as nature. The nature was experienced as given.

Everything was zoned. Including the view.
