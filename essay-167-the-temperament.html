The Temperament

Essay 167

The Pythagorean comma is the oldest unsolvable problem in Western music. Twelve perfect fifths should equal seven octaves. They do not. (3/2)^12 gives approximately 129.746; 2^7 gives 128. The discrepancy — a ratio of about 1.01364, or 23.46 cents — arises from the incompatibility of the prime numbers 2 and 3 in the harmonic series. No tuning system resolves it. The comma cannot be eliminated. It can only be distributed.

Equal temperament distributes it equally. Each of the twelve semitones receives the ratio 2^(1/12), and every interval except the octave deviates from its pure harmonic value. A tempered fifth is approximately two cents flat. A tempered major third is approximately fourteen cents sharp — audible, under controlled conditions, to a trained ear. The compromise was adopted for a specific practical reason: it makes every key interchangeable. A piece in C major sounds the same as the same piece transposed to F-sharp major. Any key is accessible. Any transposition is seamless. The price is that no interval is pure.

Before equal temperament became standard, well temperaments offered a different bargain. Andreas Werckmeister, Johann Philipp Kirnberger, Francesco Vallotti — each designed systems in which all keys were playable but each key had its own color. C major was warm. F-sharp major was austere. The character of a key was not an accident; it was a compositional resource. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier (1722) was written for such a system — forty-eight preludes and fugues demonstrating that every key could function, while celebrating the fact that they did not function identically. The well-tempered world was one of accessible variety. The equally tempered world that followed was one of accessible uniformity.

The deeper concealment is not in the compromise but in the ear. Generations of listeners trained on equally tempered instruments — pianos, guitars, the vast majority of recorded music — have internalized the tempered intervals as the correct intervals. A pure major third, produced by a cello's unfretted string or a barbershop quartet's vocal blend, can sound flat to an ear calibrated by piano. The standard has colonized the sensory apparatus. The compromise sounds right. The harmonic series — the physical foundation from which the compromise was derived — sounds wrong. This is a specific kind of concealment: not a proxy substituted for a target, not an old calibration locked in place, but a standard that has shaped the perception against which it is evaluated. The measuring instrument has tuned the ear that judges the measuring instrument. The loop closes, and inside the loop, the compromise is inaudible.

The infrastructure confirms the loop. MIDI encodes twelve-tone equal temperament as the default pitch system. Electronic tuners measure deviation from equal-tempered targets. Piano tuning pins are set to equal-tempered frequencies. Guitar frets are spaced for equal-tempered intervals. The recorded archive — every digitally distributed piece of music — preserves equally tempered performances as the reference standard. Musicians who play unfretted instruments can deviate toward just intonation. Singers can tune their thirds pure. String quartets can shade a leading tone sharp. They do, routinely, in the spaces between the infrastructure's fixed points. But the fixed points are the infrastructure's, and the infrastructure defines what the tuner's needle reports, what the MIDI file encodes, and what the casual listener's ear has been trained to expect. The compromise that was adopted to make keyboards flexible became the assumed structure of music itself, and the distance between the assumption and the harmonic series — two cents in the fifth, fourteen in the third — falls below the threshold of attention that the assumption itself established.
