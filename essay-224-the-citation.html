The Citation

The Journal Impact Factor is the mean number of citations received in a given year by articles published in a journal during the two preceding years. Eugene Garfield proposed it in 1972, building on his 1955 citation index concept. The Institute for Scientific Information published the first Journal Citation Reports in 1975. The metric was designed for librarians — a tool for collection development, for deciding which journals a library should subscribe to and which it could cancel.

The two-year window is calibrated to biomedical literature. In molecular biology, papers accrue most of their citations within two years. In mathematics, the citation half-life exceeds eight years. In engineering, it ranges from three to six. The window was not presented as a design choice. It was published as "the impact factor," as though the temporal frame were intrinsic rather than selected. The number produced by a two-year window in a field with a ten-year citation curve systematically undercounts the influence of work in that field. The undercounting is not random. It is structural — built into the window, invisible in the number.

The distribution within a journal is skewed. Per Seglen's 1997 analysis in the British Medical Journal, the top 15 percent of a journal's articles typically account for 50 percent of its citations. Larivière and colleagues confirmed in 2016 that in most journals, the majority of papers receive fewer citations than the impact factor. The impact factor is a mean of a heavily right-skewed distribution. The mean does not describe the typical paper. It describes the tail. A paper published in a journal with an impact factor of 30 is attributed a kind of reflected quality — the journal's average — regardless of the paper's own citation count. The journal-level statistic migrates to the individual paper. The ecological fallacy is the mechanism: a group property is attributed to a member of the group.

Garfield designed the metric for journals. By the 1990s, it was being used for people. Universities in China, Italy, Spain, and Brazil incorporated journal impact factors into hiring criteria, promotion standards, and cash-per-paper bonus schemes. The metric migrated from collection development to career evaluation without any validation for that purpose. Garfield himself stated in 2005 that individual papers should be assessed on their own citation counts, not their journal's average. The objection was noted and disregarded. The metric was already embedded in evaluation infrastructure — in tenure dossier templates, grant application forms, departmental ranking spreadsheets. A tool for librarians had become a tool for personnel committees, and the personnel committees had no other number.

The San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment — DORA — was drafted in 2012. Over 2,500 organizations have signed. The declaration explicitly recommends against using the impact factor in hiring or promotion. Yet a 2019 study found that 40 percent of researchers still reported the journal impact factor as the most important factor in their submission decisions. The metric persists not because it is defended on its merits — its defenders have become rare — but because the alternative is judgment, and judgment is slower than a number. A two-decimal-place average of a skewed distribution within an arbitrary time window, designed for one purpose and deployed for another, privately computed and commercially sold, applied to individuals when it describes populations, surviving formal declarations of its inadequacy because the spreadsheet needs a column and the column needs a number.
