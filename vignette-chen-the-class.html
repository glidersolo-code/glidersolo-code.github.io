The Class

Chen is looking at a college brochure his niece left on the counter. He reads aloud: "Average class size: nineteen."

Maren is washing dishes. "That's small."

"That's the institutional average. They have four hundred classes. They add up all the class sizes and divide by four hundred. Nineteen."

"And?"

"And if you ask each student how large their classes are and average their answers, you get something closer to eighty."

Maren turns off the water. "How."

"One lecture of six hundred. Three hundred seminars of eight. The institutional average counts classes: six hundred plus three hundred times eight, divided by three hundred and one. About twenty-seven hundred divided by three hundred one. About nine. Actually — let me think of a cleaner example. Say ten classes of ten and one class of five hundred. Eleven classes. Total students: six hundred. Institutional average: six hundred divided by eleven. About fifty-five."

"Okay."

"Now ask each student. One hundred students say 'my class is ten.' Five hundred students say 'my class is five hundred.' Average of their answers: one hundred times ten plus five hundred times five hundred, divided by six hundred. That's two hundred fifty-one thousand divided by six hundred. About four hundred eighteen."

"The institutional average is fifty-five. The student average is four hundred eighteen."

"Same semester. Same classes. Same enrollment. Different question."

Maren dries her hands. "The brochure answers the institution's question."

"The reader hears the student's question. 'How large will my classes be?' The number says fifty-five. Their experience says four hundred eighteen. The gap is not an error. It's structural. Whenever items vary in size, more individuals are contained in larger items. The experience-weighted average always exceeds the item-weighted average. Always. The only way to make them equal is to make every class the same size."

"Does the brochure say which average it's reporting?"

"The brochure says 'average class size.' No qualifier. The word 'average' does not specify the perspective. The reader supplies the perspective — their own. The number was computed from the institution's."

Maren picks up the brochure. Puts it down. "Tell your niece to look at the enrollment caps."

"I will. The caps tell you the size of the room. The average tells you the size of the catalog."
