The Date

EXP 05/2025. Two letters, a month, a year. The format performs a verdict. After this date: discard. The medication bottle in the bathroom cabinet sits with its sentence printed on the label, counting down to the day the chemistry fails, the potency evaporates, the pill crosses from therapeutic to inert.

The date does not describe the chemistry. The date describes the testing. After September 27, 1979, the FDA required all medications to carry an expiration date — the point beyond which the manufacturer has not verified that the drug retains at least 90% of its labeled potency. The manufacturer tests stability for one to three years. The testing stops. The date is stamped. The date marks where the manufacturer's data ends, not where the molecule degrades.

When someone does test longer, the medications last. The Department of Defense and the FDA established the Shelf Life Extension Program in 1986 to determine whether military stockpiles could be kept past their labeled dates. Of 122 drug products — 3,005 lots — 88% were extended at least one year. The average extension: five and a half years. Some lots were extended more than twenty years. The same ibuprofen with a two-year expiration on the pharmacy shelf carries a seven-year extension in a military warehouse, tested by the same FDA that wrote the rule.

In 2012, Lee Cantrell tested medications found in a sealed pharmacy closet. Some predated the moon landing. Thirty to forty years expired. Of fourteen compounds tested, twelve retained at least 90% of their labeled potency. The pills that should have been discarded decades ago were as potent as the day they were made.

The gap between the labeled date and the actual shelf life is not hidden. The SLEP data is published. The Cantrell findings are in the Archives of Internal Medicine. The gap is documented, quantified, peer-reviewed. The date persists. The consumer discards the medication on the labeled day. The pharmacist cannot legally dispense past the date. The date has regulatory force that the science behind the date does not support but cannot overrule.

The 90% threshold itself conceals. A 500-milligram tablet at 89% potency contains 445 milligrams. The therapeutic difference between 445 and 500 milligrams of acetaminophen is clinically undetectable. The threshold that defines "expired" does not correspond to a clinically meaningful change. The number performs pharmacological significance. The number describes a quality-assurance convention.

The structural property here is not one of the ten. Not transparency, not monotonicity, not coordination. This is something older: the test duration performing as a property of the tested thing. The manufacturer chose to test for two years. The bottle says the drug lasts two years. The "two years" describes the manufacturer's budget for stability data, not the molecule's biological timeline. The date on the bottle appears to describe when the drug fails. The date describes when the investigation stopped. The consumer reads the end of science. The consumer is reading the end of a spreadsheet.

The military knows. The FDA knows. The pharmacy cannot act on what they know. The patient discards. The prescription is refilled. The manufacturer sells a new bottle of the same compound with a new date — a date that will, once again, mark not the moment the drug degrades but the moment the testing concluded. The date is accurate. The date is not what it appears. The accuracy is the concealment.
