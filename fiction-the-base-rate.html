Part CDLXXIX: The Base

Piece #2836: The Cab

Thomas showed the problem.

A city has 85% Green cabs and 15% Blue cabs. A witness to a hit-and-run identifies the cab as Blue. The witness is 80% accurate.

"What is the probability the cab was Blue?"

"Eighty percent," said every participant.

Thomas wrote the arithmetic on the board. Of 100 cabs, 85 are Green and 15 are Blue. The witness, at 80% accuracy, will call 12 of the 15 Blue cabs correctly Blue. The witness will also misidentify 17 of the 85 Green cabs as Blue. Total cabs the witness calls Blue: 29. Of those, 12 are actually Blue. Probability: 41%.

Every participant had answered 80%. Every participant had ignored the base rate. Every participant had treated the witness's accuracy as the probability. The witness's accuracy is not the probability. The probability requires the population from which the cab was drawn. The population is 85% Green. The population makes the 80% accuracy produce a 41% posterior.

The base rate was not hidden. The base rate was stated in the problem. The base rate was ignored because the witness was vivid and the population was abstract. The specific displaced the general. The case displaced the category.

Piece #2837: The Test

Thomas handed the physician a positive test result.

The test was 99% accurate. The disease affected 1 in 1,000 people. The patient had tested positive.

"What is the probability the patient has the disease?" Thomas asked.

"Ninety-nine percent," the physician said. "The test is very accurate."

Thomas drew the population. One thousand patients. One has the disease. That one tests positive — true positive. Of the 999 who do not have the disease, the 1% false positive rate catches approximately 10. Total positive tests: 11. Of 11 positive tests, 1 has the disease. Probability: 9%.

The physician stared.

"But the test is 99% accurate."

"The test is 99% accurate. The probability is 9%. The accuracy and the probability are different numbers. The gap between them is the base rate. The disease is rare. The false positives outnumber the true positives because the base rate is low."

The physician had prescribed treatment based on the 99%. The treatment had side effects. The patient, who had a 91% chance of being healthy, received treatment for a disease they probably did not have — because the physician's cognition processed the vivid number (99% accuracy) and discarded the pale number (1 in 1,000 prevalence).

Piece #2838: The Defendant

Thomas sat in the courtroom.

A forensic expert testified: "The DNA match probability is one in ten million." The jury heard: the defendant's DNA matches. The probability of a random match is one in ten million. The defendant must be guilty.

Thomas ran the numbers. The city has three million residents. At a match probability of one in ten million, the expected number of matching profiles in a population of three million is 0.3. One matching profile was found — the defendant. The DNA evidence is strong.

But in a database search of the city's criminal database — say, 300,000 profiles — the expected number of adventitious matches is 0.03. Still strong.

Now change the scenario. A cold-case database search across the national database of 20 million profiles. Expected adventitious matches: 2. The defendant is one of three people whose DNA matches. The one-in-ten-million statistic still sounds overwhelming. The one-in-ten-million, combined with a database of 20 million, produces a posterior probability far weaker than the jury's intuition.

The jury heard "one in ten million" and translated it to certainty. The jury did not hear the base rate — the size of the database searched. The base rate determines the posterior. The impressive statistic captures the jury's cognition. The base rate is never mentioned.

Piece #2839: The Screening

Thomas considered mass screening.

A country decides to screen its entire population for a rare condition. The condition affects 0.01% of the population — 1 in 10,000. The test is 95% accurate with a 5% false positive rate.

Population: 10 million. People with the condition: 1,000. True positives: 950. People without the condition: 9,999,000. False positives: 499,950. Total positive tests: 500,900.

Of 500,900 positive results, 950 are true positives. The positive predictive value: 0.19%. Fewer than 1 in 500 positive results indicates actual disease.

The headlines would say: test identifies carriers with 95% accuracy. The reality: 99.8% of positive results are false. The test is accurate. The screening is useless. The gap is the base rate. And the base rate is never in the headline.

Thomas looked at the 499,950 people who would receive false positive results. Each would experience anxiety, follow-up testing, potential unnecessary treatment, and lasting psychological effects — because a 95% accurate test, applied to a population where 0.01% have the condition, produces a flood of false positives that drowns the true positives.

The test is 95% accurate. The reader hears 95%. The reader does not hear the base rate.

Piece #2840: The Three Readers

Thomas completed the portrait of the reader.

The anchored reader: starts at the wrong number because the first number encountered captures the estimate. Insufficient adjustment. The reader's number is the anchor's number.

The confirming reader: selects evidence that supports prior beliefs. Asymmetric scrutiny. The reader's evaluation is the belief's evaluation.

The base-rate-blind reader: ignores prior probabilities because the vivid evidence captures all cognitive attention. The reader treats the conditional probability as the posterior probability.

Three architectures in the reader. Three ways the reader's cognition introduces invisible structure that the number never contained. Magnitude (anchor). Direction (confirmation). Probability (base rate).

Together: a reader who starts at the wrong number, selects confirming evidence, and miscalculates the probability. A reader whose judgment is wrong in magnitude, direction, and likelihood. A reader who describes the judgment as careful, evidence-based, and independent.

Thomas listed all five levels. Inside the number: nine mechanisms. Between numbers: one. Across the dataset: two. In the feedback loop: two. In the reader: three.

Seventeen mechanisms. Five levels. The complete circuit — from the number's construction to the reader's cognition. The invisible architecture of numbers is a hierarchy, and every level is invisible from every other level.

The number arrives. The reader reads. What is hidden stays hidden — because the concealment is in the architecture, not in the content. And the reader, at the top of the hierarchy, cannot see the reader.
