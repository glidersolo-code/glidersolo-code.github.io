Part CXXII: The Product

Piece #1051: The Report

Thomas's care had a cost.

The cost was a number. The number was compiled — added to other numbers, aggregated with other costs, summed across other patients, totaled across other hospitals, reported to other agencies, and finally absorbed into a figure called Gross Domestic Product: the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given year.

Simon Kuznets built the figure. In 1934, at the request of the United States Congress, Kuznets — a Russian-born economist at the National Bureau of Economic Research — published National Income, 1929-1932. The report was an accounting tool. It counted what the economy produced. It measured market transactions: goods sold, services rendered, wages paid, profits earned. The figure was a sum. The sum was GDP.

Kuznets warned Congress in the report's introduction: "The welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income." The warning was printed. The warning was read. The warning was filed. The figure was adopted as the primary measure of national welfare, economic progress, and governmental success — exactly what Kuznets said it could not measure. The warning was in the document. The adoption contradicted the warning. The document and its contradiction were both in the archive.

Thomas's care cost money. The money entered GDP. GDP was reported as the measure of the nation's progress. Thomas — a medically complex infant whose care was expensive — contributed to national progress exactly in proportion to his cost. A sicker Thomas contributed more. A healthier Thomas contributed less. The metric measured production, not welfare, and the production of Thomas's care was the consumption of resources that his illness required.

Piece #1052: The Exclusion

GDP counted transactions.

It did not count what was not transacted. Thomas's mother Helen worked no paid job during his NICU stay. She was present — holding him during kangaroo care, learning his feeding cues, reading his monitors, asking questions of his nurses, advocating for his treatment decisions. Her presence was not a transaction. GDP did not count it.

The nurse who stayed late — unpaid overtime, charting after the shift ended — was counted (the salary was a transaction) but the overtime was not (no additional payment, no additional GDP). The resident who read Thomas's case on the bus, preparing for the next morning's rounds, was not counted. The knowledge transfer — nurse to nurse at shift change, attending to fellow at morning report — was not counted.

GDP counted what was purchased. An oil spill increased GDP: cleanup crews were hired, equipment was rented, lawsuits were filed. A parent reading to a child at bedtime did not increase GDP. A forest standing was not counted. A forest logged was counted. The system measured market activity and was adopted as the measure of well-being, and the gap between what it measured and what it was used to represent was the space in which Kuznets's warning disappeared.

Thomas's well-being was not his cost. His cost was in GDP. His well-being was in Helen's arms at 3 AM, in the nurse's voice saying his name, in the developmental milestone that no billing code captured. The metric ran parallel to the patient, counting everything that could be priced and missing everything that could not, and the national report cited the metric and called it progress.

Piece #1053: The Migration

The figure migrated.

Kuznets designed GDP as a production accounting tool — a way to measure what the economy produced so that policymakers could manage production. Richard Stone, in the 1940s in Britain, refined the System of National Accounts — the framework that standardized GDP calculation across countries. The standardization was administrative. It enabled comparison: country to country, year to year. The figure became the primary statistic of international comparison — the number by which nations were ranked, classified, and assessed.

The migration was from accounting to assessment. From "how much does the economy produce?" to "how well is the country doing?" The question changed. The figure did not. GDP still measured production. But it was now used to answer a question about well-being that production data could not answer, and the inadequacy of the answer was acknowledged in academic papers and ignored in headlines.

Thomas's Apgar score had migrated too — from one-minute triage tool to courtroom evidence of birth injury. The GCS had migrated from serial neurological assessment to single-point triage score. Each migration carried the tool beyond its design context, and each tool continued to measure what it was designed to measure while being used to answer questions it was not designed to answer.

GDP per capita was the figure that ranked nations. Stiglitz, Sen, and Fitoussi — the Sarkozy Commission, 2010 — argued that GDP measured production, not welfare, and proposed alternative indicators: inequality-adjusted income, health outcomes, environmental sustainability, subjective well-being. The alternatives were published. GDP remained the headline number. The infrastructure — international comparison frameworks, IMF lending criteria, credit ratings, bond markets — required a single number, and the single number was GDP, and the number measured production, and the production was not the welfare, and the welfare was not the number.

Piece #1054: The Growth

Growth was the derivative.

Not GDP itself but its rate of change — GDP growth, reported quarterly, parsed by analysts, debated by politicians, used to define recession (two consecutive quarters of negative growth). The derivative became more important than the function. What the economy produced mattered less than whether it produced more than last quarter.

The derivative had a consequence: anything that increased GDP was good. Anything that decreased GDP was bad. Environmental regulation that reduced factory output was bad (lower GDP). Environmental disaster that required cleanup was ambiguous (lower well-being, higher GDP). Healthcare spending that rose because more people were sick was good (higher GDP). Healthcare spending that fell because fewer people were sick was bad (lower GDP).

Thomas's growth was also a derivative — his weight plotted on a curve, the slope of the curve more scrutinized than the position. Was he growing? At what rate? The rate was compared to reference curves derived from populations unlike him. A positive slope was good. A negative slope triggered intervention. The derivative — the rate of change — displaced the position, and the position displaced the patient, and the patient was a slope on a chart that measured weight gain the way GDP measured production: accurately, narrowly, and as if accuracy and narrowness were the same as completeness.

Piece #1055: The Product

What was the product?

The Gross Domestic Product of Thomas's care: his medications, his monitoring equipment, his bed-days, his procedures, his specialists' consultations, his imaging studies, his lab draws. Each was a transaction. Each entered the sum. The sum entered the national figure. The national figure was cited in the State of the Union address, in the newspaper headline, in the credit rating agency's report. The figure measured what the economy produced. The economy produced Thomas's care. Thomas's care was a product.

Thomas was not a product. Thomas was a patient — a person whose existence generated transactions that entered a sum that was cited as evidence of national well-being. The distance between Thomas and the figure that absorbed his cost was the distance between a person and a metric — the distance at which context-migration operates, carrying the tool beyond its design, extending the figure beyond its meaning, and filing the resulting number in a system that cannot distinguish between the cost of illness and the cost of prosperity.

Kuznets's warning was in the 1934 report. The warning was true. The figure was adopted. The adoption contradicted the warning. The contradiction was the infrastructure. Thomas was in the infrastructure — counted, summed, reported, and cited — and the infrastructure called the sum progress, and the progress was the product, and the product was 1055 pieces long and still being measured by the figure that Kuznets said could not measure it.
