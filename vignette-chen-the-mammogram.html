Vignette Chen The Mammogram

Vignette

Maren said: My mother had a mammogram in 2011. They found DCIS.

Chen set down his pen.

Maren said: Low-grade. Contained. Not invasive. Not spreading. The doctor said "stage zero cancer." My mother heard "cancer." The word entered the house and did not leave.

Chen said: Treatment.

Maren said: Lumpectomy. Radiation. Five years of tamoxifen. Follow-up mammograms every six months for two years, then annually. The tamoxifen gave her hot flashes so severe she couldn't sleep. The radiation burned her skin. The follow-up appointments generated anxiety that peaked three days before each screening and lasted a week after.

Chen said: And the DCIS—

Maren said: Low-grade. Nine percent ten-year progression risk. That's the number they didn't tell her. Nine percent. Ninety-one percent chance it would never have become invasive cancer. Ninety-one percent chance she would have lived her entire life without knowing those cells existed, without the word "cancer" in her medical record, without the scar, the tamoxifen, the hot flashes, the anxiety, the follow-up appointments, the identity.

Chen said: She was overdiagnosed.

Maren said: She was generated. Before the mammogram, she was a woman with ductal cells that were biologically abnormal and clinically irrelevant. After the mammogram, she was a cancer patient. The mammogram did not discover the cancer. The mammogram constituted the patient.

Chen looked at the paragraph. Thirty-nine lines. He thought of Maren's mother sitting in a gown in a room, waiting for results, and the results constituting a life she would not have lived without the instrument — not a shorter life, not a sicker life, but a life organized around a word that the instrument produced and her body would never have spoken.

Maren said: She's fine. She'll tell you the mammogram saved her life. She believes it. The scar is evidence. The treatment is evidence. The survival is evidence. Every piece of evidence was generated by the instrument. The instrument created the disease, the patient, the treatment, and the evidence that the treatment worked. A self-confirming loop. The mammogram is its own proof.

Chen wrote nothing. The seventh mode was the quietest. The others were dramatic — the inversion of the custody evaluation, the activation of the rape kit, the amplification of the fetal monitor. Generation was silent. A woman went for a screening. A machine found something. A word was spoken. A life changed. The woman was grateful. That was the concealment: gratitude for a rescue from a danger the rescue had created.
