The Aggregate

The number beside the name is 4.2 stars. The customer reads it as a property of the restaurant the way a temperature is a property of the room. Present tense. Current condition. The number performs now.

The 4.2 contains 2017. It contains a review written by someone who ate there under a different chef, a different menu, a different owner. It contains last Tuesday. The number averages these into a single present-tense digit. The direction — whether the restaurant is improving or declining — is dissolved in the average. A place that was excellent for three years and terrible for six months might show 4.1. A place that was terrible for three years and excellent for six months might also show 4.1. The numbers are the same. The trajectories are opposite. The format cannot tell them apart.

The inputs are not randomly sampled. The first review a business receives averages 4.1 stars. The twentieth averages 3.69. Those who bother to write tend to have had extreme experiences — very good or very bad. The resulting distribution is bimodal: many fives, many ones, few threes. The average is computed as though the data were normally distributed. The number performs a central tendency. The data does not have one.

Then there is memory. The reviewer ate the meal on Saturday and wrote the review on Wednesday. The positive aspects survive the gap more vividly than the neutral ones. The review measures the memory of the meal, not the meal. The star performs the dinner. The star is the dinner as remembered five days later, edited by the narrative the reviewer told herself on the drive home.

Then there are the reviews that were never real. Estimates range from 10 to 30 percent. Platforms filter some — Yelp's algorithm suppresses roughly a quarter of submitted reviews, favoring established users over new ones. The visible number is the average of what passed a proprietary filter. The filter's criteria are undisclosed. The number performs transparency atop an opaque selection process.

A half-star increase correlates with a 5 to 9 percent increase in revenue. The number moves money. The money does not know that the number contains 2017, that the distribution is bimodal, that a quarter of the inputs were filtered by an undisclosed algorithm, that some fraction of the survivors were fabricated, that the reviews measure memory rather than experience. The money knows the number. The number is sufficient.

The format erases time the way the restaurant grade erases the inspector, the hospital star erases the methodology, the ESG rating erases the disagreement. Each format dissolves a different substance into the same residue: a number, a letter, a star. The universal solvent of measurement is format. It dissolves everything. What precipitates is always the same: a single, portable, displayable digit. The digit travels. What it dissolved does not.
