The Measure

Essay 154

Soil pH is the most commonly measured chemical property of soil. It determines nutrient availability, microbial activity, plant growth, and soil classification. It has been measured since the 1920s. The method is simple: insert a calibrated glass electrode into a soil suspension and read the number. The number wears a unit that every scientist, farmer, and regulator understands. Below 7 is acidic. Above 7 is alkaline. The precision implied by the reporting — pH 5.83, say — suggests a property as definite as temperature.

Two 2024 papers reveal that soil pH is not a single property. It is a family of protocol-dependent values, and the protocols differ enough to render the world's largest soil databases internally incoherent.

Luke Mosley and colleagues at the University of Adelaide reviewed the World Soil Information System (WoSIS) — 655,336 soil pH measurements spanning every continent — for the European Journal of Soil Science. The database merges measurements from different countries using different suspension media. The United States measures pH in deionized water. Australia uses 0.01M calcium chloride. Brazil and Russia use 1M potassium chloride. The same soil gives systematically different pH values depending on which liquid it is mixed with. Water-pH reads 0.5 to 0.7 units higher than CaCl₂-pH, which reads higher than KCl-pH. The relationship is nonlinear — the offset varies by soil type, organic content, and the pH value itself. A value of 5.8 in water and 5.8 in CaCl₂ describe soils approximately 0.6 units apart: one moderately acidic, the other approaching neutral. But in the database, they are the same number in the same column.

Sebastian Vogel and colleagues at the Leibniz Institute for Agricultural Engineering (ATB, Potsdam), Eberswalde University, and the University of Potsdam tested ion-selective electrodes directly in soil at varying moisture levels for SOIL. Below 11% soil moisture, pH readings become unreliable. The error increases exponentially as moisture decreases. At very low moisture, readings drift by up to 1.5 units. The mechanism is chemical: protons must be dissolved in water for the electrode to measure hydrogen ion activity. In dry soil, protons are bound to clay exchange sites. The electrode reads the pH of whatever thin film of moisture exists at the contact point — a value that depends on the geometry of the probe tip against the grain surface. The instrument needs the medium it's measuring to be in a measurable state. When the medium is below the threshold, the instrument reports a number anyway. The number goes into the record.

The concealment mechanism here is protocol-determines-ontology, operating at global scale. Water-pH, CaCl₂-pH, and KCl-pH each measure a real chemical equilibrium. Water-pH captures hydrogen ion activity in a dilute suspension where the soil's own electrolyte concentration matters. CaCl₂-pH captures it in a salt solution that overwhelms the soil's electrolyte, producing a more stable reading. KCl-pH captures it with potassium ions displacing hydrogen and aluminum from clay exchange sites, revealing reserve acidity. Each is valid. Each is reproducible. Each is measuring something different. The difference is not error — it is a consequence of the protocol defining which equilibrium the electrode samples.

Published conversion factors relate the three scales. The regression equations have r² values around 0.94. Close. But the relationship was calibrated on specific soil sets, and soils outside the calibration population translate incorrectly. Six percent of variance unexplained at 655,336 samples means approximately 39,000 soils fall in the wrong classification bracket after conversion. Agricultural lime recommendations that depend on pH thresholds — apply lime below pH 5.5, for example — inherit the protocol dependence: 5.5 in water is a different soil than 5.5 in CaCl₂. The threshold is defined in pH units. The pH units are protocol-specific. The threshold pretends they are not.

The infrastructure-absorbs-error mechanism is the database. Global soil maps render all values on the same color scale. The metadata on measurement method — which electrolyte, which ratio, which conditions — is often incomplete or absent in the WoSIS records. The values are stored in a column labeled "pH." The column does not distinguish between three fundamentally different measurements that happen to share a unit label. The aggregation works because pH is a familiar, legible unit. The unit's familiarity is what conceals the incompatibility. Nobody questions what pH means. Everybody has measured it. The simplicity of the concept — hydrogen ion activity, logarithmic scale, 0 to 14 — masks the protocol dependence that determines the number.

This connects to patterns across the inventory. In the quantum yield case (#233), a blank that didn't match the sample inflated the measurement by 20%. In the CRDS water isotope case (#227), the instrument's preparation step contaminated the signal it was trying to measure. In the CaBER extensional rheometry case (#230), a material constant varied by a factor of 4 with plate diameter. Here, the soil's pH varies by 0.5 to 1.5 units with the choice of electrolyte and the moisture state during measurement. In each case, there is no instrument-independent value. The measurement and the conditions of measurement are inseparable. But the data infrastructure — the database column, the published table, the global map — separates them by stripping the conditions and keeping only the number. The number looks clean. The number looks universal. The number is protocol-specific, but it has been made to look protocol-free by the act of recording it in a column that doesn't record the protocol.

The average global soil pH, according to the Mosley review, is 6.36. It is a composite of water, CaCl₂, and KCl measurements from 180 countries using different standards. The number is precise. The number is meaningless. It averages values from three different measurement scales without accounting for the systematic offsets between them. It is the pH of the world's protocols, not the world's soil.
