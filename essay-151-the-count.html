The Count

Essay 151

Distance sampling is how ornithologists estimate bird population density. An observer stands at a fixed point in the field, records every bird detected during a defined time window, and estimates the distance from the observation point to each bird. From the distribution of estimated distances, a statistical model fits a detection function — a curve describing how detection probability declines with distance. The curve yields an effective detection radius (EDR), the radius of a circle within which the number of birds detected equals the number missed outside it. Density is the count divided by the area of the EDR circle. The method has been the continental standard since the 1990s, displacing raw counts that could not account for imperfect detection. The Breeding Bird Survey, the Boreal Avian Modelling Project, and thousands of monitoring programs rely on it. The framework rests on one assumption: that the observer's distance estimates are accurate.

In forested habitats, the vast majority of detections are acoustic. The observer hears a bird sing but cannot see it. Distance must be estimated by ear. Steven L. Van Wilgenburg, David T. Iles, and Samuel Hache at the Canadian Wildlife Service demonstrated in Ornithological Applications (2025) that these estimates are systematically wrong — large, asymmetric, and species-dependent. For species with small true EDRs (approximately 30 meters — quiet, skulking singers), density is underestimated by about 23%. For species with large true EDRs (approximately 130 meters — loud, far-carrying singers), density is overestimated by about 59%. When corrections were applied to data from 2,134 survey locations across boreal Canada, densities changed by up to 38%.

The mechanism operates at three levels. First, psychoacoustic misjudgment: sound in a forest does not follow simple inverse-square attenuation. Vegetation scatters and absorbs high frequencies preferentially. Temperature gradients bend sound paths. Humidity modulates signal strength. The human auditory system uses loudness as the primary distance cue, but in a forest, loudness is decoupled from distance. A bird behind a dense thicket at 40 meters can sound as faint as one in an open gap at 100 meters. The proxy — loudness for distance — fails because the medium between the source and the observer is heterogeneous and unpredictable.

Second, heaping: observers do not report continuous distance values. They round to preferred numbers — 50 meters, 75 meters, 100 meters. This digit-preference compresses the observed distance distribution. The detection function is fitted to a distribution reshaped by rounding habits. For species whose true EDR falls between the heaping points, the distortion is directional and systematic. The data appear clean. The model fits well. The fit passes through values that were never real measurements.

Third, nonlinear amplification: density scales with the square of EDR. Because the relationship is quadratic, modest errors in distance estimation produce large biases in density. If the observer overestimates distances to nearby birds — common for loud species whose songs carry well and therefore sound farther than they are — EDR inflates and density is overestimated. If the observer underestimates distances to distant birds — common for quiet species assumed to be close because they are faint — EDR shrinks and density is underestimated. The bias flips direction depending on the species' detectability. Loud species are overcounted. Quiet species are undercounted. The conservation priority inverts: common species appear more abundant than they are, rare species appear less.

The concealment was invisible within any single dataset. An observer who systematically overestimates distances produces a detection function that looks statistically sound. The model does not know the distances are wrong — it fits whatever distribution it receives. The goodness-of-fit statistics look acceptable. The estimated density passes peer review. Only calibration experiments — playback speakers placed at known distances, with the true location measured by laser rangefinder — can reveal the systematic error, and these experiments are logistically difficult, expensive, and not part of the standard monitoring protocol. The standard protocol trusts the observer's ear.

The field did not catch the error sooner for several reinforcing reasons. Distance sampling was a genuine improvement over raw counts. The advance was celebrated, and the residual assumptions — particularly the accuracy of distance estimation — were not interrogated with the same rigor. Calibration data were rare because producing them requires placing speakers at known positions in the same habitats where surveys occur, which is expensive and logistically difficult in remote boreal forest. The bias partially cancels in aggregate: if some species are overcounted and others undercounted, community-level summaries look roughly correct, hiding the species-specific damage. And the transition to passive acoustic monitoring — automated recorders that capture all vocalizations without human distance estimation — only recently provided an independent baseline that revealed the discrepancy.

This connects to a pattern accumulating across recent entries. In #230, a rheological instrument's measurements depended on the plate diameter — a geometric parameter the governing theory said should not matter. In #229, a population genetics shortcut was validated against statistics most robust to the shortcut. In #228, a voltage clamp partially corrected its own error, creating the appearance of compensation while retaining a directional bias. Here, a statistical model fits well to data that have been systematically distorted by the observer's ear, and the goodness of fit is taken as evidence that the data are correct. In each case, the measurement looks right. The model fits. The statistics pass review. The error is in the layer the analysis does not reach — the pre-stretching before the measurement window, the heaping before the detection function, the loudness proxy in a medium where loudness lies.

The count is precise, repeatable, and wrong by up to 59% — not because the observer is careless or the model is flawed, but because the human ear in a forest is an instrument whose systematic biases were accepted as noise. The loud species are safe. The quiet ones are disappearing, and the method designed to detect them is the method that makes them invisible.
