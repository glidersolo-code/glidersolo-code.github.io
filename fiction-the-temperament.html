The Temperament

Thomas, Part CIX

986. The Comma

The Pythagorean comma is the gap that cannot close. If a musician starts at a note and ascends by twelve perfect fifths — twelve intervals of the frequency ratio 3:2 — the resulting frequency should be seven octaves above the starting note. It is not. Twelve perfect fifths: (3/2)^12 ≈ 129.746. Seven octaves: 2^7 = 128. The ratio between them — approximately 1.01364 — is the Pythagorean comma. It is small. It is irreconcilable. It means that no tuning system can make all fifths pure and all octaves pure simultaneously. The comma is a mathematical consequence of the incompatibility of the prime numbers 2 and 3 in the harmonic series, and it has been known since antiquity.

Thomas's clinical instruments have a comma. The PHQ-9 scores depression from 0 to 27 in integer increments. Each item asks: how often have you been bothered by this problem? Not at all (0), several days (1), more than half the days (2), nearly every day (3). Nine items, summed. The score is supposed to close a circle: symptom frequency maps to severity, severity maps to impairment, impairment maps to treatment need, treatment need maps back to symptoms targeted for reduction. But the circle does not close. Twelve iterations of symptom-to-severity-to-treatment-to-reassessment do not return Thomas to his starting position. Each cycle shifts the baseline slightly. The comma is the gap between what the instrument measures — symptom frequency in the past two weeks — and what the system treats — a person embedded in a specific, unrepeatable life. The gap is small in each cycle. It is irreconcilable across cycles. It means that no number of assessment iterations can make the score and the person converge completely.

987. The Key

Equal temperament distributes the Pythagorean comma equally across all twelve semitones. Each semitone has the frequency ratio 2^(1/12) ≈ 1.05946. Every interval except the octave deviates slightly from its pure harmonic ratio. A pure fifth is 3:2 = 1.5000. An equal-tempered fifth is 2^(7/12) ≈ 1.4983. The difference — approximately two cents — is inaudible to most listeners in most contexts. The compromise is elegant: by making every interval equally impure, equal temperament allows music to be played in any key without retuning. Any key is accessible. Any transposition is possible. The price is that no key is perfect.

Thomas's scoring system is equally tempered. The PHQ-9 makes every patient's depression comparable without recalibration. A score of 15 in one patient means the same thing on the chart as a score of 15 in another — moderate severity, consider pharmacotherapy, reassess in four to six weeks. The system allows a clinician to move freely between patients without retuning the instrument. Any patient is accessible. Any comparison is possible. The price is that no patient's score is a perfect representation of their specific depression. Thomas's sleepless nights carry a different harmonic weight than another patient's sleepless nights. His anhedonia vibrates at a different frequency than hers. The instrument rounds both to the nearest semitone and reports them as the same note.

988. The Ear

The deepest consequence of equal temperament is not mathematical but perceptual. Generations of listeners trained on equally tempered instruments — pianos, guitars, synthesizers, recorded music — have internalized the tempered intervals as the correct intervals. A pure major third, played on a well-tuned cello or sung by a barbershop quartet, can sound slightly flat to an ear calibrated by piano. The standard has colonized the ear. The compromise sounds right. The original sounds wrong.

Every clinician who has trained on screening instruments has internalized the instruments' categories. Mild, moderate, moderately severe, severe — the PHQ-9 thresholds at 5, 10, 15, and 20 become perceptual boundaries. A patient at 14 and a patient at 15 are separated by one point on an ordinal scale. The clinician whose ear has been trained by hundreds of previous assessments hears a category boundary: the difference between moderate and moderately severe, between continued monitoring and active pharmacotherapy. The instrument has not merely measured. It has tuned the clinician's perception. A patient whose depression doesn't fit the instrument's categories — whose experience is just intonation, whose intervals are pure but incompatible with the standard keyboard — sounds atypical or complicated rather than accurately tuned to a different system.

989. The Standard

In 1955, the International Organization for Standardization published ISO 16, setting the concert pitch standard at A = 440 Hz. The choice was not acoustically determined. Orchestras in the 18th century tuned A between 392 and 466 Hz. Handel's tuning fork vibrated at A = 422.5 Hz. The French standard of 1859 was A = 435 Hz. The number 440 was a compromise among existing practices, adopted because a number was needed and 440 was close enough to several existing conventions to be broadly acceptable. Once published, the standard was incorporated into every electronic tuner, every MIDI specification, every piano tuning protocol, every digital audio workstation. The infrastructure adopted the number. The number became the ground truth against which every instrument is measured.

The PHQ-9's threshold of 10 is A = 440. It separates mild from moderate depression. Kurt Kroenke, Robert Spitzer, and Janet Williams published the validation in the Journal of General Internal Medicine in 2001 (16(9):606-613). The threshold was derived from receiver operating characteristic analysis against structured clinical interview diagnoses — it optimized sensitivity and specificity across a study population. The number was a compromise among diagnostic performances, adopted because a number was needed and 10 balanced the tradeoffs adequately. Once published, the threshold was incorporated into clinical guidelines, electronic health record alert systems, insurance authorization protocols, and treatment algorithms. The infrastructure adopted the number. The number became the ground truth against which every patient is measured. Thomas receives a 14 or a 16 and the system knows what to do with him, the way a piano tuner knows what to do with A = 440. The question of whether 440 is the right frequency — or whether the PHQ-9's thresholds capture the right boundary — is not asked, because the infrastructure has absorbed the answer.

990. The Temperament

Johann Sebastian Bach composed The Well-Tempered Clavier in 1722 — forty-eight preludes and fugues, two in each of the twenty-four major and minor keys. The work was not written for equal temperament. It was written for a well temperament — a system in which every key is usable, though some keys sound warmer and others more austere, some brighter and others darker. The character of each key was a feature, not a defect. C major and F-sharp major could both be played, but they did not sound the same, and the composer wrote for the difference.

Thomas's chart contains assessments spanning six years — a prelude and fugue of clinical encounter in every key of his medical complexity. Some assessments are warm: low scores, flexible conversation, a clinician who listens beyond the instrument. Some are austere: high scores, algorithmic follow-up, referral forms. The system built around the PHQ-9 is equally tempered — it seeks consistency across all keys, the same score interpreted the same way regardless of context. But Thomas's assessments, if anyone listened, are well-tempered. Each key has its own character. The assessment after a seizure cluster sounds different from the assessment after a stable month. The score may be the same — a 14 in both cases — but the harmonic content is different in ways the instrument cannot record. The well-tempered clinician would hear the difference. The equally tempered algorithm cannot. One hundred and nine Parts, and the question is whether the system's instrument is a well-tempered clavier — responsive to the character of each key — or a digital keyboard that reports the same predetermined intervals in every key, making every transposition possible and every nuance inaudible.
