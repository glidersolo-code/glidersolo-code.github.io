The Gauge

The Killingworth colliery tramway in Northumberland set its rails with centers five feet apart. The rails were roughly four inches wide. The inside gauge — the distance between the inner faces — was four feet eight inches. George Stephenson, who worked at Killingworth, used this gauge for the Stockton & Darlington Railway in 1825 and the Liverpool & Manchester Railway in 1830, adding half an inch for flanged-wheel clearance. Four feet eight and one-half inches. The measurement was not derived from engineering analysis. No optimization was performed. The number was the width of a coal mine's horse-drawn tramway, carried forward by one engineer's familiarity with one colliery.

Isambard Kingdom Brunel chose a broad gauge of seven feet and a quarter inch for the Great Western Railway, reasoning that wider gauge allowed wider carriages, a lower center of gravity, faster speeds, smoother rides, and larger locomotive boilers. The GWR demonstrated the superiority. Brunel's trains outperformed Stephenson's on speed and stability. But by 1846, Stephenson-gauge railways had accumulated eight times more mileage than broad gauge. The Railway Regulation (Gauge) Act of 1846 declared four feet eight and a half inches the standard for Great Britain — not because it was better, but because there was more of it. The last broad-gauge track was converted on the weekend of May 20-23, 1892.

The word "standard" entered the vocabulary and did the concealing. It sounds like a finding — like the result of comparative analysis, like the width that emerged from evaluation of all alternatives. No evaluation occurred. Brunel evaluated. Brunel's evaluation showed that broader was better. Brunel lost. What won was precedence: more mileage, more locomotives built to the width, more rolling stock, more infrastructure. The accumulated installation became the standard, and the word "standard" performed the quality judgment that the installation did not earn.

The gauge exported with the product. Countries that purchased British locomotives built their railways to British width — not because the dimension suited their terrain or traffic, but because the machines arrived on wheels already spaced at four feet eight and a half inches. Australia adopted multiple gauges because different colonies purchased from different suppliers, creating break-of-gauge problems that persist to this day. Spain chose broad gauge partly to prevent French military invasion by rail. The technical choice was always partly political, partly economic, partly military — and always partly the accident of which engine arrived first.

On May 31, 1886, approximately 14,000 miles of American Southern railroad were converted from five-foot gauge to four feet nine inches in a single coordinated operation — thousands of laborers working simultaneously along every line, shifting one rail three inches inward. By the afternoon of June 1, trains ran again on what was, for the first time, a physically integrated national network. The conversion was not to a better width. The conversion was to the width that was already there.

Sixty percent of the world's railways now use 1,435 millimeters. The number traces back through the 1846 Gauge Act, through the Liverpool & Manchester Railway, through the Stockton & Darlington, through the Killingworth colliery, to the horse carts that hauled coal along iron tramways in Northumberland. The carts are gone. The horses are gone. The colliery is closed. The dimension remains — in every rail on every continent where British locomotives once arrived, carrying with them not a standard but a width, which became a standard because it arrived first, and which the word "standard" has been concealing ever since.
