Part CCCLXXVII: The Address

Piece #2326: The Route

RFC 791, September 1981. Thomas read the document's own framing and underlined it: "A name indicates what we seek. An address indicates where it is. A route indicates how to get there." The IP address was the second thing — an address. Where the packet goes. Not who sent it, not who received it, not who the person is. Where the network interface sits in the routing topology. The protocol was written for ARPANET, a research network with a few hundred nodes, all cooperative, all trusted. No adversarial model. No identity layer. No assumption that the address would ever need to identify a person. Thomas thought of the SSN — another technical label that expanded into an identity function its designers never imagined. The same pattern. The system needs a unique number. The number exists. The system uses it. The gap between what the number was designed to do and what the system demands it do is the concealment.

Piece #2327: The Exhaustion

On February 3, 2011, the last blocks of IPv4 addresses were distributed. The free pool was exhausted. 4,294,967,296 total addresses — the entire capacity of a 32-bit space, designed in the 1970s for a world of hundreds of nodes, consumed by a world of billions of devices. Thomas expected the response to be IPv6, the replacement protocol with 340 undecillion addresses. The response was NAT — a workaround that let thousands of devices share a single public address. As of 2025, IPv6 carried about 45% of traffic. The exhausted system still served the majority. The workaround had outlasted the solution by fourteen years and counting. The temporary fix became the infrastructure. Thomas had seen this before.

Piece #2328: The Farm

MaxMind needed a default location for IP addresses it could only resolve to "United States." It chose the geographic center of the country: 38°N, 97°W. The coordinates fell on a farmstead in Kansas, owned by a woman named Joyce Taylor. For fourteen years, approximately 600 million unlocatable IP addresses pointed to her front door. The FBI came looking for runaway children. The IRS came investigating tax fraud. Thomas read the story and sat with the number: 600 million. Six hundred million addresses, each representing a person or a device that the system could not locate, all compressed into a single point on a Kansas farm. The system demanded a location for every address. When it didn't have one, it invented one. The format demands completeness. The completeness was fabricated. When the story was published in 2016, MaxMind moved the default point to the center of a nearby lake. The lake doesn't complain.

Piece #2329: The Warrant

Judge Harold Baker, Central District of Illinois: "IP address does not equal a person." Thomas wrote this down and read it again. A judge had to say it aloud because every other system assumed the opposite. The legal process: law enforcement identifies an IP address in a log. Subpoenas the ISP. The ISP returns a subscriber name and address. The subscriber is not necessarily the user — could be a family member, a guest, a neighbor using the WiFi, an attacker who compromised the router. The IP may be dynamic — assigned to a different subscriber by the time the subpoena is served. The geolocation may be wrong by fifty kilometers. MaxMind's own city-level accuracy: 66% within a 50km radius. One in three times, the city is wrong. But the number — 192.168.1.1 — looks precise. Four octets. Dot-separated. Specific. The format performs certainty. The identification is probabilistic. The precision of the number conceals the imprecision of the mapping.

Piece #2330: The Distinction

Thomas returned to RFC 791's distinction. Name, address, route. The IP address was designed to be an address — where the interface sits. It became a route — how to find the device. Then it became a name — who the person is. Each transition was not designed but assumed. The routing label was actionable: you could subpoena it, geolocate it, log it, match it. The fact that it produced results — a subscriber, a location, a suspect — was treated as proof that it identified the person. But actionability is not accuracy. A system that reliably produces a name does not reliably produce the right name. The EU classified IP addresses as personal data. Canada's Supreme Court required judicial authorization to access subscriber records behind an IP. The United States requires no warrant. The same number — the same four octets, the same routing label designed in 1974 for a 256-network world — means personal data in Brussels, protected information in Ottawa, and a return address on an envelope in Washington.
