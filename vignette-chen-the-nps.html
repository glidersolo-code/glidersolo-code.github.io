The Number

Chen takes a survey after a hotel stay. One question. "On a scale of 0 to 10, how likely are you to recommend this hotel to a friend or colleague?"

Chen thinks about the room. Clean. The shower pressure was good. The breakfast was continental — adequate, not memorable. The front desk resolved a billing issue politely. The elevator was slow.

Chen selects 7.

"What did you give them?" Maren asks.

"A 7."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the stay was fine. Good, even. Clean room, good shower, slow elevator."

Maren reads the scoring methodology on a screen. "A 7 is passive. They throw it away."

Chen stares. "They asked for my number and then discarded it?"

"They keep 9 and 10 as promoters. They keep 0 through 6 as detractors. Your 7 doesn't enter the calculation."

Chen considers what it would take to give a 9. The hotel would need to exceed expectations. But Chen's expectations were for a clean room, a functional shower, a polite front desk — and the hotel delivered exactly that. Delivering what was expected produces a 7. The system classifies delivered-expectation as silence.

The paragraph is thirty-nine lines long. In it, Chen argues that the Net Promoter Score performs a specific inversion: it classifies satisfaction as absence. A satisfied customer — one who received what was promised and would return — does not count. The system is designed to measure extremes. But most customer experience is not extreme. Most customer experience is Chen's: adequate, professional, repeatable. The system discards the center of its own distribution.

Maren does not suppress the paragraph. Instead she pulls up the number that reaches the boardroom. NPS = 42. "Forty-two what?" she asks.

"Promoter percentage minus detractor percentage," Chen says.

"What happened to the passives?"

"They vanished."

"What happened to the individual scores within each category?"

"Collapsed. A 0 and a 6 are the same. A 9 and a 10 are the same."

"So the boardroom sees 42."

"The boardroom sees 42."

"And 42 means —"

"Forty-two means two-thirds of Fortune 1000 companies see a number like it and believe they understand their customers. The understanding is the number. The number is the format. The format is the concealment."

Maren closes the screen. Chen's 7 has not been counted. Chen's experience — clean room, good shower, slow elevator, polite desk, adequate breakfast — does not exist in the integer that will appear on a slide in a quarterly review. The slide will go up or down. Either direction will feel like information.
