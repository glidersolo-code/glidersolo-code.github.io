Part CCCLXIX: The Channel

Piece #2286: The Frequency

Channel 2 is 54 to 60 megahertz. Channel 7 is 174 to 180 megahertz. Channel 13 is 210 to 216 megahertz. The number identifies a slice of electromagnetic spectrum — a band of frequencies allocated by the Federal Communications Commission for the transmission of audio and video signals through the air.

In the beginning, the number meant the frequency. Only the frequency. Channel 7 News was not a brand. It was a location in the spectrum — 174 megahertz, receivable by rotating the dial on a vacuum-tube television set until the tuner locked onto the signal. The number told the antenna where to listen.

Then Channel 1 disappeared. On June 14, 1948, the FCC reallocated the Channel 1 frequencies — 44 to 50 megahertz — to land mobile radio and public safety. Rather than renumber the remaining channels, the Commission simply deleted Channel 1 from the table. Television starts at 2. Every television set ever manufactured in the United States begins at a number other than 1. No one has ever asked why. The answer is a spectrum allocation decision made in 1948 by engineers who are now dead, for reasons that involved interference between television broadcasters and two-way radio operators. The absence of Channel 1 is a fossil of a bureaucratic compromise that no living viewer remembers.

Piece #2287: The Hierarchy

VHF and UHF. The letters stood for Very High Frequency and Ultra High Frequency. The distinction was physical — VHF signals (channels 2–13) traveled farther, penetrated buildings better, required simpler antennas. UHF signals (channels 14–83, added in 1952) were weaker, shorter-range, harder to receive.

The FCC's original allocation created a permanent hierarchy. The stations that received VHF licenses — channels 2 through 13 — had structural advantages that no amount of programming could overcome. Better signal. Larger coverage area. Easier reception. The network affiliates claimed the low numbers. The independents got UHF. The hierarchy was electromagnetic, but it looked like quality. Channel 5 looked like a real station. Channel 48 looked like it was trying.

Congress passed the All-Channel Receiver Act in 1962, mandating that all new television sets include UHF tuners. The law addressed the hardware. It could not address the perception. Low numbers meant networks. High numbers meant reruns, local programming, and static. The frequency allocation had created a class system, and the class system outlived the frequency allocation.

Piece #2288: The Cable

Cable television arrived and severed the last connection between channel number and frequency. A cable system could assign any station to any number. The local NBC affiliate could be on channel 3 or channel 12 or channel 47 — it depended on the cable operator's lineup, not on any electromagnetic reality.

Yet many cable systems placed local broadcast stations on their traditional over-the-air channel numbers. Channel 7 on cable was the same station as Channel 7 over the air — not because the frequencies matched, but because the brand demanded it. The viewers expected it. The identity was stronger than the infrastructure.

The cable box tuned to channels that had nothing to do with the electromagnetic spectrum. Channel 58 on cable was not 734 megahertz. It was a position in a digital lineup determined by a contract between a cable operator and a content provider. The number had become pure sequence — an address in a list, not a location in physics. But the grammar of "turning to Channel 7" persisted. The verb survived the death of the mechanism. You do not tune anything. You select. The word "channel" — originally a path for water, then a path for signal — became a path for nothing. A label on a button.

Piece #2289: The Virtual

On June 12, 2009, the United States completed the digital television transition. Every analog signal went dark. The frequencies were reallocated. Stations that had broadcast on one frequency for decades were moved to different frequencies.

The problem was the brand. KTLA had been Channel 5 in Los Angeles since 1947. Its logo was a 5. Its news was "KTLA 5 News." Its building said 5. But after the digital transition, KTLA's signal was transmitted on physical RF channel 31. Channel 5 was no longer 76 to 82 megahertz. Channel 5 was a fiction.

The FCC built the fiction into the infrastructure. The ATSC standard introduced PSIP — Program and System Information Protocol — which assigned each station a "virtual channel." KTLA transmitted on RF channel 31 but appeared on every television set as Channel 5.1. The Commission explicitly recognized the need to preserve "analog channel number brand identification." Engineers designed a protocol — wrote code, drafted standards, implemented firmware — specifically to maintain a number that no longer corresponded to any physical reality.

The infrastructure rebuilt itself around the brand. The frequency bowed to the fiction.

Piece #2290: The Number

What is Channel 7?

It is not 174 to 180 megahertz. That frequency may now carry cellular data. It is not a position on a VHF dial. The dial does not exist. It is not an over-the-air broadcast frequency for the station that brands itself as Channel 7. That station transmits on a different RF channel, translated by firmware into the number the viewers expect.

Channel 7 is a brand. It was a frequency in 1941, a dial position in 1955, a cable assignment in 1985, a virtual designation in 2009, and a streaming icon in 2025. At each transition, the number survived by shedding whatever physical reality it had most recently inhabited. The frequency was reallocated. The dial was removed. The cable was cut. The virtual channel was assigned. The number persisted through every demolition because the infrastructure rebuilt itself around it every time.

The area code outlived its infrastructure passively — nobody built new technology to keep 212 meaning Manhattan. The channel number outlived its infrastructure actively — engineers built a new protocol, wrote a new standard, specifically to maintain the fiction that Channel 5 was still Channel 5.

The brand commanded the protocol. The identity commanded the engineering. The number, which began as a frequency allocation in an FCC table, now commands the systems that serve it. The servant became the master. The address became the addressee.

And somewhere in the table, between Channel 1 that was deleted in 1948 and Channel 83 that was reclaimed for cellular in 1983, the electromagnetic spectrum that gave the numbers their meaning continues to carry signals that have nothing to do with television, through frequencies that no longer belong to the channels that once bore their names.
