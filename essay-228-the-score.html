The Score

Robert Parker, a lawyer in Baltimore, founded The Wine Advocate in 1978 and adopted a 100-point scale for rating wines. The scale was borrowed from the American school grading system — 50 as the floor, 100 as the ceiling. In practice, nearly all scored wines fall between 80 and 100. Below 80 is rarely published. Below 50 is never used. The 100-point frame creates the appearance of a wide range — as though the scale measured from nothing to perfection — while the effective range is approximately twenty points. The eighty unused points below the effective range perform breadth that does not exist.

A wine is a multi-dimensional sensory object. Aroma (hundreds of volatile compounds, evolving as the wine opens). Taste (sweet, sour, bitter, salty, umami). Texture (tannin, acidity, viscosity). Temperature. Time — what happens in the glass over thirty minutes, in the bottle over thirty years. A single tasting involves dozens of perceptual dimensions unfolding across multiple timescales. The score compresses all of them into a single integer. A 92 that is austere and tannic and a 92 that is ripe and lush carry the same number. The integer consumes the dimensions. Nothing in the number indicates which dimensions contributed. Nothing in the number indicates what the wine will become — whether it is best at release or at twenty years.

Parker preferred big, concentrated, ripe wines — lower yields, longer hangtime, more new oak, more extraction. Wines that matched this style scored higher. Winemakers, observing which style produced high scores, adjusted their techniques to match. The scores changed the wines. The wines then confirmed the scores. A single point — the difference between 89 and 90 — could change a wine's wholesale price by four to eight dollars per bottle. A 95 could sell out a small producer's vintage. An 85 could make it unsellable. The measurement that was supposed to describe quality had begun to prescribe it.

Robert Hodgson, a retired oceanographer and winemaker, studied the California State Fair wine competition from 2005 to 2008. When the same wine was submitted twice under different identifiers, the results were inconsistent — gold at one judging, nothing at another. Approximately 10 percent of gold-medal wines earned gold when resubmitted. Goldstein and colleagues at the American Association of Wine Economists found in 2008 that non-expert tasters showed no reliable preference for expensive wines in blind tastings. Expert tasters showed a positive correlation between price and quality rating, but with variance large enough to make individual ratings unreliable.

The score on the shelf tag — 91, 94, 88 — is the output of a single palate, in a single setting, at a single moment. The critic tasted at a controlled temperature, in a professional sequence, through a palate calibrated by decades of practice. The consumer opens the bottle at home, at an uncontrolled temperature, with whatever food is served, through a palate shaped by whatever was eaten that day. The number travels from the critic's mouth to the shelf tag to the purchase decision without adjusting for the distance between the two contexts. An integer printed on a card, experienced as a fact about the liquid, describing one person's perception in one moment, deployed as a universal property of the bottle.
