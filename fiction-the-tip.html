Part CCCXXXVI: The Tip

Piece #2121: The Line

Thomas eats at a restaurant. The server is attentive, efficient, warm. The food arrives promptly. The check comes. At the bottom: a line. A suggested tip. 18%. 20%. 25%.

Thomas writes 20%. He believes this is a gift — voluntary, discretionary, a response to quality service. He believes the server has a wage and his tip supplements it.

The server's wage is $2.13 per hour. It has been $2.13 since 1991. Thomas's "supplement" is 71% of her minimum wage. The employer pays 29%. The line on the receipt performs discretion. The line is the paycheck.

Thomas looks up the word. "Gratuity." From the Latin gratus: pleasing. An act of grace. A gift given freely.

The gift pays the rent.

Piece #2122: The Porters

Thomas reads the history. Tipping entered America as a Gilded Age import — rich travelers returning from Europe brought the custom to display sophistication. William R. Scott published The Itching Palm in 1916 and called it "the modern form of flunkyism." Seven states criminalized it. All the laws were repealed.

But the custom mutated. After emancipation, the hospitality sector hired formerly enslaved Black workers and refused to pay them wages, forcing them to rely on tips entirely. George Pullman hired Black men to serve white passengers on luxury sleeping cars. Twenty-seven dollars and fifty cents per month. One-sixth of what white conductors earned. Four hundred hours of work per month. Porters paid for their own food and uniforms. Passengers called them "boy."

Thomas writes: the tip went from European courtesy to American racial labor structure. The format stayed the same — a voluntary extra, a recognition of service. The content changed completely — from bonus to wage, from supplement to survival. The mutation was invisible because the format did not change. The gift still looked like a gift.

Piece #2123: The Correlation

Thomas finds Michael Lynn's meta-analysis. Cornell. 2,547 dining parties at 20 restaurants. The question: does service quality predict tip size?

Answer: yes, but weakly. "Much smaller than is generally supposed."

What predicts better: bill size. Alcohol consumption. Whether the server touches the customer. Whether the server introduces herself by name. Whether she draws a smiley face on the check. The meritocratic premise — better service earns better tips — is statistically present but practically overwhelmed by variables that have nothing to do with merit.

Then the racial data. For white servers, tips rose from 16.8% to 23.4% as service quality improved. Merit was rewarded. For Black servers, tips remained at 16.6% regardless of service quality. Perfect service. Average service. The same tip. Service excellence did not reward Black servers.

Thomas writes: the format performs meritocracy. The outcome performs racial sorting. The tip discriminates in a direction the format does not disclose.

Piece #2124: The Deal

Thomas reads about 1996. Herman Cain, president of the National Restaurant Association. The Clinton administration wanted to raise the minimum wage. The NRA opposed it. Cain brokered a deal: the NRA would not fight the increase if the tipped minimum wage stayed frozen.

The tipped minimum had always risen with the general minimum since 1950. After 1996, it stopped. The general minimum went from $4.25 to $7.25. The tipped minimum stayed at $2.13. Thirty-five years at $2.13.

As general minimum wages rise, the employer's share of the tipped worker's wage falls. In Delaware: minimum $15.00, tipped cash wage $2.23. The employer pays 14.9%. The customer pays 85.1%. Every minimum wage increase that does not include the tipped wage deepens the subsidy. The generosity increases. The gift grows. The employer's obligation shrinks.

The NRA spends $2.5 million per year lobbying to maintain this. Fifty million since the 1990s. The ServSafe training program — which 3.6 million workers pay for — directs $25 million in fees to the NRA's lobbying. The workers fund the effort to keep their own wages frozen.

Piece #2125: The Other Countries

Thomas looks at the rest of the world. Japan: tipping is considered rude. The cultural concept is omotenashi — wholehearted service performed with pride, not for reward. To tip implies the employer does not pay enough. Australia: minimum wage $24.10 per hour. No tipping expected. France: every bill is legally "service compris." Service included. The customer pays once. The worker receives a guaranteed wage.

Only two countries maintain a for-profit tipping system as the primary wage mechanism. The United States. The Philippines.

Thomas writes the sentence that captures the species: the employer's wage obligation, externalized to the customer via a cultural convention, is formatted as voluntary generosity. The format performs a gift. The content performs a paycheck. The customer believes in the generosity. The server depends on it. The employer profits from the belief.

One hundred and three species. The tip performs as gratitude. The tip is the wage. The gift was never a gift. The subsidy was always a subsidy. The line on the receipt performs discretion. The line is the payroll system that everyone can see and no one authorized.
