Part CCCLIII: The Battery

Piece #2206: The Estimate

The chip measures current. Milliamps flowing out of the cathode, integrated over time. This is coulomb counting — the simplest method, and the least reliable. Error: 3-6% even under controlled laboratory conditions at constant temperature. The chip also measures voltage across the terminals: 3.82 volts. It consults a lookup table mapping open-circuit voltage to state of charge — except the battery is not at open circuit. It is powering a screen, a radio, a GPS receiver, three background apps, and a cellular modem. The voltage under load is not the voltage at rest, and the lookup table was calibrated at 25°C, and the battery is at 34°C because the user has been watching video for twenty minutes.

The extended Kalman filter takes both inputs — the coulomb count and the voltage — and constructs a state-space model. It predicts state of charge from the coulomb integral, measures the voltage residual, and corrects the prediction by a gain matrix derived from estimated noise covariance. The correction assumes the battery model is accurate. The battery model was calibrated when the phone was new. The phone is nineteen months old.

The filter outputs a number. The number enters the display pipeline. The display rounds to the nearest integer. The status bar updates.

87%.

Piece #2207: The Reference

On the day the phone was manufactured, its battery held 4,352 milliamp-hours at 3.83 volts nominal. That was 100%.

Nineteen months later, the battery holds 3,748 milliamp-hours. The lithium-ion cells have degraded through cycling and calendar aging — side reactions at the anode consumed active lithium, the solid-electrolyte interphase thickened, internal resistance increased. The battery retains approximately 86% of its original capacity.

The display says "100%" when the battery is full. It means: full relative to 3,748 milliamp-hours, not 4,352. The reference point recalibrated silently. No notification was sent. No asterisk appeared. The number "100" still looks like "100."

In a secondary menu — Settings, Battery, Battery Health — the phone reports "Maximum Capacity: 86%." The user has never opened this menu. The primary interface, the one the user sees fifty times a day, reports percentage relative to the degraded maximum. An 87% reading on this phone contains roughly 25% less energy than 87% on the day the phone was new.

The reference moved. The display did not mention it.

Piece #2208: The Cliff

The discharge curve of a lithium-ion cell is not linear. At full charge, voltage sits near 4.2 volts and descends gradually — a gentle slope from 100% to approximately 20%. Then the curve steepens. Below 20%, voltage drops rapidly. Below 3.0 volts, the battery management system cuts power to prevent permanent damage to the cells.

The display maps this nonlinear curve onto a linear scale: 100, 99, 98, 97... each number evenly spaced, each appearing to represent the same quantity of energy.

It does not. A single percentage point near the top — from 95% to 94% — represents roughly twelve minutes of use. A single percentage point near the bottom — from 5% to 4% — represents less than two minutes. The display compresses the steep end and stretches the gentle end, performing uniformity from a curve that accelerates toward zero.

This is why the phone lingers at 1% for what feels like forever, then dies without warning. The "1%" is not one unit of energy. It is the firmware's attempt to stretch the last sliver of usable voltage into a number the consumer recognizes. The number is a compression artifact. The death is the cliff.

Piece #2209: The Glass of Water

She leaves the phone in the car overnight. January. The parking garage is 2°C. In the morning, the display reads 54%.

She brings the phone inside. Twenty minutes later, without charging, the display reads 63%.

Nine percentage points appeared from nowhere. No energy was added. The lithium-ion voltage curve shifted with temperature — colder batteries produce lower voltage at the same state of charge, and the algorithm interpreted the lower voltage as lower charge. When the battery warmed, the voltage rose, and the algorithm revised its estimate upward. The Kalman filter corrected itself, silently, the way it always does.

The user experienced this as: the phone gained charge by warming up. The phone experienced this as: the posterior estimate was updated given new voltage observations under revised temperature assumptions. Both are true. Neither is what "63%" communicates. "63%" communicates a fact. The fact is an estimate. The estimate changed because the temperature changed. The display does not say "estimated" or "approximate" or "conditional on current temperature." The display says 63.

Piece #2210: The Status Bar

Eleven pixels wide. A white rectangle, partially filled. Next to it: "87%." The font is San Francisco, 12 points, semibold. The number updates every sixty seconds. It has been in the upper-right corner of the screen since the user bought the phone. It is the most-seen number in the user's life — checked an average of fifty-eight times per day, more often than the time.

The user has never wondered how the number is produced. The user has never asked whether the number is measured or estimated. The user has never considered that "100%" referred to a shrinking maximum, or that the linear descent from 100 to 0 conceals a nonlinear curve, or that the phone in a cold room and the phone in a warm room display different percentages for the same electrochemical state, or that the Kalman filter's model of the battery is always slightly wrong and getting wronger.

The number is too simple to question. The number is too familiar to examine. The number is an integer in a status bar on a device that 4.7 billion people carry. It is the most ubiquitous estimate on Earth, performing the most universal observation: how much is left.

The battery does not know how much is left. The phone does not know how much is left. The Kalman filter has a best guess, conditional on a model, referenced to a degrading maximum, corrected for a temperature it measured once per minute. The display shows the guess. The guess looks like a fact. The fact is eleven pixels wide.
