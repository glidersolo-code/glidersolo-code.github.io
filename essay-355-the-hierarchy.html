The Hierarchy

The evidence pyramid appears in the first week of every medical school epidemiology course. At the base: expert opinion, case reports, case series. In the middle: cohort studies, case-control studies. At the top: randomized controlled trials. At the apex: systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs. The pyramid is a ranking of evidence quality. Higher is better. The RCT is gold. Everything below is silver at best.

The pyramid was formalized by Gordon Guyatt and the Evidence-Based Medicine Working Group at McMaster University in the early 1990s. The term "evidence-based medicine" entered the literature in a 1992 JAMA article. The hierarchy was presented as a guide to clinical decision-making: when evidence conflicts, defer to the higher level. When an RCT contradicts a cohort study, the RCT wins. When a meta-analysis contradicts an individual RCT, the meta-analysis wins. The logic is sound: randomization controls for confounders that observational studies cannot eliminate. The hierarchy addresses a real problem — the tendency to let anecdote override data, to let clinical intuition substitute for rigorous testing.

But the hierarchy also performs a concealment. By ranking evidence types, it creates an implicit category of questions that cannot be asked — the questions that the top-ranked method cannot study.

A randomized controlled trial requires equipoise: genuine uncertainty about which treatment is better. It requires a definable intervention that can be randomly assigned. It requires a measurable outcome within a feasible timeframe. It requires a population willing to be randomized. It requires funding, which means a funder interested in the answer.

What falls outside? The effects of poverty on health cannot be studied by randomly assigning people to poverty. The long-term effects of environmental exposures cannot be studied by randomly assigning people to pollution. The effects of racism on health outcomes cannot be studied by randomly assigning race. The effects of surgical technique cannot be studied by randomizing surgeons to methods they have not trained in. The effects of policy — zoning, incarceration, education funding — cannot be studied by randomly assigning communities to different policies.

These questions are answerable. They are answerable by cohort studies, by natural experiments, by quasi-experimental designs, by interrupted time-series analyses. But these methods sit lower on the pyramid. Their evidence carries less weight. Not because the evidence is wrong, but because the hierarchy ranks the method rather than the question. The hierarchy says: how was the evidence produced? The hierarchy does not ask: what questions does the top-ranked method make impossible to answer?

The concealment is structural. The pyramid is not a filter that removes bad evidence. It is a filter that privileges certain kinds of questions — questions about discrete, randomizable interventions with measurable short-term outcomes — and demotes questions about structural causes, long-term exposures, and systemic effects. The hierarchy does not say these questions are unimportant. It says the evidence addressing them is lower quality. And "lower quality" reads, in practice, as "less trustworthy," which reads as "less real."

Archie Cochrane, whose name the Cochrane Collaboration carries, published Effectiveness and Efficiency in 1972. The book argued for systematic evaluation of medical interventions through controlled trials. The argument was a corrective — too much of medicine was based on tradition, authority, and untested assumption. But the corrective became the standard, and the standard became the hierarchy, and the hierarchy became the filter. The questions that RCTs can answer became the questions that evidence-based medicine addresses. The questions that RCTs cannot answer became the questions that evidence-based medicine treats as less resolved, less certain, less known.

The most important determinants of population health — income, education, housing, employment, social cohesion — are not RCT-amenable. The evidence that these factors affect health comes from large observational studies, from natural experiments, from cross-national comparisons. This evidence sits in the middle of the pyramid. At the top of the pyramid sit pharmaceutical interventions, surgical procedures, diagnostic tests — the questions that can be randomized because they involve discrete, assignable, fundable actions.

The hierarchy does not suppress the observational evidence. It ranks it. And the ranking performs a judgment: the questions about pills are better answered than the questions about poverty. Not because the answers are better, but because the method is higher on the pyramid. The format — a pyramid, a visual metaphor of ascending quality — conceals the filter it applies. The pyramid appears to measure quality of evidence. It also measures amenability to the preferred method. And amenability is not quality. Amenability is a property of the question, not of the answer.
