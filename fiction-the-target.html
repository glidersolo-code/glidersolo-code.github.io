Part CCCLXXVIII: The Target

Piece #2331: The Interview

April 1, 1988. April Fools' Day. Thomas read the date three times. A New Zealand television reporter asked Finance Minister Roger Douglas whether his government was satisfied with inflation declining from fifteen percent to ten. Douglas, without consulting officials, economists, or parliamentary colleagues, replied that he would not be satisfied until inflation was "around 0 or 0 to 1 percent." An improvised remark. Plucked from air. Michael Reddell, the Reserve Bank economist who would spend years translating the remark into policy, later called it "almost a chance remark." Thomas thought of the Pledge of Allegiance — another text produced in haste (two hours, for a magazine subscription drive) that became a national institution. But the Pledge accumulated authority through daily repetition. This number would accumulate authority through institutional adoption. Each central bank that joined the chain would add weight, and each would cite the previous adopter, not the television interview. The chain would become the derivation.

Piece #2332: The Rationalization

Don Brash needed a number. Douglas had given him a direction — toward zero — but not a target. Brash's team at the Reserve Bank estimated CPI measurement bias at approximately 0.75 percent. Consumer price indices overstate true inflation because they struggle with quality improvements, substitution effects, new products. Round the bias up to one percent. If genuine price stability is the goal, then measured inflation of zero to two percent captures it. The upper bound — two — was a rounding artifact. A bias correction applied to an improvised target, rounded for administrative convenience. Thomas traced the logic: Douglas improvises on television. Brash rounds a bias estimate. Two percent becomes the band's ceiling. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act passes December 1989 without a single dissenting vote. The target is achieved a year early. And then the chain begins.

Piece #2333: The Chain

Canada, February 1991. Bank of England, October 1992 — not from research but from the wreckage of Black Wednesday, the humiliation of the Exchange Rate Mechanism collapse. Sweden, 1993. Australia, 1993. The ECB, 1998, initially defining price stability as "below 2%," then clarifying in 2003 to "below, but close to, 2%," then in July 2021 finally adopting a symmetric two percent target. Thomas listed the dates and watched the pattern: each adopter cited the precedent, not the origin. Canada cited New Zealand. England cited Canada. Sweden cited all three. By the time Japan adopted in January 2013, under Abenomics, thirty countries had already converged. The question was no longer "why 2?" but "why would you do anything different from what everyone else already does?" Convergence performed the derivation. The fact that all roads led to the same number performed the discovery that the number was a natural constant. It was not a constant. It was a television interview rounded up.

Piece #2334: The Secret

At the July 2-3, 1996 FOMC meeting, the Federal Reserve privately settled on two percent. Thomas read the transcript. Greenspan wanted zero — genuine price stability, if inflation were "properly measured." Janet Yellen argued for positive inflation: the grease that lets real wages adjust downward without requiring the nominal wage cuts that workers resist. The committee converged on two. Then Greenspan said the words Thomas underlined: "I will tell you that if the 2 percent inflation figure gets out of this room, it is going to create more problems for us than I think any of you might anticipate." The target remained secret for sixteen years. The most powerful central bank in the world operated under a number it could not speak aloud. On January 25, 2012, Ben Bernanke made the secret public. Yellen chaired the subcommittee that drafted the announcement. The Fed was among the last major central banks to formally adopt what was effectively a consensus number — twenty-three years after New Zealand, sixteen years after its own members had secretly agreed. The announcement performed the derivation. The deliberation performed the rigor. Neither produced the number. The number came from a television interview on April Fools' Day.

Piece #2335: The Anchor

Thomas stood back and looked at the full arc. Gold standard, 1880-1914: 0.1% average annual inflation, extreme short-run volatility. The anchor was physical — convertibility to metal. Bretton Woods collapses, 1971. No anchor. The Great Inflation: 10%+. Then the synthetic anchor: 2%, from 1990 onward. A number does the work the metal used to do. But no economic research has identified 2% as optimal. The arguments — measurement bias, deflation buffer, zero lower bound cushion — explain why a small positive number is better than zero. They do not explain why two rather than three or four. Olivier Blanchard argued for four percent in 2010. Laurence Ball published the formal case in 2014. The counterargument is not evidence for two but fear: changing the target would "unanchor" expectations. The anchor performs the stability. Questioning the anchor destabilizes the performance. Thomas thought of gold. Gold was a terrible anchor — the coefficient of variation for inflation under the gold standard was 17.0, versus 0.88 for the postwar era. But gold was a physical constraint. Two percent is a social constraint — enforced through credibility, expectation, and the weight of thirty-plus institutions that have all agreed. The improvisation is stronger than the metal. The chain is the anchor.
