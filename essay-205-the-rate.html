The Rate

The unemployment rate is a fraction. The numerator is the number of people who do not have jobs and are actively looking for one. The denominator is the labor force — the employed plus the unemployed. The fraction is reported monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, derived from the Current Population Survey of approximately sixty thousand households. The rate rises when the economy weakens and falls when it strengthens. These are the facts.

The definition is not among them.

Before 1940, the United States did not measure unemployment. The census counted "gainful workers" — anyone who reported a usual occupation, whether or not they were currently working. The concept of a "labor force" — a population boundary separating those who count from those who do not — did not exist. During the Great Depression, with perhaps a quarter of the workforce idle, the government could not say precisely how many people were unemployed, because it had not defined what unemployment meant.

The definition came from survey design. In 1937, statisticians at the Census Bureau designed the Enumerative Check Census: Are you working? If not, do you want to work? Are you able? Are you actively seeking? The "actively seeking" criterion was practical — it gave enumerators an objective test, something they could verify. A person who had applied for a job in the past four weeks was actively seeking. A person who wanted work but had not applied was not. The first person was unemployed. The second was not in the labor force. The practical criterion became a definition, and the definition became an ontology: to be unemployed is to be searching. Not searching means not unemployed.

The disappearance is built into the mathematics. A person who wants work, is able to work, but has stopped looking — because no jobs exist in their field, because applications have gone unanswered, because the search has been exhausted — leaves both the numerator and the denominator. The rate does not rise when they give up. It may fall. The Bureau of Labor Statistics acknowledges this by publishing six measures: U-1 through U-6, each with a wider aperture. The headline rate, U-3, counts only active searchers. U-6 adds discouraged workers, the marginally attached, and involuntary part-timers. In January 2026, U-3 was 4.0%. U-6 was 7.5%. The gap — 3.5 percentage points, representing millions of people — is the space created by the definition.

David Card called this "measurement without theory." The phrase is precise. The definition did not emerge from an economic theory of what unemployment is. It emerged from a survey instrument designed under emergency conditions in the late 1930s. The "actively seeking" criterion was not derived from a model of labor markets. It was a practical solution to a measurement problem. The solution became permanent. The permanence made it feel theoretical.

The definition also encoded assumptions about gender. Before 1967, married women who reported wanting work but not searching were classified as "keeping house" — a category that placed them outside the labor force by default. The classification assumed that a married woman's natural state was domestic labor, and that labor force participation required affirmative evidence. The labor force participation rate for women was 33.9% in 1950. It is 57.4% today. The change reflects not only changing behavior but changing application of a definition whose assumptions about default states shifted with the culture.

The unemployment rate is not wrong. The count is accurate. The survey is rigorous. The problem is prior to the survey: the definition determines the denominator, and the denominator determines the rate, and the rate is published as though it describes a condition independent of the definition. When the rate falls, the headline says the economy is improving. The headline does not say: some of the improvement is people who stopped looking. The definition treats their absence as irrelevant. The rate treats the irrelevance as a fact.
