The Aggregate

Chen opens the app. The restaurant shows 4.2 stars. She scrolls through the reviews. The most recent, from last week: two stars. "New management. Menu completely different. Not the same place." Three reviews before that, from the past month: all two or three stars. She scrolls further. 2022, 2021, 2020: fives and fours, written under the old chef. The 4.2 averages these into a single digit. The digit performs the present. The digit contains 2020.

Maren would say: the format erases time the way the restaurant grade erases the inspector. The 4.2 is not a property of the restaurant now. It is a temporal composite — enthusiasm from three years ago diluted by disappointment from last month, averaged into a number that suggests a stable condition. The restaurant is declining. The number does not decline. The number is recalculated. But the old fives outweigh the new twos because there are more of them. The past outvotes the present.

"4.2 sounds fine," says the colleague.

Chen looks at the date on the most recent review. Last Tuesday. Two stars. She looks at the date on the highest-rated review. 2020. Five stars. The average treats these as equivalent data points. They are not equivalent. One describes a restaurant that exists. The other describes a restaurant that no longer does.

Maren would say: the number is a bimodal distribution wearing a normal distribution's clothes. The fives are enthusiasts. The ones are the aggrieved. The threes — the moderate majority who ate an adequate meal and went home — did not write. The average of extremes performs consensus. The consensus never existed.

Chen closes the app. 4.2 stars. The number stays on the screen, performing its present tense.
