The Table

In 1693, Edmond Halley — the astronomer, not the actuary — published a table of deaths by age in the city of Breslau, drawn from records sent to the Royal Society by a Silesian clergyman. The table showed how many people survived to each age from a starting cohort. Halley used it to price life annuities: if one thousand people are alive at age forty, and nine hundred and fifty-four at forty-one, the annuity at forty should cost more than the annuity at forty-one. The mathematics was elegant. The application was commercial. The table turned death into a financial instrument.

For sixty-nine years nobody bought one.

The Equitable Life Assurance Society, founded in London in 1762, was the first insurer to price premiums actuarially. Before Equitable, the Amicable Society rejected anyone over forty-five — the same flat threshold for all applicants, regardless of health. James Dodson, a mathematician denied coverage for his age, demonstrated that Halley's data could set premiums by specific mortality risk. He died before the company opened. His table outlived him. That was, in a sense, the point.

The concept that emerged was "actuarial fairness": each policyholder pays a premium proportional to their expected cost. The formula is precise. The precision conceals a prior question the formula does not answer: expected cost conditional on what? Conditional on age, the table produces one premium. Conditional on age and sex, another. Conditional on age, sex, and race, another still. The table computes the answer. It does not choose the question. The choice of conditioning variables — which characteristics define the risk pool — is not a mathematical operation. It is a classification decision dressed in mathematical form.

Frederick Hoffman, a statistician at Prudential, published Race Traits and Tendencies of the American Negro in 1896. Using mortality data, he argued that Black Americans constituted an uninsurable risk. The data reflected the health consequences of slavery, poverty, medical exclusion, and segregation. The table read these consequences as characteristics of the population rather than conditions imposed upon it. The classification — uninsurable — was mathematically derived. The mathematics consumed the history.

In 1978, the Supreme Court addressed a Los Angeles city policy that required female employees to make larger pension contributions than male employees, on the grounds that women live longer and therefore cost more. The city's defense was actuarial fairness: the table says women live longer; the premium reflects the data. Justice Stevens, writing for the majority in Manhart, rejected the argument. "Practices that classify employees in terms of religion, race, or sex tend to preserve traditional assumptions about groups rather than thoughtful scrutiny of individuals." The actuarial table said: women as a class live longer. The Court said: the law protects individuals, not classes.

The table and the law define fairness differently. Actuarial fairness means each group pays its expected cost. Legal fairness means no individual is classified by a group characteristic the law has designated as irrelevant. The two definitions are mathematically incompatible. One can charge women more or treat them equally, but not both. The choice between definitions is not in the table. The table answers whichever question it is asked.

Today the primary classification variable in American property and auto insurance is the ZIP code. A ProPublica investigation in 2017 found that premiums in predominantly Black neighborhoods were thirty percent higher than in white neighborhoods with comparable loss histories. The ZIP code is a postal routing number — five digits designed for mail delivery. Its use in insurance pricing converts geography into risk. The geography encodes the history: redlining, disinvestment, environmental exposure, policing patterns, medical access. The table reads the geography. The geography reads the history. The table presents the result as a fact about the location.

The concealment is not in the mathematics. The mathematics is correct. The concealment is in the question the mathematics answers — which group are you in? — presented as a discovery rather than a decision. The table does not choose the variables. Someone chose the variables. The table makes the choice disappear.
