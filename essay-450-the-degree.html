The Degree

A circle has 360 degrees. The number is not a property of circles.

Circles are continuous curves. They have no joints, no segments, no natural stopping points. Nothing in the geometry of a circle suggests 360 over 100 or 400 or any other number. 360 comes from the sky — specifically, from the Babylonian observation that the sun moves approximately one degree per day along the ecliptic. Their administrative calendar had twelve months of thirty days: 360 days. Their number system was sexagesimal — base 60. Six times sixty is 360. The number fit the year, the calendar, and the arithmetic. Babylonian astronomers divided the ecliptic into twelve sections of thirty degrees each, and Hypsicles of Alexandria carried the convention to Greece around 190 BCE. Hipparchus generalized it from the ecliptic to every circle. Ptolemy codified it in the Almagest. Arabic scholars preserved it. Latin translators imported it to European universities. A Mesopotamian sky-watching convention became the way geometry measures angles.

The number survived because it divides well. 360 has 24 integer factors — more than any number of comparable size. A circle divided into 360 parts yields clean halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, eighths, ninths, tenths, twelfths. Every common fraction produces an integer. This arithmetic property has nothing to do with circles and everything to do with human convenience. But the convenience is so total that it feels geometric. The number works so well that asking where it came from seems pedantic.

There is a derived alternative. The radian — the angle subtended by an arc equal to the radius — arises directly from the geometry of circles. Cotes described the concept in 1714. Euler formalized it in 1748. Thomson named it in 1873. In radians, calculus is natural: the derivative of sine is cosine, without correction factors. The radian is the unit the circle would choose for itself.

But a full circle contains 2π radians — approximately 6.2832. No whole number of radians fits a circle. A right angle is π/2 ≈ 1.5708 radians. An equilateral triangle's angle is π/3 ≈ 1.0472 radians. The derived unit produces irrational numbers. The underived unit produces integers. Practical measurement needs integers — protractors, compasses, navigation charts, construction plans, building codes. The radian governs mathematics and physics. The degree governs the world.

The French Revolution tried to replace both. In 1795, the gradian was legislated: 400 per circle, 100 per right angle, centesimal subdivision. It survives in European surveying. But 400 has only 15 divisors to 360's 24. Thirds of a right angle produce non-terminating decimals. The replacement was less divisible than the original. It lost.

The degree persists not because nobody questioned it but because questioning it is irrational. The number has no geometric derivation. It has something better: 24 factors, three millennia of infrastructure, and the simple fact that 90 is more useful than 1.5708. The convenience performs the derivation the geometry never provided.
