Part CCCLXXIII: The At Sign

Piece #2306: The Rate

In a 1536 letter from Seville to Rome, the Florentine merchant Francesco Lapi wrote: "a wine @, which is 1/13 of a barrel, is worth 70 or 80 ducats." The symbol was shorthand for amphora — a unit of volume, borrowed from Arabic traders who called the quarter-quintal ar-rubʿ and from whom the Spanish took arroba. The symbol had been circulating for at least a century before Lapi's letter: a 1448 Aragonese customs registry used a similar mark to record wheat. By the eighteenth century, English bookkeepers had adopted it as "at the rate of" — the version that would persist on typewriter keyboards, in ASCII at position 64, and in Unicode as U+0040 COMMERCIAL AT. The name preserves the function. The function has been dead for fifty years. Thomas thought about what it meant for a symbol to carry a name that describes what it no longer does.

Piece #2307: The Separator

Ray Tomlinson needed a character. It was late 1971, and he was adapting a single-machine message program to work across ARPANET, sending text from one DEC-10 to another. The address required two parts: user and host. He needed something between them. The character had to be on the Teletype Model 33 keyboard, had to be absent from usernames, and should ideally carry a useful connotation. He looked at the keyboard and chose @. "At" — user at host. The commercial-rate meaning was irrelevant. The six centuries of wine pricing, wheat accounting, and bookkeeping notation collapsed into a single property: the preposition. Tomlinson sent a message to himself — tomlinson@bbn-tenexa — from one machine to an identical machine sitting next to it, connected only through a network neither machine needed. The first email address. The symbol's second life. Tomlinson could not recall the message's content. The content was not the point. The @ was.

Piece #2308: The Person

On November 2, 2006, Robert Andersen posted on Twitter: "@ buzz - you broke your thumb and you're still twittering?" The platform was four months old. No engineer had designed @mentions. No product spec defined the syntax. A user looked at a symbol that meant "at this address" and used it to mean "to this person." The convention spread. By May 2008, Twitter made @username clickable. By March 2009, the feature was renamed Mentions. Thomas noted the shift: in email, @ is a preposition. User at host. A locative relationship between a name and a place. On Twitter, @ is a prefix. @username is not a name at a place. @username IS the name. The preposition became a definite article. The separator became a constitutive mark. Nobody decided this. Millions of uses decided it. The symbol absorbed the new meaning the way a sponge absorbs water — because the shape was available and the need was present.

Piece #2309: The Animals

In Italian, @ is chiocciola — a snail. In German, Klammeraffe — a spider monkey clinging to a branch. In Russian, sobaka — a dog. In Hungarian, kukac — a worm. In Hebrew, strudel — the pastry. In Greek, papaki — a little duck. In Czech, zavináč — a rolled herring. In Korean, golbaengi — a sea snail. In Swedish, snabel-a — an elephant's trunk. In Thai, wian hua — a spinning head. Thomas listed them. Every culture that encountered @ when email arrived saw a different animal in the curve. None saw an amphora. None saw a rate. None saw a design. The shape preceded the meaning. The shape was universal — a lowercase a enclosed in a spiral, present on every keyboard in the world because ASCII standardized it in 1963 for commercial bookkeeping. The meaning was local, multiple, zoological. A snail, a monkey, a dog, a worm, a duck, a herring. The glyph that meant "at the rate of" in English was named for its body in every other language. The body was the only thing visible. The history was invisible.

Piece #2310: The Museum

In March 2010, Paola Antonelli, Senior Curator of Architecture and Design at the Museum of Modern Art, added @ to the permanent collection. There was no object to acquire. The acquisition was conceptual: the museum was collecting the moment when the symbol changed its meaning. Antonelli called Tomlinson's 1971 choice "a powerful act of design." Thomas thought about that word: design. Tomlinson was looking for an unused character on a keyboard. He picked the available one. The design was retrospective — visible only in the accumulation of consequences that followed from a pragmatic choice. Three hundred and seventy-three Parts. The @ carries three meanings: rate, address, person. The first is invisible. The second is fading. The third is the only one most people have ever known. The succession of pragmatic adoptions — each one borrowing the symbol because of what it already meant, each one adding a meaning the previous adopter never intended — produces a symbol that performs the appearance of deliberate design. It was not designed. It was borrowed, three times, and the borrowing is the design.
