The Protection

The Sun Protection Factor is a ratio: the dose of UV radiation required to produce minimal erythema (visible reddening) on sunscreen-protected skin, divided by the dose on unprotected skin. Franz Greiter developed the concept in the 1960s; the FDA formalized the testing protocol. SPF 30 means the protected skin requires thirty times the UV dose to burn. The number performs linearity — SPF 30 sounds like three times the protection of SPF 10, like twice the protection of SPF 15.

The linearity is in the ratio, not in the protection. SPF 15 blocks approximately 93% of UVB radiation. SPF 30 blocks approximately 97%. SPF 50 blocks approximately 98%. SPF 100 blocks approximately 99%. The progression from SPF 30 to SPF 50 — which the consumer reads as a 67% improvement — reduces transmitted UVB from 3.3% to 2.0%. One percentage point. The number doubles from 50 to 100. The transmitted UVB drops from 2% to 1%. The consumer reads the ratio. The skin receives the percentage. The gap between them widens as the number increases — each additional SPF unit delivers less absolute protection, and the number continues to increment as though each unit were equal.

The SPF on the label is determined at a specific application thickness: 2 mg/cm², corresponding to approximately one ounce for an adult body. Diffey and Grice (Photodermatology, Photoimmunology & Photomedicine, 1997) and Autier et al. (British Journal of Dermatology, 2001) found that consumers apply 0.5 to 1.0 mg/cm² — 25 to 50 percent of the tested amount. The relationship between thickness and protection is not linear. At half the tested thickness, SPF 30 may deliver SPF 5 to 10 in practice. The number on the bottle was determined at a thickness the consumer does not use. The label says 30. The skin receives something substantially less, depending on how much product was applied, how evenly, and how recently.

SPF measures UVB protection — radiation in the 290-320 nm range, primarily responsible for sunburn. It does not measure UVA protection. UVA (320-400 nm) penetrates deeper, causes photoaging, contributes to immunosuppression, and plays a role in melanoma. A sunscreen with high SPF and poor UVA filters blocks the sunburn signal while allowing the deeper damage. The consumer does not burn and infers safety. The absence of the endpoint the SPF measures against becomes the concealment — the burn was prevented, therefore the exposure was managed, but the unburned skin continued to absorb UVA at rates the SPF number cannot describe. The FDA introduced the "Broad Spectrum" label in 2011, requiring proportional UVA protection but providing no numeric rating for it. Europe uses a UVA-in-circle logo. Japan uses the PA system (PA+ through PA++++). The same sunscreen carries different spectral information in different markets.

The test population: FDA protocol specifies 10-20 subjects with Fitzpatrick skin types I through III — fair to medium skin. The endpoint is minimal erythemal dose, determined by visible reddening. For darker skin types, erythema is harder to detect visually. The SPF was calibrated on fair skin's visible burn response and is marketed universally.

Australia capped SPF labeling at 50+ in the 1990s. The European Commission followed. The reasoning: above SPF 50, the marginal improvement is so small that higher numbers mislead consumers and encourage behavioral compensation — longer sun exposure, less frequent reapplication. The FDA proposed a US cap at 50+ in 2011. The proposal was never finalized. American shelves stock SPF 100, SPF 110. The uncapped number performs protection the physics cannot support — SPF 100 blocks 99%, SPF 50 blocks 98%, the difference is 1 percentage point of UVB transmission — and the consumer reads the doubled number and infers doubled protection. A ratio derived from a fair-skinned test panel's sunburn response to UVB only, measured at a thickness consumers do not apply, performing linear improvement through asymptotic physics, uncapped in the country with the largest sunscreen market, labeled on a bottle the consumer reads as a measure of safety.
