Part CCCXXXVII: The Chargemaster

Piece #2126: The Bill

Thomas goes to the hospital. A chest pain, quickly resolved — troponin test, EKG, four hours in the emergency department. Two weeks later, the bill arrives. Forty-seven pages. Each page itemized. Each line: a service, a quantity, a dollar amount.

Troponin test: $199.50. IV start kit: $84.00. Saline bag (1L): $137.00. "Facility fee, ED Level 4": $4,312.00. The numbers are precise. The format is familiar — quantity times unit price equals total. The same format as a hardware store receipt. Nails: 24. Price: $0.12 each. Total: $2.88.

Thomas assumes each number reflects a calculation. Someone computed the cost of performing the troponin test — the reagent, the machine time, the technician's labor, the overhead — and arrived at $199.50. Someone determined that a liter of saline costs $137 to provide.

Medicare reimburses the troponin test at $13.94. The same test. The same hospital. The same day. Ratio: 14 to 1.

Piece #2127: The Fiction

Thomas learns the word. Chargemaster. Charge Description Master. Every hospital maintains one. Twenty thousand to fifty thousand line items. A comprehensive listing of every billable service.

Steven Brill spent seven months reading hospital bills. He published his findings in Time in February 2013: "Bitter Pill." Twenty-six thousand words. He found the same pattern everywhere. Prices "devoid of any calculation related to cost." Universally regarded within the industry as — the word Brill kept hearing from hospital administrators — "fiction."

Thomas asks: if the prices are fiction, why do they exist?

Because fiction has an audience. The insured patient never sees the chargemaster price — the insurance company negotiated it down to 20-40% of listed. Medicare ignores it entirely, paying its own cost-based formula. Medicaid pays even less. The chargemaster price applies in full only to the uninsured. Those without an insurer to negotiate. Those without leverage. Those least able to pay receive the highest price — the fictional one.

The bill is more detailed than honest.

Piece #2128: The Markups

Thomas reads Gerard Anderson's research. Johns Hopkins. Health Affairs. The numbers are exact in the way that absurdity, measured carefully, becomes more absurd:

Average hospital markup across all services: 4.32 times Medicare allowable cost. For-profit hospitals: 6.31 times. Government hospitals: 3.47 times. CT scans: 28.5 times. Anesthesiology: 23.5 times. MRIs: 13.6 times.

Between 1996 and 2017, chargemaster markups increased by 155%. The standard deviation increased by 324%. Prices became simultaneously higher and more arbitrary. The gap between what the bill says and what anything costs widened, and the variation in the gap itself widened. Even the fiction stopped agreeing with itself.

Uwe Reinhardt, Princeton health economist: the system is "chaos behind a veil of secrecy."

Thomas writes: the veil is the itemization itself. The forty-seven pages. The precise dollar amounts. The format that performs cost accounting — each line item reading as "someone calculated this" — is the secrecy. Not despite the detail. Because of it.

Piece #2129: The Transparency

Thomas reads about the fix. CMS Hospital Price Transparency Rule, effective January 1, 2021. Required every hospital to publish machine-readable files of all negotiated rates. A consumer-friendly display of 300 shoppable services.

Four years later: 21% of hospitals fully compliant. The HHS Inspector General audited a sample: 46% not in compliance. CMS conducted 5,149 reviews of 3,764 hospitals. Issued 27 monetary penalties. Twenty-seven. The maximum annual fine: $2,007,500. For hospital systems reporting billions in annual revenue.

Thomas does the arithmetic. Non-compliance cost: up to $2 million per year. Revenue protected by opacity: immeasurable but certainly larger. The penalty structure made transparency optional and opacity rational.

The rule required hospitals to show their prices. The enforcement ensured they didn't have to. The format of the rule performed transparency. The content of the rule — the penalty amounts, the review pace, the compliance definition — performed permission.

Piece #2130: The Definite Article

Thomas sits with his bill. He has learned what it is and what it isn't. It isn't cost accounting. It isn't a calculation. It isn't transparent. It is a negotiating ceiling applied in full to those who cannot negotiate.

But the format. The format is a bill. An itemized invoice. Quantity, description, unit price, total. The format says: the price. Not a price. Not one of six possible prices for the same aspirin. The price. The definite article conceals the marketplace. There is no the price. There is a chargemaster price, a Medicare price, a Medicaid price, a Blue Cross price, an Aetna price, a cash price. The bill presents one — the highest, the most fictional — as if it were the only one.

One hundred and four species. The fourth dollar-format concealment: property tax (dollar encodes date), bail (dollar encodes wealth), tipping (dollar encodes paycheck), chargemaster (dollar encodes negotiating position). Each uses the same format — a dollar amount on paper — to conceal a different function. The dollar sign is the most effective concealment character in the inventory. It performs objectivity, precision, and individual calibration. It performs cost. It is not cost. It was never cost. The prices were here when they got here.
