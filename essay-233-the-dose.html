The Dose

John William Trevan, a pharmacologist at the Wellcome Physiological Research Laboratories in London, published "The Error of Determination of Toxicity" in the Proceedings of the Royal Society B in 1927. He proposed the "median lethal dose" — the dose of a substance that kills 50% of a test population. The concept was statistical: individual organisms varied in susceptibility, so a single lethal dose could not be specified. The dose that killed the most sensitive animal was different from the dose that killed the most resistant. Trevan's solution was the median — the dose at which the population split evenly between living and dead.

The LD50 became the standard metric for acute toxicity worldwide. Safety data sheets, regulatory filings, poison control databases, the Globally Harmonized System of classification — all use LD50 values. Aspirin: LD50 (oral, rat) = 200 mg/kg. Caffeine: LD50 (oral, rat) = 192 mg/kg. Table salt: LD50 (oral, rat) = 3,000 mg/kg. Vitamin D₃: LD50 (oral, rat) = 10 mg/kg. The numbers are printed on sheets used in laboratories, factories, hospitals, shipping facilities. Each describes the outcome for a population of approximately 30 to 100 inbred laboratory rats under controlled conditions. The number is experienced as a property of the substance.

The test population is inbred. Wistar rats, Sprague-Dawley rats, Swiss mice — strains maintained for genetic uniformity. The uniformity reduces variance, which improves statistical precision, which makes the LD50 more reproducible. It also means the population does not represent the genetic diversity of any natural population, including humans. The precision of the number depends on the narrowness of the test population. The applicability of the number to humans depends on the breadth that was removed to achieve the precision.

The LD50 varies by species. Dioxin (2,3,7,8-TCDD): LD50 in guinea pigs is 0.6 μg/kg; in hamsters, 1,157 μg/kg. Nearly 2,000-fold difference for the same molecule in two rodents. Thalidomide is non-teratogenic in rats at any dose, catastrophically teratogenic in humans and rabbits. Penicillin is therapeutic in humans, lethal in guinea pigs. The safety data sheet specifies the species. The extrapolation to humans is implicit — the reader encounters the number and infers relevance to their own body. The FDA uses allometric scaling factors (rat to human = 6.2, mouse to human = 12.3) to estimate equivalent human doses, assuming metabolism scales with body surface area. For many compounds, it does not.

The LD50 depends on the route. Nicotine: LD50 (oral, rat) = 50 mg/kg; LD50 (intravenous, rat) = 0.3 mg/kg. A 167-fold difference for the same compound in the same species by different routes, because absorption, distribution, and first-pass metabolism differ entirely. Safety data sheets typically report multiple routes. The Globally Harmonized System assigns hazard categories based on the most sensitive route. The label — "toxic," "harmful," "fatal" — travels to the shipping container without the route context.

The LD50 measures acute toxicity — what happens from a single exposure within 24 hours. It says nothing about chronic toxicity, sublethal effects, endocrine disruption, carcinogenicity, teratogenicity, neurotoxicity, or bioaccumulation. Asbestos has a very high acute LD50; it kills by decades of fiber accumulation. Lead has a moderate acute LD50; its primary danger is chronic neurodevelopmental impairment at exposures far below acutely lethal levels. Bisphenol A produces endocrine effects at doses thousands of times below its LD50. The metric designed for pharmacological standardization — comparing the potency of drug preparations in Trevan's era — was adopted for environmental health protection without adjusting for the difference between acute drug toxicity and chronic environmental exposure.

Why fifty percent? Trevan chose the median because it occupies the steepest part of the dose-response sigmoid — where small changes in dose produce the largest changes in response. The LD50 is the most precisely determinable point on the curve. The LD1 — the dose lethal to 1% of the population — would be more relevant to safety, but it falls on the shallow tail where measurement uncertainty is large. The number is reported because it can be measured precisely, not because it describes the dose that matters to anyone trying not to die. The fifty in LD50 is a statistical convenience — the optimization of the experimenter's precision — printed on a safety data sheet, read as a threshold of danger, describing the midpoint of a dose-response curve derived from inbred rats of a single strain, by a single route, over 24 hours, at the point where the statistician's confidence interval was smallest.
