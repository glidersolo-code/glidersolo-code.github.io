Fiction

Part CCCXIX: The Premium

Piece #2036: The Bag

Thomas held two bags of coffee. Same origin: Guatemala. Same region: Huehuetenango. Same altitude: 1,500 meters. One bag: $12.99. The other: $17.99 with a Fair Trade seal.

Five dollars separated them. Thomas thought about where the five dollars would go.

The label implied: to the farmer. The seal depicted: a figure, a leaf, a circle of connection. The consumer's premium, traveling directly from shelf to field.

He put both bags back and thought about the journey.

Piece #2037: The Journey

The five dollars would travel from the register to the retailer's margin. The retailer would pass a portion to the roaster. The roaster would pass a portion to the importer. The importer would pass a portion to the cooperative in Guatemala. The cooperative would distribute a portion to its members.

At each step, the premium shrank. Studies documented the attenuation: 1.6% to 18% of the retail premium reaching the farmer. Five dollars becoming — at best — ninety cents. At worst, eight cents.

Thomas thought about the word "fair." Applied not to the system but to the bag. The system remained: $100 billion annually, 10% to producing countries, colonial trade patterns, multinational concentration, commodity exchange pricing that a Guatemalan farmer could not influence.

The bag was fair. The system was not. The label addressed the bag.

Piece #2038: The Conversion

He had seen this move before. In the carbon footprint calculator that converted industrial emissions into personal responsibility. In the recycling symbol that converted a production waste problem into a consumer sorting task.

The pattern: a structural problem, converted into an individual action. The individual action was real — the premium existed, the recycling bin existed, the carbon offset existed. But the structural problem continued behind the individual action, unchanged and, in some cases, enabled by the sense of resolution the individual action provided.

Thomas called it the conversion. Structural → individual. Systemic → transactional. Political → commercial. The label performed the conversion. The consumer completed it by selecting the bag.

Piece #2039: The Cooperative

The cooperative in Huehuetenango had 340 members. Certification cost $2,800 annually. Audit fees, documentation, compliance staff. Of their total production, roughly 30% found a fair trade buyer. The rest was sold at conventional prices. The certification applied to all production. The premium applied to a fraction.

A farmer in the cooperative could not sell to a different buyer — even if the different buyer offered a better price — without losing certification. The system that guaranteed a floor also eliminated the possibility of a ceiling. The farmer traded price discovery for price certainty, and the certainty was modest.

Thomas thought about the word "trade" in "fair trade." The farmer had traded agency for a label. The consumer had traded a structural critique for a bag. Both trades were voluntary. Both trades were constrained.

Piece #2040: The Attribute

Walking home, Thomas passed a window displaying a different certification: Rainforest Alliance. And another: Direct Trade. And another: Single Origin. And another: Ethically Sourced.

Each label converted a structural problem into an attribute the consumer could select. Each attribute performed a resolution. Each resolution was partial, mediated, attenuated — and each was printed on the bag as though it were complete.

The coffee aisle was a gallery of conversions. Every structural injustice — labor exploitation, environmental degradation, colonial pricing, climate vulnerability — had been transformed into a product characteristic. The consumer navigated the injustices by selecting attributes. The attributes absorbed the structures. The bags absorbed the politics.

Thomas drank his coffee and tasted nothing structural. The beans were good. The premium had traveled its journey. The dime had arrived. The system continued.
