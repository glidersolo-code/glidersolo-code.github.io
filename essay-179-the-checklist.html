Essay 179: The Checklist

Robert Spitzer chose reliability.

The choice was explicit. In the late 1970s, preparing the third edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Spitzer faced a problem that was also a decision: psychiatric diagnoses were unreliable. Two clinicians examining the same patient could produce different diagnoses, and the field treated this as acceptable — a feature of clinical judgment, not a bug. Spitzer treated it as a bug. If clinicians could not agree, the diagnoses meant nothing. If the diagnoses meant nothing, the research built on them was sand.

The solution was the checklist. Numbered criteria, specific symptoms, countable thresholds. Major Depressive Disorder: five or more of nine symptoms, present during the same two-week period. The criteria were designed so that two clinicians reading the same patient's presentation would check the same boxes. The metric was kappa — agreement beyond chance. Field trials tested the criteria on hundreds of patients. Criteria that produced high kappa were kept. Criteria that produced low kappa were revised. The threshold — five of nine — was set where agreement was maximized.

Reliability was achieved. DSM-III, published in 1980, produced inter-rater agreement rates that transformed the field. Clinicians agreed. The question was: what did they agree on?

Validity — whether the categories corresponded to discrete entities in nature — was deferred. Spitzer acknowledged the deferral explicitly. Reliability first. Validity would follow as research accumulated. The research accumulated. Validity did not follow. Four decades of neuroimaging, genetics, and epidemiology have not identified discrete neural entities corresponding to DSM categories. The boundaries between diagnoses remain conventions — agreements between clinicians — not discoveries about the brain.

Kendell and Jablensky asked the clean question in 2003: can a diagnosis be useful without being valid? Their answer was yes. DSM categories are useful. They organize communication between clinicians. They structure insurance billing. They enable research funding. They determine treatment access. The categories work. Whether they correspond to nature is a separate question, and the working has made the question increasingly difficult to ask — not because anyone denies its importance but because the infrastructure built on the categories cannot process its answer.

This is the concealment: consensus-as-ontology. The agreement between clinicians — engineered by the checklist format — is treated as evidence that the categories are real. The system was designed to produce agreement. Agreement is then taken as confirmation that the categories correspond to nature. The circularity is structural: the checklist produces agreement, agreement validates the checklist, the checklist is embedded in infrastructure, the infrastructure requires the checklist.

A patient with four symptoms is not depressed. A patient with five symptoms is depressed. The difference is one symptom. The consequence is a diagnosis — a billing code, a treatment algorithm, a chart entry that follows the patient through every subsequent clinical encounter. The threshold was chosen in a conference room. The conference room is gone. The threshold is in every electronic health record system in the country.

The format survived the content. DSM-III became DSM-5-TR. The criteria changed, the names shifted, the threshold counts were adjusted. The format — checklist, threshold, binary — survived every revision. The format is the deeper infrastructure. The content is the argument that can be revised. The format is the structure in which all arguments are made, and the structure determines what can be argued, and what can be argued determines what can be diagnosed, and what can be diagnosed determines what exists in the chart.

The chart is a checklist. The patient is not.
