The Shell

A foraminiferan is a single-celled organism that builds a shell of calcium carbonate. The shell sinks to the ocean floor when the organism dies. Millions of shells accumulate over millennia, forming layers of sediment that record the chemistry of the water in which they lived. The ratio of two oxygen isotopes — ¹⁸O and ¹⁶O — in the shell depends on the temperature of the water. Colder water: more ¹⁸O incorporated. Warmer water: less. Drill a core from the deep ocean floor, measure the oxygen isotope ratio down the column, and the wiggles trace a history of temperature changes over millions of years.

This is the story as Cesare Emiliani told it in 1955, when he published "Pleistocene Temperatures" in the Journal of Geology. He measured the δ¹⁸O of foraminifera from Caribbean sediment cores and identified alternating warm and cold stages — the ice ages and interglacials of the Pleistocene, written in the shells of microscopic organisms.

The story was incomplete. In 1967, Nicholas Shackleton demonstrated that the δ¹⁸O signal in benthic foraminifera — those living on the ocean floor, in water whose temperature barely changes — still showed the same oscillations. The wiggles could not be temperature. They were ice volume. When continental ice sheets grow, they preferentially lock up ¹⁶O — the lighter isotope evaporates more readily from the ocean, falls as snow on land, and is trapped in ice. The remaining ocean water becomes enriched in ¹⁸O. The shells record that enrichment. The signal is a composite: part temperature, part ice volume, part local salinity, part biological fractionation by the organism itself.

The proxy was not a thermometer. The proxy was a multi-channel recording played through a single speaker.

Separating the channels required independent measurements. The Mg/Ca ratio in foraminifera shells responds to temperature but not ice volume, allowing the temperature component to be isolated — approximately. The uncertainties are ±1-2°C. During some intervals, the uncertainty is comparable to the signal. The measurement knows something. The measurement does not know precisely what.

From this composite signal, the Marine Isotope Stage system was constructed. MIS 1 is the Holocene. MIS 2 is the Last Glacial Maximum. MIS 5e is the last interglacial. The numbering is odd for warm periods, even for cold. The MIS framework replaced the old glacial nomenclature — Günz, Mindel, Riss, Würm — that was based on Alpine moraine sequences. The framework now organizes the entire Quaternary. It is the chronological backbone of global paleoclimate research, correlating events across oceans and continents by matching their isotope curves.

The correlation assumes global synchroneity. The δ¹⁸O signal for ice volume is approximately synchronous — when ice sheets grow, the entire ocean's isotopic composition shifts. But the local temperature and salinity components are not synchronous. Regional leads and lags of hundreds to thousands of years can exist. The framework, by correlating records to a single reference curve, smooths out the very asynchronies that contain information about how climate change propagates.

The standard against which all measurements are compared — PDB, derived from a Cretaceous belemnite fossil — was physically exhausted in the 1980s. The replacement, VPDB, is calibrated against a secondary reference material. The absolute zero point of the measurement system is an artifact that no longer exists.

The shell on the ocean floor recorded everything that happened to the water around it. The measurement system extracted one signal from the recording and called it temperature. The correction extracted another signal and called it ice volume. The framework organized both into a global chronology. At every step, the multi-dimensional became one-dimensional, and the one-dimensional became the infrastructure through which all subsequent observations were interpreted.
