The Sprayer

The herbicide label says: mix one ounce per gallon, apply at one gallon per thousand square feet. Three numbers. The consumer reads a closed system — measure, mix, walk.

The system opens the moment the trigger does.

The tank marking is molded into polyethylene during manufacturing. The actual volume may differ from the marking by 10 to 15 percent. A tank marked "1 gallon" that holds 1.15 gallons dilutes the concentrate before the first spray. The gallon is a feature of the mold, not a measurement of the vessel.

Pressure begins to decay the instant the nozzle opens. A hand-pump sprayer reaches 40 to 60 PSI at full pressurization. At 40 PSI the nozzle delivers a wide fan at 0.4 gallons per minute. Seconds later — 30 PSI — the flow slows, the droplets enlarge, the fan narrows. The consumer who pumps once and walks is applying a decreasing volume to every successive square foot of lawn. The beginning of the pass and the end of the pass receive different doses from the same sprayer in the same minute. The label's rate assumes constant pressure. The physics of a sealed tank with a shrinking air volume ensures the pressure is never constant.

Walking speed determines the actual coverage rate. Two miles per hour delivers twice the volume per square foot as four miles per hour over the same area. Nozzle height determines swath width — higher means wider but thinner coverage. Neither variable appears on the label. Both are set by the consumer's body: stride length, arm fatigue, the decision to rush the last strip before the tank runs out.

A Nebraska study found that two-thirds of applicators applied pesticides at rates with errors exceeding 5 percent. The calibration protocol exists — extension services at Ohio State, Colorado State, and Penn State publish step-by-step guides: spray into a container over a timed pass at a measured distance, calculate output per thousand square feet, adjust. The protocol converts the body into a calibrated instrument. Almost no consumer performs the protocol. The label provides the three numbers and the three numbers look complete.

The format is the closure. Three numbers on a label perform as a system because three numbers feel like enough — a ratio, a rate, a quantity. The five variables the three numbers do not capture (tank tolerance, pressure decay, walking speed, nozzle height, arm fatigue) are invisible not because they are hidden but because the three numbers on the label already look like the whole answer. The stripe across the lawn — where the first pass killed the clover and the second pass merely yellowed it — is the gap between the label's system and the yard's.
