The Metaphor

Joseph J. Schildkraut published "The Catecholamine Hypothesis of Affective Disorders" in the American Journal of Psychiatry in 1965. The logic ran backward: drugs that affect monoamines seem to help depression, therefore depression might be caused by monoamine deficiency. Schildkraut wrote: "A rigorous extrapolation from pharmacological studies to pathophysiology clearly cannot be made."

The qualification did not survive.

Alec Coppen shifted the focus to serotonin in the British Journal of Psychiatry (1967). Also cautious. Also provisional. The caution would not be quoted. The hypothesis would be.

But the phrase that conquered was not "the serotonin hypothesis." The phrase was "chemical imbalance." Two words, neither scientific. "Chemical" — vague enough to mean neurotransmitter without requiring the listener to know what a neurotransmitter is. "Imbalance" — implying a known correct balance, a measured deviation, and a possible correction. Three claims embedded in one word. Zero established by research.

No patient was ever tested for a chemical imbalance. No assay existed. No baseline was defined. The phrase performed a diagnostic measurement that was never performed.

In 1997, the FDA issued guidance making broadcast direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical advertising practical. DTC spending rose from $360 million in 1995 to $1.3 billion in 1998. Pfizer's Zoloft advertisement — the animated "sad blob" — stated: "Depression is a serious medical condition that may be due to a chemical imbalance, and Zoloft works to correct this imbalance." GlaxoSmithKline's Paxil website compared serotonin balance to a cake recipe. In May 2003, the Irish Medicines Board banned GlaxoSmithKline from claiming paroxetine corrects a chemical imbalance.

SSRIs block serotonin reuptake within hours. Therapeutic effects require two to four weeks. If depression were simply low serotonin corrected by raising serotonin, the delay is unexplained. Proposed actual mechanisms — receptor desensitization, BDNF-mediated neuroplasticity, synaptic restructuring — correspond to the timeline but not to the metaphor.

Moncrieff and colleagues published the umbrella review in Molecular Psychiatry (2022). Six domains of evidence. Conclusion: "There is no consistent evidence of there being an association between serotonin and depression." Tryptophan depletion did not produce depression in 566 healthy volunteers. The largest genetic studies found no serotonin gene differences.

Surveys find 85-90% of the public believes depression is caused by a chemical imbalance. The metaphor survived its own refutation because the metaphor was more useful than the correction. "Your brain chemistry is off and this pill fixes it" is actionable. "Depression involves complex interactions among genetics, environment, neural circuits, inflammation, and psychosocial factors, and we don't fully understand why SSRIs sometimes help" is accurate.

The metaphor performed a mechanism. Not a number that performed a rule. Not a shape that performed a hierarchy. A figure of speech that performed causality — implying measurement, baseline, deviation, and correction without any of these existing. The advertising was the distribution channel. The 90% belief was the market share. The refutation was the correction that arrived too late, in a format too complex, for a metaphor too useful.
