The Octane

They were looking at a gas pump. Three numbers on yellow labels. 87. 89. 93.

"Octane rating," Maren said. "Higher is better."

"Higher is more resistant to knocking. In an engine designed for 87, using 93 provides no additional power, no improved efficiency, no benefit of any kind. The two fuels contain the same energy — approximately 120,000 BTU per gallon. The higher number means the fuel resists premature detonation more effectively. In an engine that does not prematurely detonate on 87, the additional resistance is unused."

Chen's paragraph was about the test engine. The Cooperative Fuel Research engine, designed in 1929, is a single-cylinder variable-compression-ratio engine with a bore of 82.55 mm and a stroke of 114.3 mm. The design is ninety-seven years old. It is still the standard instrument. Two test methods: ASTM D2699 at 600 rpm and 52°C (Research Octane Number), ASTM D2700 at 900 rpm and 149°C (Motor Octane Number). The same fuel produces different numbers under each method. The United States displays neither — it displays the average, the Anti-Knock Index. Europe displays the RON. The same gasoline carries different numbers on different continents.

"So 87 here is 91 there."

"Same molecules. Different display convention. And the reference fuels — isooctane defined as 100, n-heptane defined as 0 — were chosen by Thomas Midgley Jr. at General Motors in the 1920s. The same Midgley who solved the knocking problem by adding tetraethyl lead to gasoline. The octane number during the leaded era measured knock resistance without measuring the neurotoxin that produced the resistance. The number on the pump in 1970 and the number on the pump in 2000 were the same measurement. The chemistry behind them had changed completely."

"And the word 'premium' —"

"The word says quality. The number says knock resistance. The consumer reads both and infers: better fuel. The Federal Trade Commission has stated explicitly that higher octane provides no benefit in engines not designed for it. Approximately sixteen percent of US gasoline sales are premium. A significant proportion of those vehicles require regular."

The paragraph sat at 0.8. It was, like an octane number, a measurement that appeared to describe a property of the substance while describing a property of the test — which engine, which speed, which temperature, which averaging convention, which country's display standard — the three digits performing the precision of a property of the fuel while encoding a 1920s test engine, a country-specific average, and a word that implied quality where the number measured only resistance.
