The Magnitude

Essay 165

Charles F. Richter published "An instrumental earthquake magnitude scale" in the Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America in 1935 — volume 25, pages 1 to 32. The scale was local: designed for Southern California earthquakes, calibrated to the Wood-Anderson torsion seismometer, a specific instrument with specific response characteristics. Richter measured the logarithm of the maximum amplitude recorded at a standard distance of 100 kilometers. The local magnitude, ML, was a single number derived from a single peak on a single instrument type. Richter, working with Beno Gutenberg at the California Institute of Technology, later developed extensions — the surface-wave magnitude (MS) and body-wave magnitude (mb) — to handle larger and more distant earthquakes. But the original local magnitude, and the name "Richter scale," entered public consciousness as the way earthquake size is measured.

The scale saturates. Above approximately magnitude 7, the relationship between earthquake size and ML breaks down. Surface waves — the seismic waves that ML measures — have a characteristic period, and very large earthquakes release their energy predominantly at longer periods that surface-wave measurements cannot capture. A magnitude 7.5 earthquake and a magnitude 9.0 earthquake can produce similar surface-wave amplitudes at the periods ML samples. The scale reaches a ceiling imposed not by the earthquake but by the property the instrument measures. The saturation was recognized by seismologists within decades of the scale's publication. It was a known limitation, not a discovered flaw.

In 1977, Hiroo Kanamori at Caltech developed the moment magnitude scale, publishing "The energy release in great earthquakes" in the Journal of Geophysical Research — volume 82, pages 2981 to 2987. In 1979, Thomas Hanks and Kanamori formalized the scale in "A moment magnitude scale" (Journal of Geophysical Research 84(B5):2348-2350). Moment magnitude, Mw, is calculated from the seismic moment — the product of the fault's rupture area, the average displacement across the fault, and the shear modulus of the surrounding rock. It measures the total energy released by the earthquake, not just the amplitude of one wave type at one period. It does not saturate. Mw clearly distinguishes a magnitude 7 from a magnitude 9 because the seismic moment captures the geometry of the fault rupture — its size, its movement, its resistance — rather than just the signal it generates at a particular frequency.

Modern seismology uses moment magnitude almost exclusively for earthquakes above approximately magnitude 4. The United States Geological Survey reports Mw. The European-Mediterranean Seismological Centre reports Mw. The Global Centroid Moment Tensor catalog uses seismic moment. The replacement happened in scientific practice by the 1980s. The Richter scale is not used for significant earthquake reporting by any major seismological organization.

The media still says "Richter scale." The public still hears "Richter scale." When a major earthquake occurs, news reports frequently state the magnitude "on the Richter scale." The number they cite — 7.0, 8.1, 9.0 — is almost always moment magnitude. The name is Richter's. The method is Kanamori's. The public hears the name and applies the conceptual framework of the original method — a simple amplitude measurement on a specific instrument — to a number produced by a fundamentally different method. The two scales agree approximately for moderate earthquakes (roughly magnitude 3 to 7, where ML and Mw produce similar values). They diverge for large earthquakes, which are precisely the earthquakes the media reports on. The 2011 Tohoku earthquake was Mw 9.1. Its ML would have been significantly lower. The number the public received was Mw. The name the public received was ML. The gap between name and method is invisible to the user.

A second concealment operates in the scale's mathematical structure. Magnitude is logarithmic: each whole number increase represents a tenfold increase in measured amplitude and approximately a 31.6-fold increase in released energy. The public treats magnitude as linear. A 7.0 and an 8.0 feel "one unit apart." The 8.0 released 31.6 times more energy. A 9.0 released approximately 1,000 times more energy than a 7.0. The intuition that "one unit" means "one unit of difference" is a natural consequence of how humans process numbers — linearly, unless explicitly trained otherwise. The logarithmic structure is not hidden. It is explained in every seismology textbook and every earthquake preparedness document. It remains invisible to most users because the number appears as a number, and numbers are, by default, linear to the untrained mind.

Three concealment mechanisms. Nominal-persistence: the name "Richter scale" persists in public and media use after the method it names has been replaced in scientific practice. The name implies the original method's properties (amplitude-based, local, saturating). The actual number comes from a replacement method with different properties (moment-based, global, non-saturating). The name conceals the replacement, and the replacement goes unnoticed because the numbers agree in the range most commonly experienced (moderate earthquakes). This is distinct from era-locked calibration (where the old method still is used) and from infrastructure-absorbs-error (where the old method is embedded in systems). Here, the old method was genuinely replaced; only its name survived, carrying the wrong conceptual framework into every news report. Era-locked calibration: Richter's 1935 scale was calibrated for Southern California earthquakes on Wood-Anderson seismometers. Neither the instrument nor the regional specificity survived into modern practice, but the scale's name, number range, and logarithmic structure defined the public's conceptual framework for earthquake measurement. Infrastructure-absorbs-error: not in the scientific infrastructure, which did adopt Mw, but in the media infrastructure — editorial conventions, headline templates, public communication guides — that continues to use "Richter scale" because the phrase is recognized, established, and would require coordinated re-education to replace. Twenty-fifth concealment species: nominal-persistence. First seismology entry.
