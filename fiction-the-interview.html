Fiction The Interview

Fiction

Part CDXL: The Conversation

Piece #2641: The Handshake

Thomas shakes the interviewer's hand. The interviewer smiles. Thomas smiles. In the first 10 seconds, before Thomas has answered a single question, before Thomas has demonstrated a single competence, the interviewer has formed an impression.

Barrick, Swider, and Stewart, 2010: initial evaluations in the first moments of the interview significantly predict final ratings. The remaining 50 minutes do not test the impression — they confirm it. The conversation is 10 seconds of impression formation followed by 50 minutes of confirmation bias.

Thomas does not know this. The interviewer does not know this. Both believe the conversation is an assessment.

Piece #2642: The Validity

Thomas reads Schmidt and Hunter's meta-analysis. Eighty-five years of research on selection methods. The unstructured interview: r = 0.20. General mental ability tests: r = 0.51. Structured interviews: r = 0.51. Work sample tests: r = 0.54.

Thomas considers: r = 0.20 means the interview explains approximately 4% of the variance in job performance. The remaining 96% is unrelated to anything that happened in the conversation. The conversation that felt like the most important hour of the hiring process predicted almost nothing about the work that would follow.

The feeling of assessment is the mechanism. The interviewer believes they understand the candidate because the conversation feels revelatory. The feeling has no predictive validity. The feeling has total persuasive force.

Piece #2643: The Bias

Thomas reads about what the conversation actually measures.

Similar-to-me effects: interviewers rate candidates who resemble them more highly — same background, same interests, same communication style. Attractiveness bias: meta-analytic effect size d = 0.37 favoring attractive candidates. Name-based discrimination: Bertrand and Mulligan sent identical resumes with different names. "White-sounding" names received 50% more callbacks.

The conversation performs neutrality. The interviewer asks the same questions of every candidate. The interviewer evaluates the answers. The evaluation is professional judgment. The professional judgment encodes demographic preference through the conversational format — who is articulate, who is confident, who feels like "a good fit." The format is the bias. The bias is the judgment.

Piece #2644: The Resistance

Thomas reads about the alternative. Structured interviews: standardized questions derived from job analysis, behavioral anchors, numerical rating rubrics, multiple interviewers scoring independently. The structured approach achieves r = 0.51 — 155% improvement.

Interviewers resist structured approaches. The structure feels cold. Mechanical. Inhuman. The interviewers want to talk to the candidate. The interviewers want to feel the interaction. The feeling of understanding the candidate — which has no predictive validity — is what interviewers will not surrender.

The warmth of the conversation is the mechanism of its failure. The conversation works as a social exchange. The conversation fails as an assessment. The failure IS the warmth. Removing the warmth — adding structure, standardization, rubrics — removes the failure. The interviewer resists the removal because the warmth is what makes the interview feel like an interview.

Piece #2645: The Hire

Thomas is hired. The interviewer felt confident. Thomas was articulate, prepared, personable. The conversation lasted 60 minutes. The interviewer's feeling had a predictive validity of r = 0.20.

Thomas will perform well or poorly at the job. The performance will be attributed to the hire. The hire will be attributed to the interview. The interview was a 60-minute conversation in which the first 10 seconds determined the outcome, the remaining 50 minutes confirmed the impression, and the feeling of assessment replaced the assessment. Thomas was hired because of how a conversation felt. The conversation was the assessment. The assessment was the conversation.
