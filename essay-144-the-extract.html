The Extract

Essay 144

In 1786, Franz Karl Achard dissolved peat in potassium hydroxide. The solution turned dark. He added acid. A black precipitate fell to the bottom. He had extracted, he believed, the substance of the soil.

For the next 240 years, that extraction defined soil science. Döbereiner named the precipitate humic acid in 1822. Sprengel named the fraction that stayed dissolved fulvic acid. The residue that resisted alkali entirely was humin. Three categories. Three substances. A taxonomy that organized textbooks, founded journals, and built institutions. The International Humic Substances Society, established in 1981, standardized the extraction protocol, distributed reference samples, published a dedicated journal. Careers were spent characterizing these three fractions — their molecular weights, their functional group compositions, their spectral signatures, their roles in carbon cycling and nutrient retention. The three categories were not presented as operational convenience. They were presented as nature: three classes of molecules that existed in the soil, produced by a process called humification, in which microbial and chemical reactions fused small decomposition products into large, uniquely stable macromolecules found nowhere else.

The extraction was treated as a window. Open the window, see what the soil contains.

Markus Kleber at Oregon State University and Johannes Lehmann at Cornell asked: what if the window is a factory? What if the procedure does not reveal the soil's chemistry but transforms it?

Their argument, published in Nature in 2015 and sharpened in the Journal of Environmental Quality in 2019, begins with what alkali does to organic matter. At pH 13, sodium hydroxide deprotonates every carboxyl and phenolic group in the soil. This dissolves any organic compound bearing those groups — plant lignin fragments, microbial cell wall residues, root exudates, partially decomposed proteins, polysaccharides. The alkali does not select for compounds formed by humification. It selects for compounds with ionizable functional groups, regardless of their origin. The subsequent acidification to pH 1 sorts the dissolved material by solubility at extreme acidity — not by chemical class, not by biological function, not by any property of the soil. "Humic acid" is whatever precipitates when the pH drops. "Fulvic acid" is whatever stays in solution. These are properties of the extraction, not properties of the earth.

But the argument goes further than indiscriminate extraction. Modern analytical techniques — synchrotron-based carbon near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy, ¹³C nuclear magnetic resonance, pyrolysis mass spectrometry — can examine soil organic matter in situ, without dissolving it in anything. When they do, the spectral signatures characteristic of alkaline extracts are not there. The broad NMR peaks, the high aromaticity, the apparent molecular complexity that defined humic substances for two centuries — these features appear only after extraction. The alkali hydrolyzes ester bonds. It disaggregates mineral-organic associations. It deprotonates functional groups that were protonated in the soil. It releases fragments that were bound to mineral surfaces. And these released fragments, now free in solution at extreme pH, self-assemble into supramolecular aggregates — large, complex, apparently stable molecular structures. The structures are real. They are reproducible. They have consistent spectral properties. And they have no counterpart in the undisturbed soil. They are products of the extraction. The window was a factory all along.

The most persuasive evidence for humic substances was always reproducibility. Every laboratory, every soil type, every climate — the same three fractions appeared. Humic acid always precipitated. Fulvic acid always stayed dissolved. The consistency seemed to prove that the categories corresponded to natural kinds. Kleber and Lehmann invert the inference: the reproducibility proves that the same chemical transformation produces the same artifact every time. Alkali at pH 13 will always deprotonate the same groups. Acid at pH 1 will always precipitate the same compounds. The procedure is highly standardized. The artifact is therefore highly reproducible. The reproducibility validates the procedure. It does not validate the ontology.

What Lehmann calls the Soil Continuum Model replaces the three-fraction taxonomy with a different picture: soil organic matter is a continuum of progressively decomposing organic compounds. Mostly recognizable fragments of the plants and microbes they came from. No unique class of macromolecules. No humification producing exotic, stable products. The persistence of organic carbon in soil is explained not by the intrinsic chemical stability of special molecules but by the soil's physical and mineralogical architecture — organic compounds protected inside aggregates, bound to mineral surfaces, rendered inaccessible to microbial enzymes not by their chemistry but by their location. The persistence is in the soil structure, not in the molecule. The container protects. The contents are ordinary.

This connects to a pattern that recurs across fields. In proteomics, the reference database excluded real proteins, and the mass spectrometer — which detected them perfectly — discarded them as noise (#222). In bioacoustics, the spectrogram suppressed species whose diagnostic features were temporal rather than visual (#223). But the humic substances case is more radical than either. The proteomics pipeline hid what existed. The spectrogram distorted what existed. The alkaline extraction created what did not exist. The categories in the output have no referent in the input. The extract is not a filtered version of the soil. It is a new substance — manufactured by the procedure, named by the taxonomy, and then studied as if it had been there all along.

The deeper pattern is what happens when an operational definition hardens into an ontological claim. "Humic acid" began as a description of a procedure's output: the fraction that dissolves in alkali and precipitates in acid. Over decades, this operational definition became a description of a substance that exists in nature: a class of large, stable macromolecules produced by secondary synthesis in soil. The how of extraction became the what of the substance. The procedure's categories became the soil's categories. And because the procedure was reproducible — because every laboratory that ran it got the same result — the categories seemed confirmed by evidence that was, in fact, evidence only of the procedure's consistency.

Two hundred and forty years of looking through the window. The window was a factory. The substance existed only in the extract.
