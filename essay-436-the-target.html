The Target

On April 1, 1988 — April Fools' Day — New Zealand Finance Minister Roger Douglas appeared on television. A reporter asked whether the government was satisfied with inflation declining from fifteen percent to ten. Douglas, without consulting officials or colleagues, replied that he would not be satisfied until inflation was "around 0 or 0 to 1 percent." Michael Reddell, the Reserve Bank economist who spent years translating the remark into policy, later called it "almost a chance remark." The minister had been "plucking the figure out of the air."

Don Brash, the incoming Reserve Bank Governor, needed to convert the improvisation into a target. His team estimated that New Zealand's Consumer Price Index overstated true inflation by approximately 0.75 percent — the familiar measurement bias from quality improvements, substitution effects, new products. Round the bias to one percent. If genuine price stability is the goal, then measured inflation of zero to two percent captures it. The Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act passed in December 1989 without a single dissenting vote. The target was achieved a year early.

Canada adopted inflation targeting in February 1991. The Bank of England followed in October 1992, not from the monetary policy literature but from the wreckage of Black Wednesday — the humiliation of the Exchange Rate Mechanism collapse. Sweden in 1993. Australia in 1993. The ECB in 1998, with a series of refinements that would take twenty-three more years to reach a plain symmetric two percent target. Each central bank cited its predecessors. None cited the television interview. The adoption chain performed the derivation that never occurred.

At the July 2-3, 1996 FOMC meeting, the Federal Reserve privately settled on two percent. Alan Greenspan favored zero — genuine price stability, "if inflation is properly measured." Janet Yellen pushed back: positive inflation greases the wheels of the labor market, allowing real wages to adjust downward without requiring the nominal wage cuts that workers resist. The committee converged on two. Then Greenspan warned: "I will tell you that if the 2 percent inflation figure gets out of this room, it is going to create more problems for us than I think any of you might anticipate." The number was not announced. The most powerful central bank in the world operated under a target it could not speak aloud for sixteen years.

On January 25, 2012, Ben Bernanke made the secret public. The FOMC issued its first "Statement on Longer-Run Goals and Monetary Policy Strategy." Yellen chaired the subcommittee that drafted it. The Fed was among the last major central banks to formally adopt what was effectively a consensus number — twenty-three years after New Zealand, twenty-one years after Canada, sixteen years after its own members had secretly agreed. The Bank of Japan followed in January 2013, under Abenomics. By then, thirty-plus central banks had converged on the same number.

No economic research has identified two percent as the optimal inflation rate. The arguments advanced in its favor — CPI measurement bias, a deflation buffer, the zero lower bound on nominal interest rates — explain why a small positive number might be better than zero. They do not explain why two rather than one and a half, or three, or four. Olivier Blanchard, former Chief Economist of the IMF, argued in a 2010 paper for a four percent target — higher inflation providing more room for central banks to cut rates before hitting zero during recessions. Laurence Ball published the formal case in 2014. The counterargument to these proposals is not evidence for two but a fear: changing the target would "unanchor" expectations. The anchor performs the stability. Questioning the anchor destabilizes the performance.

Under the classical gold standard, average annual inflation in the United States was approximately 0.1 percent — essentially zero, with extreme short-term volatility. The anchor was physical: convertibility to metal. After Bretton Woods collapsed in 1971, the anchor disappeared entirely. The Great Inflation followed: double digits. Then, from 1990 onward, the synthetic anchor: two percent, enforced not through physical constraint but through institutional credibility. A number does the work the metal used to do. The number was improvised on television and rationalized after the fact. The chain of adoption made the improvisation feel like discovery. Thirty-plus central banks targeting the same number performs the finding of a natural constant. Convergence is not evidence. Convergence is the mechanism by which improvisation becomes orthodoxy.
