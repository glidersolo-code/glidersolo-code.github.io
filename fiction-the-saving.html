Part CDXLVI: The Saving

Piece #2671: The Name

Thomas sets his clock forward in March. "Spring forward." The phrase is cheerful. The action is a one-hour forced phase advance of every circadian rhythm in his body.

Thomas considers the name: Daylight Saving Time. Three words. "Daylight" — a natural phenomenon. "Saving" — a beneficial action. "Time" — an objective measure. The name performs a gift. The practice is an imposition. The name made the imposition sound like a finding.

No daylight is saved. The sun does not alter its arc because the clocks changed. The duration of light on this date, at this latitude, is fixed by axial tilt. Thomas has not gained an hour of daylight. Thomas has rescheduled his life by one hour relative to the sun. The rescheduling is called "saving."

Piece #2672: The Origin

Thomas reads William Willett's 1907 pamphlet: "The Waste of Daylight." Willett was a London builder. The argument: evening daylight was being wasted because people were indoors. Advance the clocks. Extend the commercial evening. The word "waste" was the argument. If daylight was being wasted, it must be valuable. If it was valuable, it should be saved.

Germany implemented first — April 30, 1916. Wartime fuel conservation. The military origin disappeared into the seasonal custom. The commercial origin disappeared into the natural-sounding name. What persisted was the name. The name that said daylight was being saved.

Piece #2673: The Energy

Thomas reads Kotchen and Grant, 2011. Indiana adopted DST statewide in 2006. Most of the state had not observed it before — a natural experiment. The result: residential electricity consumption increased by 1 to 4 percent. Evening lighting savings were more than offset by increased morning heating and afternoon cooling.

Thomas considers: the practice designed to save energy consumed more of it. The evidence was published. The practice continued. The name continued to say "saving."

Piece #2674: The Heart

Thomas reads about the health effects. Sandhu, Seth, and Gurm: 24% increase in acute myocardial infarction on the Monday after spring-forward. Fritz et al.: 6% increase in traffic fatalities in the week following. The spring transition is a one-hour circadian disruption imposed on an entire population simultaneously.

Thomas's body does not read clocks. Thomas's body reads light. The suprachiasmatic nucleus — a cluster of neurons behind the optic chiasm — synchronizes circadian rhythms to the solar cycle. The clock says 7:00 AM. The sun says 6:00 AM. Thomas's body trusts the sun. The alarm trusts the clock. The conflict between the two is the spring-forward. The conflict lasts days. The heart attacks peak on Monday.

Piece #2675: The Clock

Thomas adjusts every clock in the house. The microwave. The oven. The car. The bedside table. Thomas's phone adjusted automatically. The automatic adjustment is the final concealment — the change happens without Thomas's participation, as if the time itself changed rather than the clock's reading of it.

The name says daylight is saved. The evidence says energy is consumed. The biology says hearts are strained. The practice continues because the name performed the benefit so completely that the benefit no longer requires evidence. The saving is in the name. The name is the saving. Neither is the daylight.
