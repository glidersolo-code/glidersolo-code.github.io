The Base

A city has 85% Green cabs and 15% Blue cabs. A witness to a hit-and-run identifies the cab as Blue. The witness is tested and found to be 80% accurate in distinguishing Blue from Green under similar conditions.

What is the probability the cab was Blue?

Most people answer 80%. The witness is 80% accurate. The witness said Blue. Therefore, 80% probability of Blue.

The correct answer is 41%.

The calculation: of the 85 Green cabs, the witness will misidentify 17 as Blue (20% error rate). Of the 15 Blue cabs, the witness will correctly identify 12 as Blue (80% accuracy). Total cabs identified as Blue: 29. Of those, 12 are actually Blue. 12/29 = 41%.

The 80% accuracy is real. The 80% is not the probability. The probability requires the base rate — the prior probability that a randomly selected cab is Blue. The base rate is 15%. The base rate transforms the 80% accuracy into a 41% posterior probability. Without the base rate, the 80% performs as the answer. The 80% is not the answer.

Kahneman and Tversky, 1972, 1973. The base rate fallacy. The reader ignores prior probabilities when evaluating diagnostic evidence. The evidence — vivid, specific, case-relevant — captures all the attention. The base rate — general, statistical, category-level — is invisible. Not hidden. Invisible. The base rate sits in plain view. The reader's cognition walks past it.

The medical version is lethal. A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test is 99% accurate. A patient tests positive. The physician, the patient, and the insurance company all estimate approximately 99% probability of disease.

The actual probability is approximately 9%.

The arithmetic: in 1,000 people, 1 has the disease and tests positive (true positive). 999 do not have the disease, but the 1% false positive rate means approximately 10 will test positive (false positives). Total positive tests: 11. Of those, 1 has the disease. 1/11 = 9%.

A 99% accurate test. A 9% probability. The gap between the accuracy and the probability is the base rate. The base rate is the boring number. The test result is the vivid number. The vivid number wins.

This is the third architecture in the reader. Anchoring captures the magnitude — the first number determines the estimate. Confirmation bias captures the direction — the prior belief determines which evidence is sought. Base rate neglect captures the probability — the vivid evidence displaces the prior probability. Three cognitive mechanisms. Three ways the reader introduces invisible architecture that the number never contained.

The reader who is anchored at the wrong number, selects confirming evidence, and ignores the base rate will produce a judgment that is wrong in magnitude, direction, and probability — and will describe that judgment as careful, evidence-based, and independent.

The format — a test result, a witness identification, a diagnostic number — performs as sufficient. The reader sees the evidence. The evidence is vivid. The base rate is pale. The vivid displaces the pale. The reader's cognition is the architecture. The reader is the invisible channel.
