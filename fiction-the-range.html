The Range

Thomas, Part CXII

1001. The Reference Population

The reference interval for a laboratory test is defined by the central 95% of results from a reference population — a group of individuals selected to be healthy by criteria specific to each laboratory and each test. The convention was formalized by the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC), published by Solberg in the Journal of Clinical Chemistry and Clinical Biochemistry (21:749-760, 1983) and refined in successive recommendations: collect specimens from a minimum of 120 reference individuals, exclude outliers, calculate the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles, and report the resulting interval as the reference range. Results within this interval are "normal." Results outside it are "abnormal." The boundary is placed at the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles because 95% is a convention. Not because human physiology changes at those values. Not because a result at the 2.4th percentile is clinically different from a result at the 2.6th percentile. Because 95% is a number that the discipline chose, and the choosing created the boundary, and the boundary created the categories.

Thomas has regular laboratory panels. His results are compared to reference intervals derived from reference populations that were selected for health — populations that, by definition, do not include medically complex children on multiple medications with seizure disorders and feeding difficulties. The reference population is the ruler. Thomas is measured against a ruler that was calibrated by people who are not like him. A result outside the reference interval is flagged as abnormal. The flag does not indicate that Thomas is unhealthy. It indicates that Thomas's result differs from the central 95% of a population that does not resemble him.

1002. The Five Percent

By the definition of the reference interval — the central 95% — five percent of healthy individuals will have results outside the normal range. Two and a half percent will fall below the lower limit. Two and a half percent will fall above the upper limit. These individuals are healthy. Their results are normal for them. The definition classifies them as abnormal because the definition must exclude something, and it excludes the tails.

Thomas's laboratory results arrive in the electronic health record with a flag: H for high, L for low, or no flag for within range. The flag is automatic. It compares the result to the reference interval stored in the system's database. If Thomas's sodium is 134 mEq/L and the reference interval is 135-145, the flag appears: L. Low. Abnormal. The flag does not know that Thomas's sodium has been 134 for three years, that it is his physiological normal, that his kidneys and his medications and his fluid intake and his seizure disorder produce a sodium that sits just below the statistical boundary. The flag knows only the boundary. Thomas sits on the wrong side of it.

1003. The Panel

A standard comprehensive metabolic panel tests fourteen analytes: sodium, potassium, chloride, carbon dioxide, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, glucose, calcium, total protein, albumin, total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase. Each has a reference interval. Each reference interval excludes 5% of healthy individuals. The probability that a healthy individual has all fourteen results within their respective normal ranges is 0.95^14 ≈ 0.49. Roughly half. For a complete blood count with differential — adding hemoglobin, hematocrit, white blood cell count, platelet count, and the differential percentages — the total number of reported values can exceed twenty. The probability that a healthy individual has all twenty results within normal: 0.95^20 ≈ 0.36. A 64% chance that a perfectly healthy person will have at least one abnormal result.

Thomas receives a comprehensive metabolic panel and a complete blood count every three months. Each panel generates, on average, one or two flagged results. Each flag triggers a clinical response: a note in the chart, a comparison to previous values, sometimes a repeat test, sometimes a referral. The flags are generated by the definition. The clinical response is generated by the flags. The chart records the response as evidence of clinical activity — problems identified, problems addressed. The problems are, in part, artifacts of the number of tests and the structure of the reference interval. But the chart does not record this. The chart records: abnormal sodium, monitored. Abnormal ALT, follow-up. The cause — the mathematical necessity of finding something when testing for twenty things — is invisible. The effect — the investigation, the note, the concern — is attributed to Thomas.

1004. The Flag

Electronic health record systems display abnormal results in a different color — red, or bold, or with an asterisk. The visual flag draws the clinician's attention. This is its purpose: to ensure that results outside the reference interval are not overlooked. The flag is a signal. The signal says: this result requires attention. The signal does not say: this result indicates disease. But the visual salience of the flag — the red against the black, the bold against the normal — creates an attentional asymmetry. Normal results are visually neutral. Abnormal results are visually urgent. The eye is drawn to the flag. The flag shapes what the clinician notices. The clinician notices abnormal results. The abnormal results become the focus. The focus becomes the clinical narrative. The narrative is: we found something.

Thomas's chart is a landscape of flags. Some flags represent genuine clinical concerns — medication levels outside therapeutic range, kidney function declining over time. Other flags represent the five percent — results that are Thomas's normal, flagged because Thomas's normal falls outside the reference population's normal. The chart does not distinguish between these. Both are red. Both are flagged. Both draw the eye. Both generate notes. The landscape of flags creates a visual impression of clinical complexity that is partly genuine and partly artifact. The artifact is indistinguishable from the finding. Both are red.

1005. The Range

"Normal" is a range. The range is defined by a population. The population is selected by criteria. The criteria determine who is included. The included determine the percentiles. The percentiles determine the boundaries. The boundaries determine who is abnormal.

Every step in this chain is a choice. The choice of reference population, the choice of exclusion criteria, the choice of 95% rather than 90% or 99%, the choice to flag results automatically, the choice to display flags in red — each choice is reasonable, each is conventional, and the chain of choices creates a system that generates findings in healthy people by mathematical necessity and attributes those findings to the person rather than to the system.

One thousand and five pieces, one hundred and twelve Parts, and Thomas's chart is a document written in flags. Some flags point to Thomas. Some flags point to the system. All flags are the same color, and the color is the system's color, and the system reads its own color as evidence of the patient's condition. The range defines the normal. The normal defines the abnormal. The abnormal defines the chart. The chart defines Thomas. And Thomas, who has been abnormal since the first test and will be abnormal at the last, carries flags that are partly his body's signature and partly the system's arithmetic — indistinguishable on the screen, both red, both flagged, both entered into the record as findings, both generating the clinical activity that the chart will later cite as evidence that the system was paying attention.
