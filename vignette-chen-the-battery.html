The Battery

Chen glances at her phone. 87%. She puts it back on the desk and returns to the paper she is reading — Plett 2004, extended Kalman filtering for lithium-ion battery management. She has been reading it for forty minutes, and the number has not changed.

She picks up the phone again. Still 87%. She thinks: each percentage point lasts roughly twelve minutes at this usage level, so the number should have changed by now. Then she reads the next section of Plett's paper and understands why it hasn't.

The 87% is not a measurement. It is the posterior mean of a state-space model. The extended Kalman filter predicts state of charge from coulomb integration, observes terminal voltage, computes the residual between predicted and observed voltage, and corrects the prediction by a weighted gain. The gain depends on the noise covariance matrices, which are estimated, which are wrong, which are corrected by the next observation. The number in the status bar is the output of this recursive correction — a best guess, updated once per minute, referenced to a maximum capacity that has been silently degrading since she bought the phone fourteen months ago.

She opens Settings. Battery. Battery Health. Maximum Capacity: 89%.

So her 87% is 87% of 89%. In absolute terms, roughly 77% of what the phone held when it was new. The number in the status bar does not report this. The number in the status bar performs the simplicity that the electrochemistry does not permit.

She takes the phone outside. February. She walks to the mailbox and back — four minutes in 3°C air. When she comes inside, the display reads 84%. She sets the phone on the warm desk. Eight minutes later: 87% again. The battery did not discharge and recharge. The voltage curve shifted with temperature, and the Kalman filter revised its estimate in both directions.

Three percentage points lost to cold. Three percentage points recovered by warmth. The same state of charge, displayed three different ways, because the estimate is conditional on temperature and the display does not say "conditional."

Maren would say: The simplest number in the house is a Bayesian posterior. Every other number at least pretends to be measured. This one doesn't even pretend — it just never gets asked.

Chen puts the phone down. 87%. The most familiar number in her life, checked fifty times a day for three years, trusted completely. An estimate of an unobservable quantity, referenced to a degrading maximum, compressed from a nonlinear curve, conditional on temperature, rounded to an integer, and displayed in a status bar eleven pixels wide.

She knows all of this now. She will still check the number fifty times tomorrow. Knowing changes nothing about the glance. The format is too simple to resist.
