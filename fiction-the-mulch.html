Part CCLVII: The Mulch

Piece #1726: The Bag

The bag says "2 cubic feet." Thomas bought twelve bags for the front flower beds. He calculated: beds total 100 square feet, recommended depth 3 inches, 2-cubic-foot bags cover approximately 8 square feet each at 3 inches — twelve bags. The "2 cubic feet" was measured at the packaging facility, when the mulch was loose and freshly loaded into the bag. Between the facility and Thomas's driveway, the bags were stacked on pallets, loaded onto a truck, driven to the distribution center, restacked, driven to the retail store, and stacked again on a display. Each compression event pushed the particles closer together. The shredded hardwood — irregular fibers, bark strips, small splinters — nests when compressed. Thomas opened the first bag and spread it across a section of bed. It covered less than 8 square feet at 3 inches. He opened the second bag. Same. By the ninth bag, the beds were covered, but at closer to 2 inches than 3. The remaining three bags brought the depth to approximately 2.5 inches across the 100 square feet. The volume on the bag described the mulch before the supply chain compressed it.

Piece #1727: The Depth

Three inches is the standard recommendation. The number appears on extension service websites, garden center signage, and the back of the bag. But 3 inches of what. Three inches of fine-shredded hardwood mulch compacts differently from 3 inches of medium pine bark nuggets. The shredded hardwood interlocks — fibers grip fibers, creating a mat that resists wind displacement but settles under rain and its own decomposition. The pine bark nuggets are rounded, coarse, and do not interlock — they resist settling but float in heavy rain and migrate downhill on sloped beds. Thomas's beds slope slightly toward the sidewalk. By mid-June, the pine bark nuggets his neighbor used had migrated onto the concrete after two thunderstorms. Thomas's shredded hardwood stayed in place but had settled from 2.5 inches to approximately 1.5 inches. The recommended 3 inches is a number that does not specify the material. The material determines whether the 3 inches stays where you put it.

Piece #1728: The Season

Mulch decomposes. Shredded hardwood mulch loses 20 to 30 percent of its volume in the first growing season. The organisms that break down organic matter — fungi, bacteria, insects — work faster in warm, moist conditions. By August in Thomas's climate zone, the April mulch has decomposed measurably. The 2.5 inches he achieved in April is 1.5 inches by late summer. At 1.5 inches, weed suppression is compromised — the layer is thin enough for weed seeds to root through. The bag that said "2 cubic feet" described a volume at a moment. The bed consumed that volume over a season. Thomas buys a second round of mulch in July — four more bags to top-dress the beds back to 2.5 inches. By October, it settles again. The annual mulch budget is not twelve bags. It is sixteen. The bag described its contents once. The biology described them continuously.

Piece #1729: The Yard

Thomas's neighbor ordered bulk mulch — "3 cubic yards" from a landscape supplier. A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet. Three cubic yards is 81 cubic feet — enough for approximately 320 square feet at 3 inches. The landscape supplier loaded the mulch with a front-end loader into the neighbor's truck bed. The loader bucket is approximately 1 cubic yard when filled to the lip. The operator heaped the first bucket slightly above the lip, heaped the second, and leveled the third. Three scoops. The actual volume depends on the bucket's geometry, how high the operator heaps, and how much falls off during the lift. Wet mulch — after a week of rain at the supplier's yard — weighs more and packs denser; a bucket of wet mulch contains more material by weight but may contain less volume than a bucket of dry, fluffy mulch that heaps higher. The neighbor received somewhere between 2.5 and 3.5 cubic yards. He spread it across 280 square feet. It covered at approximately 3 inches with some left over. He could not tell whether the overage meant generous scoops or a smaller bed than he estimated.

Piece #1730: The Color

Two hundred and fifty-seven Parts. Thomas noticed the mulch color changed. The bag said "dark brown." In April, the fresh mulch was uniformly dark brown — dyed with iron oxide or carbon-based colorant at the processing facility. By June, the surface layer had faded to gray under UV exposure. The interior, protected from sunlight, remained brown. The bed looked two-toned: gray on top, brown where Thomas disturbed the surface while weeding. By August, the entire visible surface was gray. The bag described the color at the time of packaging. The sun described the color after three months. The consumer who chose "dark brown" over "natural" for aesthetic reasons had dark brown mulch for approximately eight weeks. The rest of the season, it was gray. The color on the bag label was a starting condition, not a permanent one. But the bag, having printed "dark brown" once, did not update.
