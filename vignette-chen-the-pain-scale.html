The Pain Scale

A Chen-Maren Vignette (II)

The committee assessed difficulty.

Not the paragraph's difficulty as experienced by a reader — that would require reading the paragraph, sitting with it, registering the resistance or the yield, the moments of friction or clarity. The committee assessed difficulty numerically: the Flesch-Kincaid readability score. Grade level 16.2. The number entered the assessment file. The assessment file processed the number alongside other metrics — enrollment impact, citation count, committee rating. The number looked like the other numbers. It was not like the other numbers.

"Grade level 16.2," Hendricks said. "That's graduate-level difficulty. Above our threshold for undergraduate inclusion."

"The Flesch-Kincaid formula counts syllables per word and words per sentence," Maren said. "It does not read the paragraph."

"It measures readability."

"It measures syllable count and sentence length. It calls the result 'readability.' The word does the rest."

The formula was transparent — anyone could calculate it. Average words per sentence multiplied by 0.39, plus average syllables per word multiplied by 11.8, minus 15.59. The output was a grade level. Chen's paragraph, with its long sentences and polysyllabic vocabulary, scored 16.2 — the numerical equivalent of "too difficult." The formula could not distinguish between difficulty that was unnecessary and difficulty that was necessary. It could not distinguish between a paragraph that was obscure and a paragraph that was precise. It counted syllables. The count became a grade level. The grade level became a judgment.

"The paragraph is at 0.8 density," Chen said. "The density is a different kind of difficulty — not obscurity but compression. The Flesch-Kincaid cannot see the difference. It sees syllables."

The assessment file now contained two numbers about the same paragraph: density 0.8 (from close reading) and readability 16.2 (from syllable counting). The two numbers occupied adjacent columns. The formatting made them appear commensurable — comparable, combinable, equally valid as data points. The density reflected what the paragraph did. The readability score reflected what the paragraph's surface looked like to a formula that could not read.

The committee used the number. Not because they believed syllable counting measured understanding. Because the infrastructure required a readability metric, and the metric required a number, and the number required a scale, and the scale required a formula, and the formula required inputs it could count. The committee was competent. The committee knew the difference between counting syllables and assessing comprehension. The committee used the number anyway, because the assessment form had a field for it, and the field required a value, and the absence of a value would trigger a compliance flag, and the compliance flag would require more documentation than the number.

Chen's paragraph continued at 0.8 density and 16.2 readability. The density was invisible to the formula. The formula was visible to the infrastructure. The infrastructure processed what it could see.
