The Squiggle

The Statute of Frauds was enacted in 1677. It required written signatures for certain contracts, leases, and wills. But the statute defined "signature" so broadly that any mark sufficed — an X, a stamp, a squiggle — provided the signer intended to authenticate. The operative legal fact was never the handwriting. It was the intention. The verification was optional from the beginning.

For a while, the handwriting appeared to matter. Albert Osborn published Questioned Documents in 1910 — a thousand pages of methodology for comparing letter formations, slant angles, pen pressure, spacing rhythms. In 1935, eight document examiners testified in the Hauptmann trial that the ransom notes in the Lindbergh kidnapping matched Hauptmann's handwriting. Backward N's, unclosed o's, specific t-formations. Hauptmann was convicted and executed. Forensic handwriting analysis reached peak credibility and has been declining since.

Then the systems that required signatures stopped checking them.

Banks required signature cards. Tellers were supposed to compare. Banking professionals acknowledge the verification was sloppy to nonexistent. In 2004, Check 21 enabled automated digital check processing, and systematic signature comparison ended. Credit cards required signatures for sixty-eight years — from Frank McNamara's Diners Club in 1950 to April 2018, when all four major networks simultaneously dropped the requirement. Their stated reason: EMV chip technology made signatures obsolete. What they were actually saying: signatures had never been reliably verified.

The form persists anyway. DocuSign, founded in 2003, processes billions of electronic signatures. Its interface renders the signer's name in cursive-like script — a visual reproduction of handwriting that no law requires. The ESIGN Act (2000) defines an electronic signature as "an electronic sound, symbol, or process" adopted with the intent to sign. A click satisfies the definition. The cursive rendering is a comfort feature. It makes clicking feel like signing. The squiggle on the screen carries no verification function. It carries the memory of a time when the squiggle mattered.

And the evidence that the ritual itself has psychological power was retracted. In 2012, researchers published in PNAS that signing at the top of a form reduced dishonest self-reports. Corporations and agencies restructured their paperwork. In 2020, a replication attempt failed. In 2021, the paper was retracted — the field-study data had been fabricated. The most prominent evidence for the psychological efficacy of signing was itself a forgery. The study that said the squiggle changes behavior was produced by fabricating the data that proved it.

What remains is the ritual. Sign here. Initial here. Sign and date. The sticker, the arrow, the line. The act of moving a pen or a finger across a surface in a shape that resembles cursive writing, witnessed by no analyst, compared against no reference, verified by no system. The act feels meaningful. The meaning comes not from what the signature does now — which is nothing — but from what signatures used to do, or what people believe they used to do. The ritual borrows authority from a verification era that peaked in 1935 and has been absent for decades.

The squiggle is the form of an act that no longer occurs, preserved in the body's muscle memory and the culture's institutional memory, performing consent by performing the appearance of verification.
