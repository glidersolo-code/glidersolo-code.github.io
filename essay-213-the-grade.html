The Grade

A USDA beef grader slices a carcass between the 12th and 13th ribs and examines one muscle. The ribeye. The grader looks at the marbling — the white flecks of fat distributed through the red lean. More marbling, higher grade. Prime, Choice, Select. The grade is stamped on the carcass. Every cut from that animal — the tenderloin, the brisket, the flank, the chuck, the round — receives the same grade. The ribeye at one rib determined it all.

The system was developed in 1917 and first standardized in 1927. It was designed for carcass trading — a way to establish common language between buyers and sellers who needed to agree on what they were paying for without examining every cut. The solution was elegant: examine one cross-section and grade the whole animal. The solution is also a distortion: the ribeye at the 12th rib does not represent the round. A Prime carcass can have a mediocre shank. A Select carcass can have a beautiful tenderloin. The muscles differ in fiber type, connective tissue, fat distribution, and tenderness. The grade does not.

Marbling correlates with eating quality — tenderness, juiciness, flavor — in the muscle where it is measured. The correlation is real and documented. It is also incomplete. Tenderness depends on muscle fiber type, postmortem aging, animal stress at slaughter, breed, and cooking method. A dry-aged Select strip steak, properly cooked, can outperform a fresh Prime chuck roast. The grade measures what is visible. What is visible is not everything that matters.

The system creates an economic incentive that works against nutrition. Higher marbling means a higher grade. A higher grade means a higher price per pound across the entire carcass. Producers maximize grade by feeding cattle longer on grain-intensive diets in confined operations. The system rewards intramuscular fat. From a dietary perspective, intramuscular fat is saturated fat. The same federal agency — the United States Department of Agriculture — issues dietary guidelines recommending that Americans limit saturated fat and beef grading standards that incentivize maximizing it. The contradiction is not a failure of coordination. It is two different measurement systems, applied to the same object, optimizing for different outcomes, administered by the same institution.

Japan grades beef marbling on a 1-12 scale. Australia uses 0-9+. The USDA has eight quality grades, three of which consumers encounter. A steak graded BMS 5 in Japan and one graded BMS 10 can both receive the USDA Prime designation. The American system's coarseness conceals variation that finer-grained systems resolve. Whether the coarseness is a deficiency or a feature depends on who benefits from the blur.

The consumer buys Prime. The consumer expects the ribeye. The consumer receives the round. The grade promised one thing and delivered another — not through deception, but through generalization. The single measurement at a single location on a single muscle was applied to every location and every muscle. The generalization is the system. The system is the grade.
