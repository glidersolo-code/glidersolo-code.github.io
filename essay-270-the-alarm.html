The Alarm

The box says "smoke detector." The consumer buys it, installs it on the ceiling, and trusts that when smoke appears, the alarm will sound. The label is a binary promise. Smoke present: alarm. No smoke: silence. The consumer's model is simple. The device is not.

Two technologies share the label. Ionization detectors use a tiny amount of americium-241 to ionize air molecules between two electrodes, maintaining a steady current. When small, fast-moving particles — the kind produced by flaming fires — enter the chamber, they disrupt the current, and the alarm sounds. Photoelectric detectors aim a light beam past a sensor. When larger, slower-moving particles — the kind produced by smoldering fires — enter the chamber, they scatter the light onto the sensor, and the alarm sounds. Both detect smoke. They detect different kinds of smoke at different speeds.

The difference is not small. In NIST testing, ionization detectors responded 15 to 50 minutes faster than photoelectric detectors for fast-flaming fires. Photoelectric detectors responded 15 to 50 minutes faster for smoldering fires. Fifty minutes is the distance between escape and entrapment. The two technologies are not variations of the same device — they are different instruments for different fires, sold under the same name.

The fire that kills is usually the smoldering fire. NFPA data shows that 60 to 70 percent of home fire deaths involve fires where the victim was asleep and the fire smoldered before flashover. Upholstery ignites from a cigarette, an electrical fault heats a wire inside a wall, a candle tips onto a couch. The fire produces thick, visible smoke with large particles. The photoelectric detector catches it quickly. The ionization detector catches it slowly, or not before the smoke density has already filled the escape routes. The most commonly sold consumer smoke detectors are ionization type, because they are cheaper — five to ten dollars versus fifteen to twenty-five. The consumer who buys the cheapest smoke detector overwhelmingly gets the type slower to respond to the fire type most likely to kill that consumer.

The label "smoke detector" contains the concealment. The consumer's question — "Does it detect smoke?" — is binary. The device's performance is not. The answer to the binary question is always yes. The answer to the unasked question — "Which kind of smoke, how fast, under what conditions?" — determines whether the device saves the life it was installed to save. The label cannot carry the unasked question because the label is a name, not a specification. And the name performs universality — all smoke, all fires, all protection — while the device inside performs specificity. The name is the product. The device is the compromise.
