Part CCCLXXVI: The Bars

Piece #2321: The Sand

Norman Joseph Woodland sat on a beach in Miami in 1949 and dragged his fingers through the sand. He was thinking about Morse code — dots and dashes, thin and thick, the language of telegraphy applied to something nobody had tried before: making a machine read a product. He dragged his fingers downward through the patterns, and the dots became thin vertical lines. The dashes became thick vertical lines. Thomas read about this and sat with it for a long time. A man on a beach, thinking about Morse code, accidentally invented the visual language that would identify every manufactured product on Earth. The patent was filed in 1949. Woodland and his partner Bernard Silver built a prototype reader using a 500-watt incandescent bulb — lasers didn't exist yet. They sold the patent in 1952 for $15,000. Silver died of leukemia in 1963 at thirty-eight, eleven years before the first barcode was scanned. He never saw what the sand drawing became.

Piece #2322: The Gum

June 26, 1974, 8:01 a.m. Marsh supermarket, Troy, Ohio. Clyde Dawson reached into a shopping cart and selected a ten-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit chewing gum. He passed it over the scanner. Sixty-seven cents. The gum and the receipt are now in the Smithsonian. Thomas stood in a grocery store aisle and tried to feel the weight of the moment — the first commercial scan. An ordinary act that became historical only because it was first. The gum was chosen deliberately, Dawson later said, in appreciation of Wrigley's early adoption of the code. But what struck Thomas was the price: sixty-seven cents, recorded nowhere on the gum itself. The barcode contained no price. It contained twelve digits pointing to a database. The database contained the price. The gum was identified, not described.

Piece #2323: The Sticker

Before barcodes, every item in a supermarket had a price sticker. Hand-applied, one per unit, readable by any customer who picked up the item. After barcodes, retailers began removing them. The labor savings were enormous — price stamping was one of the most labor-intensive tasks in the industry. Shelf-edge labels replaced item-level pricing. Thomas remembered his grandmother checking prices by picking things up. His children had never done this. They checked their phones, or they didn't check at all. The barcode had replaced visible pricing with an opaque reference, and within a generation the reference felt normal. Several states passed item-pricing laws in the 1970s — Massachusetts, Michigan. But the stickers retreated anyway. The consumer lost the ability to verify the price at the product and gained a system that was faster, more efficient, and entirely controlled by the store's database. The improvement was real. The information loss was invisible.

Piece #2324: The Prefix

Thomas learned that companies paid between $250 and $10,500 for a GS1 company prefix, depending on how many products they needed to identify. A short prefix — six digits — left room for 100,000 product numbers. A long prefix — ten digits — left room for 10. Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola: short prefixes, vast product catalogs. A local soap maker: a long prefix, ten products. Every barcode looked the same. Same width, same pattern of black and white lines, same twelve digits beneath. Thomas held two products side by side — one from a multinational, one from a farmer's market vendor — and the barcodes were visually identical. The format performed equality. The fee structure executed hierarchy. The bars concealed which company had paid $250 for ten slots and which had paid $10,500 for a hundred thousand.

Piece #2325: The Key

The barcode contains no price. No description. No ingredients. No country of origin. No manufacturing date. No expiration date. Thomas stared at the twelve digits beneath the bars on a cereal box and tried to find information in them. There was none. The barcode was an address — a pointer to a database controlled by whoever operated the scanner. The same barcode on the same product would retrieve a different price at a different store, or at the same store on a different day. The barcode was not the product's identity. It was a key to a room the consumer couldn't enter. Ten billion keys are turned every day. The rooms contain whatever the stores decide to put in them. The bars say nothing. The bars look like they say everything.
