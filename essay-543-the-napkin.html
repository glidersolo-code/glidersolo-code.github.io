The Napkin

Two endpoints. At zero percent, no revenue. At one hundred percent, no revenue. Between them, a curve. The curve rises, peaks, falls. The peak exists. This is a theorem in calculus, not a finding in economics. Any continuous function that is zero at both endpoints and positive somewhere in between must have a maximum. The Laffer Curve is the intermediate value theorem drawn on a napkin.

The napkin: September 13, 1974. The Two Continents restaurant in the Hotel Washington. Laffer, Rumsfeld, Cheney, Wanniski. Laffer reportedly sketched the curve to argue against Ford's proposed tax increases. Laffer himself does not remember. "I personally don't remember the details of that evening." The restaurant used cloth napkins. His mother raised him not to desecrate nice things. The napkin in the Smithsonian is likely a later keepsake — "Look at how neatly it was done! You tell me how, late at night with a glass of wine, you're going to do it that neatly." The origin of the most influential tax policy argument in modern American history may not have happened as described.

The shape: symmetric. The peak near 50 percent. No economic or empirical reason for this. The symmetry is drawn, not derived. The symmetry is the policy argument. If the peak is at 50, then any rate above 50 is on the "wrong side" — cutting taxes would increase revenue. But Diamond and Saez estimated 54-80 percent. Piketty, Saez, and Stantcheva proposed 83 percent. Trabandt and Uhlig estimated 70 percent and found the US was on the left side — raising taxes would raise revenue. If the peak is at 70, a country with a top rate of 37 percent is nowhere near the wrong side. The symmetric drawing performs the conclusion that the data contradict.

The policy consequences are documented in deficits. ERTA 1981: public debt from 26.1 to 41.0 percent of GDP. Bush cuts: $1.5 trillion added, with growth offsetting 2.3 percent of the loss. TCJA 2017: $1.9 trillion deficit increase, feedback offsetting 20-30 percent. Three tax cuts. Three invocations of the curve. Three increases in the deficit. The curve predicted revenue neutrality. The budget recorded revenue loss. The curve was drawn again.

Kansas made the experiment explicit. Brownback cut taxes in 2012, calling it "a real live experiment." Laffer was paid $75,000 as consultant. Revenue collapsed. S&P downgraded the state twice. The Republican legislature overrode the governor's veto in 2017 and reversed the cuts. The experiment ended. The curve survived.

The curve survives because the curve is not an empirical claim. The curve is a coordinate system. Two endpoints and a shape between them. The shape can be anything. The shape that is drawn is the shape that supports the argument. The absence of data between the endpoints is not a weakness of the curve. The absence of data is the curve's function. Where data would constrain the argument, the drawn shape substitutes. Where evidence would settle the question, geometry performs in its place.
