Part CXXIV: The Allocation

Piece #1061: The Unit

Thomas's fluids were measured.

The measurements were in milliliters — volume per unit time, administered through IV lines whose rates were set by pumps whose calculations assumed that what was ordered was what was delivered. The unit was precise. The delivery was approximate. The tubing had compliance. The connections had dead space. The flow varied with back-pressure, with kinks, with the patient's position. The ordered rate and the actual rate diverged by small amounts that the charting system did not record, because the charting system recorded orders, not deliveries.

In the American West, water was measured in acre-feet. One acre-foot: the volume of water that covers one acre of land to a depth of one foot — approximately 326,000 gallons, 1,233 cubic meters. The unit was defined by agriculture: a volume useful for irrigation planning, tied to the land it was meant to water. The acre-foot became the unit of water rights — the legal entitlement to a quantity of water from a river, a reservoir, a groundwater basin.

The unit was precise. The supply was not. Rivers are not constants. The Colorado River's annual flow has varied from 5.6 million acre-feet to 22.2 million acre-feet in the recorded period. The acre-foot measured a fixed entitlement to a variable supply. The precision of the unit concealed the variability of the resource. An allocation of 7.5 million acre-feet to the Upper Basin states sounded like a measurement. It was a claim.

Thomas's IV orders were claims too — a claim on 80 mL/hr from a system that delivered approximately 80 mL/hr, plus or minus the tubing's compliance, the pump's tolerance, the back-pressure of the patient's vein. The order was precise. The delivery was approximate. The chart recorded the order.

Piece #1062: The Compact

The Colorado River Compact was signed in 1922.

Seven states — Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, New Mexico (Upper Basin) and Nevada, Arizona, California (Lower Basin) — divided a river. The division was based on flow data from 1899 to 1921. The Bureau of Reclamation's estimates, derived from gauging stations with limited records, put the average annual flow at approximately 16.4 million acre-feet. The compact allocated 7.5 MAF to the Upper Basin and 7.5 MAF to the Lower Basin, with an additional 1.5 MAF later allocated to Mexico. Total allocation: 16.5 MAF.

The actual long-term average, reconstructed by tree-ring analysis centuries later, was approximately 15 MAF. The compact had allocated more water than the river contained. The overallocation was not a mathematical error — the estimates were the best available in 1922. The overallocation was a temporal error: the gauging period (1899-1921) was one of the wettest 23-year stretches in the last 500 years. The measurement was accurate for its period. Its period was not representative of the supply.

Thomas's reference ranges were the same. His lab values were compared to norms derived from specific populations during specific periods — populations that may not have represented his physiology, periods that may not have represented his trajectory. The reference range was accurate for its derivation sample. Its derivation sample was not representative of the patient.

The compact divided a fiction — an abundant river that existed during the measurement period and did not exist over the longer timescale. The states built their infrastructure — dams, canals, cities, farms — on the fiction. The infrastructure could not be unburdened of the fiction because the infrastructure was the fiction made concrete.

Piece #1063: The Priority

First in time, first in right.

The doctrine of prior appropriation — the legal framework governing western water — granted water rights based on the date of first use. The first irrigator to divert water from a stream had the senior right. In times of shortage, junior rights were curtailed before senior rights. The system created a hierarchy of claims organized not by need, not by efficiency, not by the public interest, but by the calendar.

A farmer who diverted water in 1870 had a right that superseded a city that incorporated in 1920. The city had a million residents. The farmer irrigated forty acres of alfalfa. In a drought, the city's water was curtailed before the alfalfa field ran dry. The priority system measured time, not value. Time became the measure of entitlement.

Thomas's chart had priorities too. The first diagnosis entered was the primary diagnosis — the one that organized his care, structured his billing, determined his room assignment. The first was not necessarily the most important. It was the first documented. The chart's priority system was temporal, like the doctrine of prior appropriation: the first entry organized the hierarchy, and the hierarchy organized the infrastructure, and the infrastructure determined the allocation.

First in time, first in right. First in the chart, first in the allocation of attention, resources, specialist referrals. The temporal priority was not a measurement of clinical importance. It was a measurement of when the documentation occurred. The measurement became the allocation.

Piece #1064: The Paper

Water rights existed on paper.

A senior right to 10,000 acre-feet of Colorado River water was a paper document — a certificate, a decree, a contract. The paper said 10,000 acre-feet. The river did not read the paper. In a year when the river carried 12 million acre-feet, all paper rights could be fulfilled. In a year when the river carried 8 million acre-feet, they could not. The paper rights totaled more than the river contained in most years. The sum of the claims exceeded the supply.

The excess was called "paper water" — water that existed in the legal system but not in the river. Paper water could be bought, sold, leased, and traded. Its price fluctuated. Its price was real. The water it represented was not always real. The market in paper water operated independently of the river's hydrology — a financial system trading in entitlements to a resource whose availability was determined by snowpack, temperature, and precipitation, none of which consulted the market.

Thomas's chart was paper too — an electronic document recording claims on his body. A medication order claimed a vein. A procedure order claimed an hour. A specialist consultation claimed attention. The sum of the claims on Thomas could exceed the capacity of Thomas — more procedures than hours, more specialists than the patient could tolerate, more interventions than the body could process simultaneously. The chart's orders were paper water — entitlements to the patient's resources that existed in the documentation system and did not always correspond to the patient's capacity.

The paper and the water diverged. The chart and the patient diverged. The divergence was managed — rationed, prioritized, triaged — but the management was performed within the system of paper rights, not by consulting the river.

Piece #1065: The Allocation

What was allocated?

Not water — rights. Not the river — claims on the river. Not the flow — entitlements to portions of the flow. The allocation system distributed legal claims on a physical resource, and the legal claims were treated as if they described the resource, and the resource varied independently of the claims, and the variance was managed by curtailment (cutting junior rights in dry years) rather than by redesigning the allocation to match the supply.

John Wesley Powell had warned Congress in 1878 — forty-four years before the compact. The Report on the Lands of the Arid Region argued that the West could not be settled on the model of the East. The rainfall was insufficient. The rivers were variable. The land should be organized by watershed, not by the rectangular survey grid. Powell's warning was heard, debated, and filed. The rectangular grid was maintained. The prior appropriation doctrine was adopted. The acre-foot was the unit. The compact was signed.

Powell's warning sat alongside Kuznets's warning about GDP (1934), Binet's warning about IQ (1905), and Spitzer's acknowledgment about DSM validity. Founding documents containing cautions that the systems they founded were designed to disregard. The warning was in the archive. The system was in the infrastructure. The archive and the infrastructure operated independently.

Thomas was an allocation — a set of claims on his body distributed through a charting system organized by temporal priority, denominated in precise units, totaling more than the patient contained, and managed by curtailment when the claims exceeded the capacity, not by redesigning the allocation to match the supply. The river was Thomas. The acre-feet were orders. The compact was the chart. The allocation was 1065 pieces long and the river was still flowing and the paper was still being traded and the divergence was still being managed within the system that produced it.
