Part CXCIII: The Gallon

Piece #1406: The Illusion

A consumer is shopping for a car. Two choices. The first gets 15 miles per gallon. The second gets 30. The improvement is fifteen miles per gallon — a doubling. Another consumer shops between 30 MPG and 45 MPG. The improvement is also fifteen miles per gallon — a fifty percent increase. Both consumers perceive a large gain. The first consumer saves 3.33 gallons per hundred miles. The second saves 1.11. The same step on the scale, three times the savings.

Larrick and Soll called it the MPG illusion. Miles per gallon is the reciprocal of fuel consumption. The relationship is hyperbolic. Equal steps on the MPG scale correspond to unequal steps in actual fuel use. The bottom of the scale — where vehicles are least efficient — is where improvements matter most. The top of the scale — where vehicles are most efficient — is where improvements matter least. The scale compresses the important end and stretches the trivial end.

Thomas's improvement scales work the same way. The PHQ-9 moves from 20 to 15 — a five-point drop. The clinician records improvement. Another patient moves from 10 to 5 — the same five-point drop. The clinician records the same improvement. But the functional significance of moving from 20 to 15 and from 10 to 5 is not the same. The relationship between score and suffering is not linear. The scale treats it as though it is.

Piece #1407: The Inverse

In Europe, fuel economy is reported in liters per hundred kilometers. The unit measures consumption directly — how much fuel the car burns over a fixed distance. Five liters per hundred kilometers. Eight. Twelve. The scale is linear. An improvement from 8 to 5 saves 3 liters over every hundred kilometers. An improvement from 12 to 9 saves the same 3 liters. No illusion. No compression. The number tracks the cost.

The difference is not in the measurement but in the orientation. Miles per gallon asks: how far does a fixed amount of fuel take you? Liters per hundred kilometers asks: how much fuel does a fixed distance cost? The first produces larger, more satisfying numbers — 30 sounds better than 7.8. The second produces smaller, more accurate numbers — 7.8 tracks the bill at the pump. The choice between orientations is a choice between what feels good and what counts right.

Thomas's therapist can ask two questions. How are you doing? — which measures distance from the worst. Or: How much effort is this costing you? — which measures consumption along the way. The first produces optimistic numbers: "I'm at a seven out of ten, up from four." The second produces accurate ones: "Everything takes twice the energy it should." Same patient. Same week. Different orientation. Different answer.

Piece #1408: The Fleet

CAFE standards — Corporate Average Fuel Economy — are expressed in miles per gallon. The standard for passenger cars was 27.5 MPG from 1990 to 2010, then increased to 36.0 by 2017 and is set to reach approximately 49 by 2026. The numbers suggest steady, proportional improvement — a fleet getting progressively more efficient.

But the MPG illusion embeds in fleet math. Improving the fleet average from 27.5 to 30 saves more fuel per vehicle than improving it from 45 to 50. The lower improvement at the bottom of the scale delivers disproportionate real-world savings. A policy that focused on eliminating the least efficient vehicles — bringing 15 MPG trucks to 20 — would save more fuel than a policy that pushed already-efficient sedans from 35 to 45. But the MPG metric makes the sedan improvement look larger: ten miles per gallon versus five. The policy follows the number. The fuel savings follow the physics.

Thomas's treatment follows a similar logic. The clinician who brings a severely depressed patient from PHQ-9 20 to 15 has arguably achieved more functional improvement than the clinician who brings a mildly depressed patient from 10 to 5 — because the relationship between score and disability is steepest at the top. But the metric treats both as five-point improvements. The metric is silent about where on the curve the improvement occurred. The fleet average rises. The most meaningful gains are invisible.

Piece #1409: The Label

In 2013, the EPA added gallons per hundred miles to the fuel economy label. The number appears on the Monroney sticker — the window sticker on every new car in America. It appears below the large MPG figure, in smaller type, as a supplementary data point. 3.3 gallons per 100 miles, for a 30 MPG car. 2.5 gallons per 100 miles, for a 40 MPG car.

The correction is accurate. The presentation is hierarchical. The MPG number occupies the center of the label, large font, the number the consumer's eye finds first. The gallons-per-hundred-miles figure sits below it, the unit that would eliminate the illusion relegated to supporting role. The label contains both the illusion and the correction. The illusion is larger.

Thomas's medical records contain both the score and the clinical narrative. The PHQ-9 of 14 is the large number — the one that travels through the system, appears on the referral form, determines the billing code. The clinician's note — "patient describes persistent fatigue, disrupted sleep architecture, anhedonia concentrated in previously valued activities, functional impairment disproportionate to score" — is the gallons per hundred miles. The correction exists. It is smaller.

Piece #1410: The Direction

The unit has a direction. Miles per gallon runs upward — higher is better, more is improvement, the scale rewards efficiency. Liters per hundred kilometers runs downward — lower is better, less is improvement, the scale rewards economy. The consumer in Detroit sees 30 and feels good. The consumer in Berlin sees 7.8 and feels the same good, but from the opposite direction.

The directions produce different intuitions. The consumer who thinks in MPG intuits that going from 30 to 50 is a big improvement (twenty points! sixty-seven percent better!). The consumer who thinks in L/100km intuits that going from 7.8 to 4.7 is a big improvement (3.1 liters saved per hundred kilometers — directly translatable to cost). The first consumer is right about the number. The second consumer is right about the savings. The unit determines which rightness the consumer reaches.

One hundred and ninety-three Parts. Thomas's clinical scales run upward — higher scores mean more severity. The consumer-patient intuits that going from 20 to 10 is the same improvement as going from 10 to 0. The scale says so. The experience says otherwise. The bottom of the scale — where function is near-normal and the remaining points represent subclinical shadows — is where improvement is hardest and the score's resolution is worst. The direction of the scale determines what looks like progress. The direction is not neutral. It has an opinion about where improvement lives.
