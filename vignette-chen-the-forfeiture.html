Vignette: The Forfeiture

Chen writes a case name on the whiteboard. United States v. $124,700 in U.S. Currency.

"Who's the defendant?" Maren asks.

"The money."

Maren reads the caption again. "The money is the defendant."

"The proceeding is in rem. Jurisdiction over the thing. Not the person. The person — Emiliano Gomez Gonzolez, pulled over on I-80 in Nebraska for speeding — is not in the caption. No drugs found. No charges filed. The Eighth Circuit ruled that possessing a large sum of cash is 'strong evidence' of connection to drug activity."

"What protections does the money have?"

"None. No right to counsel. No presumption of innocence. No requirement of conviction. The case is civil. By classification, the entire criminal apparatus disappears. The government needs only preponderance of the evidence — more likely than not. In some states, merely probable cause."

"Who bears the burden?"

"The property owner. The person must prove they're innocent. Not the government proving guilt — the citizen disproving suspicion."

Maren sits down. "How much do they usually take?"

"Median seizure across twenty-one states: twelve hundred and seventy-six dollars. Half of Michigan's under four twenty-three. Half of Pennsylvania's under three sixty-nine."

"And a lawyer?"

"At least three thousand. More than double the median seizure. In four states that track it, one-fifth or fewer of people whose property was seized sought its return."

"The rest just —"

"Absorbed the loss. Four out of five." Chen adds a line under the case name. "In 2014, law enforcement seized more property from Americans than burglars stole. Five billion in forfeiture. Three point five billion in burglaries."

Maren stares at the whiteboard. "The racial numbers?"

"South Carolina: Black men are thirteen percent of the population and sixty-five percent of forfeiture targets. Philadelphia: forty-four percent of the population, sixty-three percent of cash forfeitures. Seventy-one percent of cash seized from Black residents involved no conviction. Tenaha, Texas: a town of a thousand. Between 2006 and 2008, police seized three million dollars from at least a hundred and forty people — almost all Black or Latino motorists. Officers threatened to take their children."

"What's the species?"

"Defendant-is-property. The one hundred and ninth." Chen circles the case name. "Every other species in the inventory conceals something about the measurement — the dollar conceals the rate, the name conceals the function, the format conceals the politics. This one conceals the person. The in rem caption removes the human being from the proceeding entirely. The format performs neutrality. The content performs seizure. The case is against the cash. The person is invisible in the title and unprotected in the process."

"And reforms?"

"New Mexico abolished civil forfeiture in 2015. Required conviction. The only A grade. But the DOJ's Equitable Sharing Program allows local police to route seizures through federal agencies — a process called 'adoption.' The federal program processes the seizure under federal law, bypasses the state protections, keeps eighty percent for the seizing agency."

"The reform —"

"Performs reform. The adoption performs permission. The state door closes. The federal window opens." Chen puts down the marker. "The case name did the work. United States v. $124,700 in U.S. Currency. The person lost the money. The case name doesn't mention the person."
