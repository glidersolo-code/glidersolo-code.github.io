The Horse

James Watt needed to sell steam engines in the 1780s. His customers were mine operators who used horses to lift water and ore. Watt needed a comparison. He measured the work output of horses at a London brewery — probably Barclay's — turning a mill wheel. One horsepower: 33,000 foot-pounds per minute. 550 foot-pounds per second. 745.7 watts. The number was reportedly overestimated — Watt inflated the sustained work output of a typical horse so his engines would compare favorably, delivering more than the rated number promised. The unit was a sales tool. The comparison animal was stronger than a real horse. The customer received more than advertised. The marketing was designed to undersell the product.

Horsepower is not one unit. Mechanical horsepower: 745.7 watts (Watt's definition). Metric horsepower (PS, Pferdestärke): 735.5 watts (75 kg raised one meter per second). Electrical horsepower: 746 watts. Boiler horsepower: 9,810 watts — approximately 13 times the mechanical. Each is called "horsepower." Each describes a different quantity. The International System uses the watt — named after the man who invented the unit it replaces. The kilowatt is precise, unambiguous, internationally standardized. It has not replaced horsepower in consumer markets. Automobile manufacturers report engine output in horsepower because the word is understood, because the animal comparison is intuitive, and because 300 hp sounds more impressive than 224 kW, though they describe the same engine. The larger number sells the car.

Before 1972, American automakers reported "gross" horsepower: measured at the crankshaft with accessories removed, no air filter, open exhaust headers. After SAE adopted net ratings, horsepower was measured with all standard accessories attached. The same engine in the same car — a 1971 Chevrolet Corvette rated at 365 gross hp, a 1972 at 255 net hp. The number dropped 110 points. The engine did not change. The test changed. European manufacturers use DIN standards; Japanese use JIS. The same engine can produce different numbers under different national standards. The label says "horsepower." It does not specify which standard produced the number.

Engine horsepower is measured at the crankshaft — brake horsepower. The power that reaches the wheels is lower, reduced by drivetrain losses: transmission, differential, axle friction. A car rated at 300 bhp might deliver 250-270 at the wheels — a 10-17% loss depending on drivetrain type. The consumer drives the car on wheel horsepower. The spec sheet reports crankshaft horsepower. The two numbers describe different locations in the same powertrain. The specification always reports the higher number.

A Tesla Model S Plaid produces 1,020 horsepower from electric motors. There is no horse. There is no combustion. There is no crankshaft in the traditional sense. The unit invented to compare steam engines to brewery horses in the 1780s describes electric vehicles that share no mechanical principle with either. The kilowatt exists. It is exact. It is internationally standardized. It is not used on spec sheets because 300 hp sells more cars than 224 kW. A unit derived from a possibly overestimated brewery horse, defined differently in different countries, measured under different standards that produce different numbers for the same engine, reported at the crankshaft rather than at the wheels, used to describe machines that contain no horse, no steam, and sometimes no combustion — printed on a window sticker, experienced by the consumer as a property of the car.
