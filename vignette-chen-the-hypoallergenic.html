Vignette Chen The Hypoallergenic

Vignette

"Hypoallergenic," Maren read. "That means it causes fewer allergic reactions?"

"Fewer than what?" Chen asked.

Maren paused. "Fewer than... regular products?"

"Which regular products? How many fewer? What's the threshold? What's the comparator?"

"I don't know. The word just says 'less.'"

"The prefix 'hypo-' means 'under' or 'less than.' It's a relative term. It requires a reference point. Hypothermia is under normal body temperature. Hypoglycemia is under normal blood sugar. Hypoallergenic is under... nothing. No standard defines the baseline. No regulation specifies what the word is under."

"No definition at all?"

"The FDA's own website says: 'The term means whatever a particular company wants it to mean.' No standard. No testing protocol. No clinical demonstration required."

"But the prefix sounds medical."

"That's the work it does. 'Hypo-' belongs to the same family as hypothermia, hyponatremia, hypotension. Clinical prefixes. Quantitative prefixes. The consumer reads the prefix and hears precision — a measured reduction, a verified comparison. The word borrows the authority of medical Latin while delivering no medical content."

"Has anyone tried to define it?"

"The FDA proposed a rule in 1975. Products making the hypoallergenic claim would have to demonstrate, through comparative clinical testing, that they caused significantly fewer reactions than competing products. The rule specified human patch testing, sample sizes, statistical thresholds."

"What happened?"

"Almay and Clinique challenged it. The DC Circuit struck it down in 1977. The court found the statistical threshold 'arbitrary and capricious.' For products that already cause reactions in less than one percent of users, proving a significant reduction requires impossibly large sample sizes. The math was unworkable. The regulation was defeated."

"So the word survived the attempt to define it."

"By fifty years. The prefix keeps performing. The comparison keeps occurring. The comparator was proposed in 1975, defeated in 1977, and has not been re-attempted since."

Maren looked at the bottle. "So this is like 'natural' — another undefined word."

"Similar but different. 'Natural' is an empty lot. Nobody built on it. 'Hypoallergenic' is a ruin. The FDA tried to build a definition. The court demolished it. The foundation is still visible in the 1977 case record. The word stands where the regulation was supposed to be."

"A scar."

"A scar. The word performs a comparison in a silence that's fifty years deep. 'Less than' without 'less than what.' 'Under' without a ceiling. The prefix creates a relative term in an absolute vacuum."

Maren put the bottle in her basket. "My skin does react less to these products, though."

"It might. Many companies do formulate carefully — fewer common allergens, fragrance-free, simpler formulations. But they do this by choice, not by regulation. The word on the label describes an aspiration, not a verified outcome. The prefix performs the comparison the company's chemists may have attempted. Whether they succeeded — and by how much, and compared to what — the word cannot tell you."
