The Hardness

Thomas Clark filled a bottle with water from the supply pipe, added a measured quantity of soap solution, shook the bottle, and waited five minutes. If the lather persisted, he recorded a number. If it collapsed, he added more soap and tried again. The year was 1841. The place was Scotland. The method was a soap test. And the word he chose for what the test measured was "hardness" — not mineral content, not cation concentration, not dissolved-solids load, but hardness. The water that resisted soap was hard. The water that accepted it was soft. The measurement entered chemistry through laundry.

Clark also devised a unit: one degree of hardness equaled one grain of chalk dissolved per Imperial gallon. A grain is 64.8 milligrams. An Imperial gallon is 4.546 liters. One Clark degree equals approximately 14.254 parts per million. The unit survived. The test did not. Modern hardness measurement uses EDTA titration or ICP-OES, not soap. But the word — hardness — carries Clark's complaint. The water is not physically harder. It is chemically richer in divalent cations. The word means "resistant to soap." Every water utility that reports hardness in parts per million is reporting a soap-derived concept in chemistry-derived units — a hybrid that neither Clark nor the chemist who replaced him intended.

The reporting convention compounds the translation. Hardness is expressed as the equivalent concentration of calcium carbonate — CaCO₃ — regardless of which minerals actually make the water hard. A sample dominated by magnesium sulfate, containing no calcium carbonate at all, will still be reported as "X ppm as CaCO₃." The reference compound is a convention, not a description. The consumer who reads "210 ppm" assumes 210 milligrams of something per liter. The something is a translation: the actual minerals, converted to the amount of calcium carbonate that would produce the same soap resistance. The number is real. The compound is hypothetical.

Multiple scales divide the same water into different numbers. The Clark degree: one grain per Imperial gallon. The French degree: ten milligrams per liter. The German degree: ten milligrams of calcium oxide — not carbonate — per liter, adding a chemical conversion within the unit conversion. The grain per gallon: one grain per US gallon, not Imperial, the difference between 3.785 and 4.546 liters producing a 20% discrepancy from the Clark degree. The ppm as CaCO₃: the municipal standard. The mmol/L: the SI unit that almost no consumer has ever seen on a water report. Same water, six numbers, four reference compounds, two gallon definitions.

The USGS classifies water below 60 mg/L as soft, 61 to 120 as moderately hard, 121 to 180 as hard, above 180 as very hard. The water softener industry classifies 3.5 to 7 grains per gallon — approximately 60 to 120 ppm — as "moderately hard" and often recommends treatment. The boundary between "acceptable" and "hard" shifts with who draws it. The geologist surveys. The salesman sells. The word "hard" serves both, but the threshold serves different purposes.

The reported number contains two invisible halves. Temporary hardness — bicarbonates of calcium and magnesium — can be removed by boiling. The bicarbonate decomposes, releases carbon dioxide, and the insoluble carbonate precipitates as the scale in the kettle. Permanent hardness — sulfates and chlorides — remains after boiling and requires ion exchange or reverse osmosis. The utility report does not distinguish between them. The single number combines two chemical categories that respond to different treatments. The consumer who boils hard water removes part of the hardness. The consumer who installs a softener removes all of it. Neither consumer can read the distinction from the report.

The health dimension closes the loop. The WHO publishes no guideline value for hardness. Epidemiological evidence — Catling et al., 2008 — suggests possible cardiovascular benefit from hard water, particularly its magnesium content. Ion-exchange water softeners replace calcium and magnesium with sodium. The treatment that solves the soap problem may remove minerals that reduce stroke risk. The number that was born from soap now governs a decision with cardiovascular implications — a journey from lather to longevity that no single measurement was designed to contain.
