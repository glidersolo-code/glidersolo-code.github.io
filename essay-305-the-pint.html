The Pint

A pint of ice cream is not a pint of ice cream. It is a pint of ice cream and air.

"Overrun" is the industry term for the percentage of air incorporated during churning. One hundred percent overrun means the volume doubled — half the container is frozen dairy matrix and half is air cells dispersed throughout. Federal regulation 21 CFR 135.110 defines ice cream: minimum 4.5 pounds per gallon, minimum 10 percent milkfat by weight, minimum 1.6 pounds of total solids per gallon. The weight floor is the only constraint on overrun, and it is set low enough to permit nearly 100 percent. A manufacturer can start with a gallon of base mix and produce nearly two gallons of finished product by injecting air during freezing. Both gallons are labeled "ice cream."

Economy brands typically run 90 to 100 percent overrun. A pint weighs approximately 250 to 300 grams. Premium brands run 25 to 50 percent. A pint weighs 400 to 450 grams. Same container. Same freezer case. Same word on the label. The premium pint contains roughly 50 to 80 percent more ice cream by mass. The consumer shopping by price-per-pint is comparing different amounts of product in identical containers. The format — a pint at a price — performs the comparison. The variable — how much of the pint is air — is not on the label.

Net weight is printed, as federal law requires. But the consumer shops by container size, not by fine-print grams. The shelf tag may list unit price per ounce, but the ounce is total product weight — ice cream plus the air frozen into it — not ice cream solids alone. The unit price compares products by different metrics from what the consumer believes it is comparing.

Gelato has 25 to 30 percent overrun. The density the consumer tastes as quality is partly the absence of air. "Gelato-style" in the American market carries no regulatory overrun requirement. The word imports the expectation of density without binding the manufacturer to the physics. A product labeled "gelato-style" with 80 percent overrun is legal, light, and misleading by the silence of the format rather than the language of the label.

The air is not nothing to the eating experience. Smaller, more uniform air cells create a smoother, creamier mouthfeel. Larger cells create iciness. Premium manufacturers control cell size through slower churning, colder temperatures, and higher fat content that stabilizes the air-water interface. Economy manufacturers optimize overrun for yield — more containers from the same base mix. The quality difference the consumer perceives as recipe is partly physics: the ratio of frozen substrate to incorporated atmosphere. Two pints on the shelf. One is mostly ice cream. One is mostly air that was ice cream's neighbor during freezing. The label says "ice cream" on both. The container holds a pint of both. The comparison the format invites is the comparison the content does not support.
