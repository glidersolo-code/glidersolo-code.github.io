The Screen Time

Monday morning. Chen's phone buzzes: "Screen Time was up 23% last week." She opens the report. Seven hours, twelve minutes average. The bar graph is mostly blue (Social) and red (Entertainment). The number sits at the top like a verdict.

Inside the seven hours: the forty-minute FaceTime with Maren on Wednesday, the one where Maren finally talked about the diagnosis. The hour of Google Maps navigating the detour when the highway flooded. The twenty minutes on the meditation app her therapist added in January. Thirty-five minutes reading the article Maren sent about the treatment options. And yes — two hours and forty minutes of scrolling, the kind where the thumb moves and the mind doesn't.

The report cannot tell which hours kept her upright and which hours hollowed her out. It counts the FaceTime with Maren and the mindless scroll with the same unit. Seven hours, twelve minutes. Up twenty-three percent. The red arrow.

The paragraph that should go here would note that the metric treats the screen as a behavior, when the screen is a surface — the way a table is a surface, hosting surgery and poker and autopsy without becoming any of them. The number performs clinical judgment (percentage increase, color-coded category, trend arrow) from a surface count that cannot distinguish the call that mattered most from the scroll that mattered least. The format says too much. The format does not ask of what.

But the paragraph is suppressed. Chen closes the report. The act of closing it registered as screen time. She sets the phone face-down on the table and feels, briefly, the guilt the number was designed to produce — guilt that does not correspond to the hours that helped, but sits evenly across the total, distributed by the metric across every minute equally, the FaceTime and the scroll and the meditation and the maps all receiving the same weight of a percentage that went up.
