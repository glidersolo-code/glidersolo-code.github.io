The Percentage

The phone says: 30% chance of rain. The number guides the day — umbrella or no umbrella, outdoor plans or indoor, drive or walk. The percentage performs a single probabilistic claim about a single event: will it rain here, on me, today?

The number is not a single probability. The National Weather Service defines Probability of Precipitation using the equation PoP = C × A, where C is the forecaster's confidence that precipitation will occur somewhere in the forecast area, and A is the percentage of the area expected to receive measurable precipitation if it occurs. A forecaster 100% confident that rain will cover 30% of the area reports the same "30%" as a forecaster 50% confident that rain will cover 60%. A patchy drizzle and a coin-flip storm produce the same number. The consumer cannot decompose the product. The consumer receives "30%." The components are gone.

Gigerenzer and colleagues surveyed residents of five cities — Amsterdam, Athens, Berlin, Milan, New York — in 2005. The question: what does "30% chance of rain tomorrow" mean? Answers split roughly evenly among three interpretations: it will rain for 30% of the time, it will rain over 30% of the area, and on 30% of days like this it has rained before. No majority in any city selected the formal definition. The percentage format communicates a clarity the definition does not contain.

The threshold adds another layer. "Measurable precipitation" means at least 0.01 inches — approximately a quarter of a millimeter. A forecast of "60% chance of rain" is a 60% probability of exceeding this threshold. The threshold determines what counts as rain. It permits a drizzle lasting three minutes. It also permits a downpour lasting three hours. The percentage communicates likelihood of crossing the threshold without communicating what happens above it. A separate forecast product estimates amount, but the probability dominates the consumer interface — the umbrella icon, the percentage, the hourly color gradient. The number answers "will it rain?" without answering "how much?"

Temperature performs the same erasure differently. The app says "78°F." Not "76-81°F." Not "78 ± 3°F." A single digit. Mean absolute error at 24 hours: 2-3°F. At seven days: 5-6°F. By day nine or ten, the forecast loses skill entirely — performing worse than simply using the historical average for that date. The ten-day forecast displays ten temperatures in a row, each identically precise, each identically formatted, declining in accuracy from left to right while the display remains constant. The interface does not distinguish between the skilled early days and the unskilled later ones. The row of numbers performs uniformity over a gradient of deteriorating confidence.

The hourly forecast performs one more precision. "2 PM: 40%. 3 PM: 60%. 4 PM: 50%." Hour by hour, the probability changes, creating a temporal narrative — rain builds in the afternoon, peaks at three, subsides by four. But the underlying models have temporal granularity of one to six hours depending on resolution. The "hourly" display interpolates between model time steps. The interpolation is invisible. The consumer plans by hours. The display meets the consumer's temporal granularity by performing a temporal resolution the model may not support.

The format is the product. The product erases the components. The consumer reads a simple percentage and plans a day around an answer to a question the number was not designed to answer. Will it rain on me, at my house, at three o'clock? The number says 30%. The number means something else entirely. But the format — a percentage, a single number, clean and unambiguous on the screen — performs the answer to the question that was asked.
