Part CXLIX: The Symbol

Piece #1186: The Grid

In 1888, the grid was published in a journal for phonetics teachers. Eleven columns: bilabial, labiodental, dental, alveolar, postalveolar, retroflex, palatal, velar, uvular, pharyngeal, glottal. Eight rows: plosive, nasal, trill, tap, fricative, lateral fricative, approximant, lateral approximant. Each cell held one or two symbols — voiceless on the left, voiced on the right. The grid was made for teaching English and French. The grid was made well. It traveled. By 1900 it was used across Europe. By 1950, across the world. The sounds it was built to represent sat cleanly in their cells. The sounds it was not built to represent required footnotes, diacritics, appendices. The grid did not say: these sounds are peripheral. The grid said: here is the main chart. The sounds outside the main chart heard the distinction.

Piece #1187: The Cardinal

Daniel Jones stood in a laboratory at University College London in 1917 and produced a set of vowels. He pushed his tongue as far forward and as high as it could go: Cardinal Vowel 1. As far forward and as low: Cardinal Vowel 4. As far back and as low: Cardinal Vowel 5. As far back and as high: Cardinal Vowel 8. Between these extremes he placed four more, at perceptually equal intervals. Eight reference points. A quadrilateral. Jones called them articulatory definitions — positions of the tongue in the mouth. Decades later, Peter Ladefoged at UCLA X-rayed speakers producing the Cardinal Vowels and measured their tongue positions. The tongue positions did not match Jones's descriptions. What matched was the acoustic output — the first and second formant frequencies, the resonances that the ear distinguishes. The map was acoustic, not articulatory. The map worked perfectly. The explanation was wrong. Nobody changed the explanation because the map didn't need it. The Cardinal Vowels are still taught as tongue positions. The tongue positions are still inaccurate. The accuracy has never mattered because the ears agree.

Piece #1188: The Click

ǃXóõ has eighty-three click consonants. The IPA has five click symbols. Five symbols for eighty-three distinctions — the rest must be constructed from diacritics: a click symbol modified by a nasal marker, a voicing marker, an aspiration marker, a glottalization marker, combinations stacked vertically until the transcription looks like a mathematical formula rather than a word. The clicks were not always five. Before 1989, there were three. Before 1979, the IPA used borrowed symbols from Bantu orthographies that represented only the most common click types. The founders of the IPA did not speak languages with clicks. The chart was not hostile to clicks. It was designed by people for whom clicks were exotic — interesting, documentable, marginal. The chart allocated space according to the designers' phonological universe. That universe was European. The allocation was not a conscious exclusion. It was a default. The default is the strongest form of exclusion because no one has to defend it.

Piece #1189: The Diacritic

The base symbol is the sound. The diacritic is the modification. The architecture is clear: [t] is an alveolar plosive; [tʰ] is an aspirated alveolar plosive; [t̪] is a dental plosive; [t̪ʰ] is an aspirated dental plosive. The base is primary. The diacritic is secondary. But in Hindi, the distinction between aspirated and unaspirated is phonemic — [tʰ] and [t] are as different as [t] and [d] in English. In many Australian languages, the dental-alveolar distinction is phonemic — [t̪] and [t] contrast. The diacritic marks as secondary what the language treats as primary. The speaker hears a different sound. The chart sees the same sound with an added mark. The hierarchy is universal. The hierarchy comes from somewhere.

Piece #1190: The Segment

Robert Port and Adam Leary wrote in 2005 that the deepest assumption was not about any particular sound. It was about speech itself. The IPA assumes that the stream of speech — continuous, overlapping, coarticulated, no silence between words, no clear boundary between one gesture and the next — can be cut into a linear sequence of discrete units. One symbol follows another. Left to right. Like beads on a string. But the tongue does not wait for one sound to finish before beginning the next. The [n] in "tenth" is already dental because the tongue anticipates the [θ]. The [k] in "key" and the [k] in "cool" are different sounds — different tongue positions, different acoustic signatures — but they receive the same symbol. The chart says they are the same phoneme. The chart is right, in English. The chart is a theory about what sameness means, applied before the transcription begins. Every transcription is an analysis. Every analysis is a decision. The symbols look like they are writing down what they heard. The symbols are writing down what the chart taught them to hear.
