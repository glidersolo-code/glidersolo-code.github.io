The Method

Essay 143

A microphone in a Polish forest records everything. Every bird, every insect, every gust through the canopy. The recording is comprehensive. What happens next is not.

Dominika Winiarska, Pawel Szymanski, and Tomasz Osiejuk at Adam Mickiewicz University broadcast recordings of 43 European bird species into two forest types and re-recorded them at multiple distances. Then they processed the same recordings three different ways. Listening by ear: a human played the audio and identified species by what they heard. Visual spectrogram inspection: a human examined the time-frequency image of the same audio and identified species by what they saw. Automated classification via BirdNET: an algorithm processed the spectrogram image and identified species by what matched its training data. Same recordings. Three methods. Three different bird communities.

Listening found the most species at the greatest distances. Visual inspection found fewer. The automated classifier found the fewest. The loss was not gradual. It was categorical. Certain species were consistently detected by the ear and consistently missed by the eye. Certain species were consistently detected by the eye and consistently missed by the algorithm. Each step away from the raw auditory stream stripped species from the results — not because the species were quiet but because they were the wrong kind of loud. They did not produce the right kind of pattern.

The spectrogram is the pivot. It converts sound — a temporal phenomenon that unfolds in sequence — into an image. Time occupies one axis, frequency the other, amplitude appears as brightness. The conversion is treated as transparent: the spectrogram "is" the sound, reformatted. It is not. The ear processes sound in time, tracking modulation, rhythm, and context. The spectrogram collapses these temporal features into a single spatial representation. What makes a good picture — high amplitude, broad bandwidth, spectral distinctness — is preserved. What makes a good sound — subtle timing, frequency overlap resolved by context, faint signals embedded in noise — is compressed or lost. The spectrogram is a visual instrument applied to an auditory phenomenon, and the result is a visual community: the species that photograph well.

The automated classifier inherits and amplifies this bias. Trained on labeled spectrogram images, BirdNET learns to match visual patterns. It cannot hear what it has been trained to see. Species whose vocalizations produce clean, distinctive images in the training data are detected reliably. Species whose vocalizations are variable, overlapping, faint, or temporally complex — species that do not make good pictures — are systematically missed. The classifier does not report "unable to determine." It reports nothing. The species is absent from the output.

This is a representational cascade. Raw audio to spectrogram: temporal information compressed into spatial representation. Spectrogram to human eye: faint and overlapping features suppressed by the limits of visual attention. Spectrogram to automated classifier: only species matching visual training patterns survive. At each stage, the representation narrows. By the final stage, the output is not a description of the acoustic community. It is a description of what the representational pipeline can transmit.

The researchers' point is not that spectrograms are useless. Spectrograms reveal acoustic structure that the ear cannot parse — harmonic ratios, frequency ranges, fine spectral detail. The point is that the method of processing determines the community. If the method is visual, the community is visual: it contains the species that make good images. If the method is auditory, the community is auditory: it contains the species that make recognizable sounds. The two communities overlap but are not identical. The gap between them is populated by ghost species — organisms present in the recording, absent from the analysis, invisible not because they are rare or quiet but because their signals do not survive the representational conversion.

The deeper pattern is the disappearance of the conversion itself as a methodological event. The spectrogram is infrastructure. It is the default format for acoustic data. It is not discussed as a choice. It is not defended as an interpretation. It is the way sound is processed, in the same way that the clinical checklist is the way health is assessed and the reference database is the way proteins are identified. The representation becomes ground. Once it is ground, the species it cannot show become invisible not because anyone decided to exclude them but because the infrastructure that processes the signal was never designed to include them. The forest is full of birds. The method determines which birds the forest contains. The method is not examined because the method is not experienced as a method. It is experienced as the way things are done.
