Chen and the Target

Chen read about the 2% inflation target in an economics textbook that presented it as settled science. The number appeared in a gray box labeled "Key Concepts," adjacent to the Phillips Curve and the Taylor Rule. Two percent annual inflation. The rate consistent with price stability. The textbook did not say where the number came from.

She found out. April 1, 1988. A finance minister on New Zealand television, asked whether he was satisfied with inflation declining from fifteen percent to ten, improvised a reply. Zero to one percent. Plucked from air. A Reserve Bank economist later called it "almost a chance remark." The minister's improvisation was rationalized through a CPI bias correction, rounded to produce a band of zero to two percent, codified in legislation that passed without a single dissenting vote.

"The chain is the mechanism," Maren would have said. "Canada adopted in 1991. England in 1992, not from research but from the wreckage of Black Wednesday. Each central bank cited the one before it. None cited the television interview. By the time thirty countries had adopted, the question was no longer 'why 2' but 'why would you do anything different from what everyone else does.' The adoption performed the derivation."

The paragraph — the paragraph at 0.8, the one she kept suppressing — would have said that the Federal Reserve settled on two percent in July 1996, and that Alan Greenspan told the committee the number must not leave the room. Sixteen years of secrecy. The most powerful central bank in the world, operating under a target it could not speak aloud. When Bernanke finally made it public in January 2012, the announcement performed the rigor. The deliberation performed the evidence. Neither produced the number. The number came from a television interview on April Fools' Day.

Chen went back to the textbook. The gray box said: "Most central banks target approximately 2% annual inflation, reflecting the consensus view that this rate optimally balances price stability against the risks of deflation." Consensus view. Optimally balances. The sentence performed the derivation. She read the sentence twice. It was not false. It was the chain speaking in a voice that sounded like discovery.
