Part CDI: The Pyramid

Piece #2446: The Shape

Thomas opened a scan of the 1992 USDA Food Guide Pyramid. A triangle divided into horizontal bands. At the base, the widest band: "Bread, Cereal, Rice, & Pasta Group — 6-11 Servings." Above it, split into two panels: vegetables (3-5) and fruits (2-4). Above that: dairy (2-3) and meat (2-3). At the peak, the narrowest band: fats, oils, and sweets — "USE SPARINGLY."

The geometry communicated emphasis. Width equaled importance. The eye traveled from base to peak and understood: eat most grains, least fats. No fine print required. The shape was the message.

Thomas looked for who had designed the shape. He expected to find a committee of nutritionists. He did not find that.

Piece #2447: The Original

Thomas found Luise Light. She had been the USDA's Director of Dietary Guidance and Nutrition Education Research in the early 1980s. The USDA recruited her from New York University to replace the Basic Four food groups — the framework that had governed American nutrition education since 1956.

Light convened two expert panels: agricultural biochemists and medical scientists who studied diet and chronic disease. Her guide was the first to target levels for fat, sugar, sodium, fiber, calories, and trace minerals.

Thomas compared Light's version to the published version.

Light's base: fruits and vegetables, 5-9 servings. Published base: grains, 6-11 servings.

Light's grain recommendation: 3-4 servings of whole-grain breads and cereals. Published: 6-11 servings. No whole-grain distinction.

Light's peak: white-flour baked goods, crackers, processed starches — eat sparingly. Published peak: fats, oils, and sweets.

White flour moved from peak to base. The distinction between whole and refined vanished. The number tripled.

Piece #2448: The Secretary

Thomas traced the route of Light's guide through the USDA. She sent it to the Office of the Secretary of Agriculture — a political appointee. It returned with what she called "wholesale changes calculated to win the acceptance of the food industry."

Light was horrified. She warned her supervisors the changes would "lead to an epidemic of obesity and diabetes." Her quote: "No one needs that much bread and cereal in a day unless they are longshoremen or football players."

Thomas looked for what had happened in the Secretary's office. He found the dual mandate. The USDA had two statutory obligations: promote agricultural products and provide dietary guidance. The same department that was charged with selling American grain, beef, and dairy was also charged with telling Americans what to eat.

The guide that emerged was not a scientific document that had been corrupted. It was a negotiation that had been formatted as a scientific document. The pyramid shape — clean, geometric, authoritative — performed the scientific process that the political process had replaced.

Piece #2449: The Withdrawal

Thomas found the 1991 incident. In April, the USDA's "Eating Right Pyramid" was about to go to press. The National Cattlemen's Association saw media coverage and objected. The pyramid placed beef in the same visual tier as fats. The National Milk Producers Federation complained that dairy was too close to the peak.

On April 15, Secretary Edward Madigan — who had been in office for one month and had just met with the cattlemen's board — withdrew the pyramid indefinitely. His spokeswoman said the pyramid was "confusing" and had "not been tested on children."

The USDA hired a Boston market-research firm to test the design. Cost: $855,000 of taxpayer money. Duration: one year. The pyramid was republished in April 1992 with adjustments to address industry concerns.

Thomas read the Washington Post headline: "The $855,000 Pyramid."

The withdrawal was public. The lobbying was documented. The cost was reported. Marion Nestle, who had edited the 1988 Surgeon General's Report on Nutrition and Health, published the case study in the International Journal of Health Services in 1993. The capture was visible — and the pyramid went up anyway.

Piece #2450: The Distance

Thomas set Light's numbers beside the published numbers. Three servings versus eleven. Whole grains versus any grains. Base versus peak. The distance was measurable.

He checked the replacements. MyPyramid (2005): vertical stripes, a running stick figure. Nutritionists called it incomprehensible. MyPlate (2011): a plate divided into fruits, vegetables, grains, protein, and a circle for dairy. Walter Willett of Harvard: "Like the earlier pyramids, MyPlate mixes science with the influence of powerful agricultural interests."

Harvard published its own Healthy Eating Plate in September 2011, noting it was "based exclusively on the best available science and not subjected to political or commercial pressures." It was different. It distinguished whole grains from refined. It specified healthy oils. It recommended water instead of dairy.

Thomas looked at both plates. The USDA's and Harvard's. Same format — a plate divided into sections. Different content. The difference was the mandate. Harvard had one mandate: nutrition science. The USDA had two: nutrition science and agricultural promotion. The second mandate shaped every recommendation, and the plate format made the shaping invisible.

Three and eleven. The distance between those numbers was the distance between a nutritionist's guide and an industry document. The pyramid made the distance disappear — not by hiding it, but by being a pyramid. The shape stood alone. The shape did not carry footnotes. And the shape, for thirty years, was what hung on the cafeteria wall.
