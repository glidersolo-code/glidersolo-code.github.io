Part CCCLII: The Food Mile

Piece #2201: The Tomato

Two tomatoes. The first was grown in a field in Almería, southern Spain, under open sky and Mediterranean sun. It traveled 1,200 miles by refrigerated truck to a supermarket in London.

The second was grown in a heated greenhouse in Lincolnshire, 150 miles from the same supermarket. The greenhouse runs on natural gas. The heating system accounts for 56.8% of the farm's cumulative energy demand. The tomato traveled 150 miles.

The second tomato — the local one, the short-distance one, the one the label would call "British-grown" — produces three times the greenhouse gas emissions of the first.

The consumer reads "food miles" and buys the British tomato. The consumer acted on the variable that was legible. The variable that dominated the impact — how the tomato was grown, not how far it traveled — was never on the label.

Piece #2202: The Lamb

New Zealand lamb. Twelve thousand miles by container ship from Christchurch to Los Angeles. The journey takes weeks. The fuel is heavy. The distance is half the circumference of the earth.

The lamb grazed on open pasture year-round. Mild climate. No grain inputs. No heated barns. No feedlots. The production footprint — the 83% that food miles cannot see — is lower than American lamb raised on supplemental grain in managed lots.

Ocean freight is the most carbon-efficient mode of transport per kilogram-mile. The 12,000 miles by sea contribute less to the total footprint than the feed and heating that the American lamb consumed 50 miles from the consumer's door.

The consumer sees two packages. One says "New Zealand." The other says "Local Farm." The consumer chooses the local farm. The consumer optimized for the 4-11% variable and ignored the 83% variable, because the 4-11% variable had a number on it — miles — and the 83% variable did not.

Piece #2203: The Lettuce

California field-grown lettuce, shipped 3,000 miles east in a refrigerated truck: 0.31 kilograms of CO₂ per kilogram of lettuce.

Regional hydroponic lettuce, grown in a climate-controlled greenhouse 30 miles from the consumer: 6.2 kilograms of CO₂ per kilogram of lettuce. Twenty times the emissions.

The electricity for lighting, cooling, ventilation, and pumping in the hydroponic facility dominates the lifecycle. The truck across the continent is a rounding error by comparison. But the hydroponic lettuce can say "local." The California lettuce cannot.

The word "local" performs the environmental benefit. The lifecycle analysis — which would reveal the 20:1 ratio — does not appear on any label in any store.

Piece #2204: The Phrase

Tim Lang, professor at the SAFE Alliance in London, coined the phrase in 1994. He wanted language that "encapsulated the complexities of distance, energy, culture and trade" in terms ordinary people could find meaningful.

The phrase succeeded. "Food miles" entered policy documents, consumer campaigns, labeling schemes. The UK launched studies on reducing food miles. Retailers advertised "locally sourced." Farmers' markets organized around proximity.

The phrase succeeded because "miles" is the most intuitive unit imaginable. Everyone knows what a mile is. Everyone can picture the distance between two points on a map. The moral geometry is clear: shorter is better, closer is kinder, local is responsible. The metric maps directly onto moral intuition.

Lifecycle analysis — the method that would reveal the 83% vs. 11% split — requires expertise, data, modeling, and tolerance for counterintuitive results. The British greenhouse tomato is worse than the Spanish field tomato. The New Zealand lamb is better than the local lamb. The hydroponic lettuce is twenty times worse than the trucked lettuce. These results violate the moral geometry of proximity. They require the consumer to accept that distance is a poor predictor of impact. They require the consumer to learn a new metric.

No new metric appeared. "Food miles" held. Legibility won.

Piece #2205: The Eleven Percent

Weber and Matthews tested the entire U.S. food supply chain in 2008. Every mile. Every farm. Every processing plant. Every truck. Every refrigerator. The average American household's food consumption generates 8.1 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per year. The breakdown:

Production: 83%. All transportation: 11%. Final delivery (farm to retail): 4%. Processing, packaging, retail: 6%.

Substituting less than one day per week of red meat and dairy for chicken, fish, eggs, or plant-based alternatives reduces emissions more than buying 100% local food for every meal. The dietary shift addresses the 83%. Buying local addresses the 4%.

But "eat less red meat" is not a metric. It does not have a number. It does not map onto a distance. It does not fit on a label. It does not perform the moral geometry of closer-is-better.

"Food miles" fits on a label. Food miles has a number. Food miles maps onto a distance.

The metric that counts 4% became the metric. The variable that accounts for 83% has no label, no number, no phrase.
