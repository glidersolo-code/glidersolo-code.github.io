Part CCCL: The Impedance

Piece #2191: The Current

Fifty microamps at fifty kilohertz. The current enters through the left foot, travels up the left leg, crosses the pelvis, descends the right leg, and exits through the right foot. The journey takes a fraction of a second. The device records the impedance — 487 ohms.

That is the measurement. One number. One frequency. One pathway through part of one body.

The trunk — where the visceral fat sits, where the organs nest, where the body stores what matters most to the health questions the user is asking — contributes less than 5% of the total impedance. The current barely passes through it. The legs are most of the signal. The arms are invisible. The scale has no access to the majority of the body it is about to describe.

Piece #2192: The Equation

The impedance enters the equation. The equation was built by Lukaski in 1985, refined by Sun in 2003 from 1,829 subjects in the NHANES III database — 1,474 white, 355 Black, ages 12 to 94. The equation takes height squared divided by resistance, adds body weight, subtracts a fraction of age, adjusts for sex, and produces an estimate of fat-free mass.

Height squared divided by resistance. This single variable explains 82% of the variance. Adding body weight raises it to 92%. The impedance measurement — the thing the scale actually performed, the fifty microamps through the feet — contributes the remaining 8%.

The equation does the work. The impedance confirms it.

The manufacturer does not publish which equation it uses. The user cannot inspect the coefficients, cannot know which reference population calibrated them, cannot determine whether the equation was validated against DEXA or hydrodensitometry or a four-compartment model. The equation is proprietary. The display is public.

Piece #2193: The Thirteen

The screen lights up. Thirteen metrics, one after another:

Body fat: 22.4%. Muscle mass: 56.2 kg. Bone mass: 3.1 kg. Body water: 54.8%. Visceral fat rating: 7. BMR: 1,612 kcal. Metabolic age: 34. Subcutaneous fat: 18.1%. Protein: 17.6%. Skeletal muscle: 46.3%. Body fat mass: 16.8 kg. Lean body mass: 58.2 kg. BMI: 24.1.

Thirteen numbers. Each displayed to one decimal place. Each formatted as an independent measurement of a distinct compartment of the body. Each derived from the same 487 ohms.

Bone mass: 3.1 kg. Bone is dielectric. Impedance cannot detect it. The scale estimated bone as approximately 6% of fat-free mass — a fixed proportion applied identically to the marathon runner and the sedentary office worker. When compared to DEXA, the error ranges from 34% to 93%.

Visceral fat rating: 7. The current did not pass through the viscera. The scale computed this from age, sex, height, and waist circumference inferred from impedance patterns — demographic variables wearing the costume of a body scan.

Thirteen numbers from one number. The display is a hall of mirrors.

Piece #2194: The Water Glass

She steps on the scale at 6:47 AM, before coffee, after the bathroom, wearing nothing. Body fat: 22.4%.

She drinks 500 milliliters of water. Sixteen ounces. She steps on the scale again at 7:12 AM. Body fat: 20.1%.

She lost 2.3 percentage points of body fat in twenty-five minutes. By drinking a glass of water. The water lowered her impedance — more fluid means more conductivity — and the equation interpreted the lower impedance as more lean mass. The display performed a change in body composition from a change in hydration.

The same shift — two to nine percentage points — occurs with exercise within two hours, meals within three hours, menstrual cycle phase, ambient temperature, foot calluses that alter electrode contact, and time of day. The number the consumer tracks as a stable property of the body is primarily a measurement of how much water is in it right now.

But the display says "Body Fat %." Not "Impedance-derived estimate of body fat percentage assuming standard hydration." The format performs the stability.

Piece #2195: The Firmware

On March 3rd, his body fat reads 24.8%. On March 4th — same time, same hydration, same clothing, same stance on the same scale — it reads 22.1%.

The scale updated overnight. New firmware. New equation.

Eighty-two percent of major firmware updates across seven leading brands introduce statistically significant baseline shifts. The algorithm changed. The body did not.

He opens the app. The trend line shows a sudden improvement. The graph celebrates the drop with a green arrow. He feels good about his progress. The progress was written by an engineer who adjusted a regression coefficient by 0.04.

Tinsley and colleagues tested fifteen BIA devices on the same seventy-three people. The body fat estimates spanned a 15.2-percentage-point range. Two devices, same person, same morning: one says 18%. The other says 29%.

The display does not show which equation it used, which population calibrated it, or how last night's firmware changed the answer. The display shows a number. The number has a decimal point. The decimal point performs the precision.
