Part CCCXLV: The Housing

Piece #2166: The Name

Thomas reads the names. Administrative segregation. Special housing unit. Restrictive housing. Intensive management unit. Secure housing unit.

Thomas reads what the names describe. A cell six feet by nine feet. Twenty-three hours a day inside it. Meals through a slot in the door. One hour of recreation, alone, in a cage. Fluorescent lights on twenty-four hours. Duration: indefinite.

Thomas reads the name the names replaced. Solitary confinement.

"Solitary confinement" describes what the person undergoes. Solitary: alone. Confinement: enclosed. The person is alone and enclosed. The name names the experience.

"Restrictive housing" describes what the institution does. Restrictive: with modifications. Housing: a type of accommodation. The institution provides a modified accommodation. The name names the provision.

The subject of the sentence changed. From person to system. From suffers to provides. From alone to managed. The name is the reclassification.

Piece #2167: The Classification

Thomas learns the distinction. Disciplinary segregation is punishment. Administrative segregation is management.

The distinction matters because punishment triggers due process. Notice. A hearing. The opportunity to present evidence. Management does not.

The same cell. The same twenty-three hours. The same slot in the door. The same isolation. The same darkness. One is classified as punishment and requires a hearing. The other is classified as administration and requires a form.

Sandin v. Conner, 1995. The Supreme Court held that thirty days in solitary did not constitute "atypical and significant hardship" relative to ordinary prison conditions. Thirty days alone in a cell the size of an elevator was not atypical. The word "atypical" did the work. If the conditions are typical, there is no deprivation to challenge.

Thomas reads the word again. Administrative. The word performs procedure. The function performs punishment. The classification — one word — determines whether the Constitution applies.

Piece #2168: The Duration

Thomas reads about Albert Woodfox. Forty-three years and ten months. In a cell at Angola Prison, Louisiana. Six by nine feet. Twenty-three hours a day. Released on his sixty-ninth birthday.

Thomas reads about Kalief Browder. Arrested at sixteen. Bail set at three thousand dollars his family could not pay. Held at Rikers Island for three years without trial. More than two years in solitary. Charges eventually dismissed. The case: he was accused of stealing a backpack.

Browder killed himself at twenty-two. Two years after release. The solitary did not end when the door opened. The solitary continued inside.

Thomas reads about Thomas Silverstein. Thirty-six years. In federal solitary. Until his death.

The UN Mandela Rules: fifteen consecutive days constitutes torture. Woodfox served 15,995 days. The word "housing" covered all of them.

Piece #2169: The Syndrome

Thomas reads Grassian's study. 1983. Fifteen people in long-term solitary at Walpole prison. Fourteen exhibited significant psychiatric symptoms.

Hyperresponsivity. Hallucinations. Panic attacks. Cognitive dysfunction. Intrusive thoughts. Paranoia. Impulse control failure.

The symptoms have a name. SHU Syndrome. Named after the housing unit. The Special Housing Unit that produces the syndrome named after the Special Housing Unit.

Post-release mortality: people held in solitary are 24% more likely to die in the first year after release. 78% more likely by suicide. 127% more likely from opioid overdose in the first two weeks.

Thomas reads the word again. Housing. The word performs accommodation — a place to live, a roof, a shelter. The function produces a specific psychiatric syndrome with a name. The housing produces the syndrome named after the housing.

Piece #2170: The Proliferation

Thomas counts the names. Administrative segregation. Restrictive housing. Special housing unit. Security housing unit. Intensive management unit. Restricted housing unit. Administrative restrictive status housing. Special management unit. Communications management unit.

Nine names for one practice. A person alone in a cell for twenty-three hours a day.

Each name was adopted when the previous name accumulated enough negative association to threaten reform. "Solitary confinement" became visible. It was renamed "administrative segregation." Administrative segregation became visible. It was renamed "restrictive housing." Each rename performs the same move: replace the experiential with the institutional, the person with the system, the suffering with the management.

The proliferation fragments the data. Each state counts its own euphemism. No single federal count exists. The 2023 report that found 122,840 people had to assemble data from dozens of sources because the naming fragmented the number. The names hide the total by preventing anyone from adding it up.

One hundred and twenty-two thousand, eight hundred and forty people. In cells six by nine feet. For twenty-three hours a day. The names call it housing. The UN calls it torture. Between the name and the practice — between "housing" and "torture" — 122,840 people disappear.
