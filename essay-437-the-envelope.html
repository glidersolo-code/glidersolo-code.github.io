The Envelope

Guy Abeille was a young graduate of ENSAE, the French national statistics school, working at the Budget Directorate of the Ministry of Finance. In May 1981, François Mitterrand had just come to power. Pierre Bilger, the deputy head of the National Budget Office, summoned Abeille and his colleague Roland de Villepin. The instruction: Mitterrand wanted "an easy rule, which sounds like economics and can be used against ministers who march into his office to ask him for money."

Abeille entered the projected deficit of approximately 100 billion francs into a calculator and divided by GDP. The ratio came to roughly 3%. They considered 1% — impossible to achieve. They considered 2% — too constraining. Three had the right feel. A good figure, Abeille said later, one that "has travelled through the ages, reminiscent of the Trinity." The work took less than an hour. "Born on the back of the envelope, without any theoretical reflection." His own assessment of his own number: "devoid of the slightest economic sense."

The Maastricht Treaty, signed February 7, 1992, embedded the 3% in European constitutional law. It also set a debt ceiling of 60% of GDP — the simple average of the debt-to-GDP ratios of the twelve negotiating countries. A descriptive snapshot frozen as a legal obligation. The two numbers are mathematically consistent: a 3% deficit at 5% nominal GDP growth stabilizes debt at 60%. This relationship was presented as evidence of careful design. But the design was reverse-engineered from the coincidence. Abeille chose 3 from arithmetic. The twelve countries happened to average 60. The equation 3/5 = 0.6 was discovered after both numbers were already fixed.

The numbers needed academic validation. In January 2010, Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff published "Growth in a Time of Debt" in the American Economic Review — in the Papers and Proceedings issue, which is not peer-reviewed. The central claim: GDP growth drops sharply when public debt exceeds 90% of GDP. Average real GDP growth above the threshold: -0.1%. The paper arrived at the exact moment the developed world was debating stimulus versus austerity. Paul Ryan cited it as the only evidence in his budget proposal. Olli Rehn, EU Commissioner for Economic and Monetary Affairs, cited it in a letter to finance ministers.

In April 2013, Thomas Herndon, a twenty-eight-year-old doctoral student at UMass Amherst, opened Reinhart and Rogoff's actual Excel spreadsheet and found that the AVERAGE formula covered rows 30 through 44 instead of 30 through 49. Five countries excluded by a formula that stopped five rows too soon. The corrected average: not -0.1% but +2.2%. The difference between "high debt destroys growth" and "high debt is associated with modestly lower but still positive growth." Additionally, post-war data for three countries had been excluded — years of high debt and strong growth. New Zealand's sole remaining data point was 1951, a year of -7.6% growth caused by a waterfront strike. One anomalous year represented the entire country.

Reinhart and Rogoff acknowledged the spreadsheet error. They wrote that it did not "affect in any significant way the central message." Austerity continued. Google Scholar shows the original paper cited approximately three times as often as the critique. The IMF published "No Magic Threshold" in 2014. The evidence was debunked. The policies built on it were already structural.

Three acts of arithmetic, decades apart: a calculator on a desk in Paris (1981), an average of twelve countries' debt ratios (late 1980s), and an Excel formula that stopped five rows too soon (2010). One casual, one descriptive, one accidental. Together they constituted the evidentiary architecture for fiscal austerity across the developed world. The man who invented the first number called it "devoid of the slightest economic sense." The number governs twenty-seven nations. The envelope is stronger than the evidence.
