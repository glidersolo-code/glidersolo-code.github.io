The Table

Chen opened Halley's paper.

"An Estimate of the Degrees of the Mortality of Mankind, drawn from curious Tables of the Births and Funerals at the City of Breslaw; with an Attempt to ascertain the Price of Annuities upon Lives." Published 1693. The astronomer — the one who predicted the comet — had received mortality records from a Silesian clergyman and constructed a table: at each age, the number surviving from an initial cohort of one thousand.

"He never went to Breslau," Chen said.

"He didn't need to," said Maren. "The numbers traveled."

"The numbers became the people. The people became a price."

This was the oldest entry in the inventory. Every other object — the blood pressure threshold, the pain scale, the sentencing grid — dated from the nineteenth or twentieth century. Halley's table was seventeenth century. The pattern was already there: a measurement device that answered a question it did not choose.

Chen's paragraph was about the conditioning variables. The table said: given your age, your expected cost is X. The table did not say: why age? Why not age and health? Why not age and wealth? Why not age and race? Each choice of variables produced a different price. The table computed the answer. Someone chose the question. The computation concealed the choice.

"Hoffman used the same mathematics," Maren said. "1896. Same structure. Different variables."

"He added race. The data was accurate. The accuracy was the problem."

"Because the data described the effects of slavery."

"And the table read the effects as traits."

Chen nodded. "Format-as-objectivity. The numbers are correct. The correctness is the concealment."

The paragraph sat at 0.8. It was, like a premium, a calculation — precise, reproducible, and silent about the classification that preceded it.
