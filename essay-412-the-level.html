The Level

A person is "B2 in French." The label sits on a résumé, an immigration form, a university application. It performs a stable property of the speaker — a measured quantity, like blood pressure or credit score. The label is trusted because it looks like a measurement. A letter and a number. Clean, comparable, sortable.

The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages was not designed as a measurement instrument. The Council of Europe developed it between 1989 and 2001 as a pedagogical alignment tool — a shared vocabulary for describing what language learners can do, so that a teacher in Helsinki and a teacher in Lisbon could mean the same thing when they said "intermediate." The six levels (A1 through C2) are defined by task-based descriptors that are deliberately approximate: "can deal with most situations likely to arise while travelling" is B1. "Can understand the main ideas of complex text on both concrete and abstract topics" is B2. The framework does not specify what test to use, what score to require, or how to handle a person who reads at C1 and speaks at A2.

Language ability occupies at least eleven dimensions. The CEFR assigns one label. A cardiologist who reads English-language journals cannot order dinner in London. A teenager who texts in rapid colloquial French fails the formal writing section. The label must choose. It chooses by collapsing the profile into a composite, and the composite into a letter-number pair that fits on a form.

France requires A2 for a multi-year residence card, B1 for the ten-year card, B2 for citizenship. Germany requires B1 for permanent residency. Austria requires A1 before entry for family reunification. The UK raised its Skilled Worker visa to B2 in 2026. In every case, a descriptive framework designed for curriculum planning became a binary legal gate. The threshold fiction is not in the test — the test measures what it measures. The fiction is in the import: treating an approximate pedagogical description as a precise regulatory boundary.

The difference between high A2 and low B1 may be a single test item. One additional correct answer on a listening comprehension passage. One examiner's judgment of a spoken response. That difference determines whether a family lives together or apart. The framework was never designed for this weight. The alphanumeric format enabled the import — it looks like a grade, fits on an application, can be compared to a cutoff. The description became the threshold. The alignment tool became the gate.

A person who navigates daily life in three languages at A2 each may function more effectively than a B2 monolingual. The framework cannot see this — it tests one language at a time, treating each as a closed system, ignoring the multilingual reality where speakers code-switch and negotiate meaning across languages and function without native-like proficiency in any single one.

The level is not a lie. The level is a description doing the work of a measurement, in a system that needs it to be more precise than it is, applied to stakes its designers never imagined. The description performs the threshold. The curriculum guide performs the immigration gate. The approximate becomes the absolute at the moment the form is stamped.
