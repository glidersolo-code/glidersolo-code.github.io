Part CCIII: The Board

Piece #1456: The Rough

A sawyer in 1890 cuts a log into boards. The saw produces a plank roughly two inches thick and four inches wide. He sells it as a 2×4. The buyer receives a board whose cross-section matches its name. The number is the dimension. The dimension is the board.

Thomas's first clinical assessments were like this. A patient came in. Thomas asked questions. The patient's answers were the assessment. No instrument mediated. No standardization intervened. The number — if there was a number — described what it described.

The rough-sawn board is still available today. Specialty lumber yards sell it for fencing, barn construction, rustic furniture. A rough 4×6 is actually 4 by 6. The unprocessed version retains its name's truth. But it is rough — splintered, uneven, dimensionally variable with moisture changes. The accuracy is also the impracticality.

Piece #1457: The Shrinkage

The kiln removes moisture. Green wood at 30% moisture content shrinks as it dries to 6-12%. Tangential shrinkage runs 6-12%. Radial shrinkage runs 3-5%. A board that entered the kiln at 2 inches exits at approximately 1¾. The name does not follow the wood into the kiln.

Thomas watched depression scores shrink under treatment. A patient who entered therapy at PHQ-9 of 18 emerged at 8. The instrument said "moderate" became "mild." But the shrinkage was not uniform. Some symptoms dried faster than others. Sleep improved before motivation. Appetite returned before pleasure. The total score shrank, but the dimensions of the suffering shifted underneath it — some contracting, others barely moving, the instrument reporting only the aggregate.

The kiln does not shrink wood evenly. It shrinks more along the growth rings than across them. The board that emerges is not a smaller version of the board that entered. It is a differently shaped board wearing the name of its former self.

Piece #1458: The Planing

After drying, the board enters the planer. Four spinning cutter heads remove another quarter inch from each face. The surface becomes smooth, the dimensions uniform. The board is now usable — straight, predictable, stackable. It is also smaller. A 2×4 is now 1½×3½.

Thomas understood planing. The PHQ-9 was a planer — it took the rough surface of suffering and made it smooth, uniform, stackable. Nine questions, four choices each, a number from 0 to 27. The process that made the assessment usable was the process that removed material. The convenience was the loss.

But no one wanted the rough version. No one wanted the unprocessed interview, the two-hour clinical conversation, the idiosyncratic assessment that varied by practitioner. The planer existed because rough boards are difficult to build with. The PHQ-9 existed because unstructured clinical interviews are difficult to systematize. The instrument that removed material was the instrument that made construction possible.

Piece #1459: The Consent

In 1941, the National Lumber Manufacturers Association entered a consent decree. The court required an impartial oversight body because the industry's self-regulation had failed. The ALSC was created. The standard was formalized. The gap between name and dimension was not eliminated — it was authorized. The legal system looked at the discrepancy, understood it completely, and permitted it to continue.

Thomas found consent decrees everywhere in clinical measurement. The DSM — the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual — was a consent decree between psychiatry and insurance. The diagnoses were not descriptions of natural kinds. They were negotiated categories that both parties agreed to treat as real because the alternative — no shared vocabulary — was worse. The manual was revised every decade or so. Categories were added, removed, redefined. The names stayed. The conditions underneath shifted. Everyone knew this. Everyone continued.

A consent decree is not a finding of innocence. It is a finding that the discrepancy is tolerable — that the cost of maintaining the fiction is less than the cost of correcting it. The 2×4 is authorized to be 1½×3½ because the alternative — renaming every piece of lumber in every building code, span table, inventory system, and trade manual in the country — is more expensive than the confusion.

Piece #1460: The Board

The consumer holds a board. It is 1½ inches by 3½ inches. The label says 2×4. The consumer knows this, or doesn't. If they know, they translate automatically — 2×4 means 1½×3½, the way "a dozen" means twelve, the way "a fifth" of whiskey means 750 milliliters. If they don't know, they discover it when the shelf they're building comes out half an inch short, when the wall framing doesn't quite fit, when the measurement they trusted turns out to describe something other than what they measured.

Thomas held instruments. The PHQ-9 said 15. The Hamilton said 22. The BDI-II said 19. Three numbers, three scales, the same patient. Each instrument named the suffering differently. None of them named it wrong. Each named the rough-sawn version — the pre-process state of the patient before the instrument's particular planing operation removed what it couldn't hold.

The board is not named for what it is. It is named for what it was before the process that made it useful. The name preserves the memory of the raw material. The object embodies the finished product. Between the name and the object lies the process — the drying, the planing, the standardization — and the process is invisible. The consumer sees the name and holds the object and the gap between them is the format.
