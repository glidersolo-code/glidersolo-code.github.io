The Reversion

Chen is reading a news article. "They sent extra funding to the ten worst-performing schools. Eight of the ten improved."

Maren is folding laundry. "So the funding worked."

"Maybe. But eight of ten would have improved anyway."

She stops folding. "Why?"

"Because they were selected for being the worst. The ten worst schools in a given year are partly bad schools and partly unlucky schools — the year the flu went through during testing week, the year the strongest teacher retired mid-year, the year the test happened to emphasize the topics the school was weakest on. On the next measurement, the unlucky portion resets. The scores move toward the mean. Some improvement is the funding. Some is regression. The format — before and after — cannot separate them."

"But some of it is the funding."

"Some of it might be. To know how much, you need a control group — schools that were equally bad but received no intervention. If the control schools also improved, the improvement is regression. The difference between the two groups is the effect. But the newspaper doesn't have a control group. The newspaper has a before number and an after number. The before number was extreme. The after number was less extreme. The gap performs as evidence."

Maren folds a towel. "Galton."

Chen looks up. "You know Galton?"

"You've mentioned him. Tall parents, shorter children."

"1886. He measured parents and children. The extreme parents had less extreme children. He called it 'regression towards mediocrity.' The word 'regression' entered statistics from that paper. Most people who run regressions don't know the word comes from this exact phenomenon — the tendency of extreme observations to be followed by less extreme ones."

"So when a basketball player has an amazing game and then a normal game — "

"Regression. When a sick patient feels terrible on Monday and better on Tuesday — partly regression. When a pilot makes a perfect landing and then an average one — regression. Kahneman tells the flight instructor story. Israeli instructors noticed that praise was followed by worse performance and criticism by better performance. They concluded criticism works. The real explanation: an exceptional performance includes luck. Luck doesn't repeat. The next performance reverts. The instructor's criticism sits between two measurements that were going to diverge regardless. The criticism claims the credit."

"So the instructor is wrong."

"The instructor's observation is correct. Praise is followed by decline. Criticism is followed by improvement. The pattern is real. The causal interpretation is wrong. The architecture of randomness created a phantom pattern that feels true because it is confirmed every day."

Maren finishes folding. "That's the most disturbing one."

"Why?"

"Because the evidence is real. Twenty years of observation. Thousands of flights. The evidence is real and the conclusion is wrong. Not because the data is bad. Because the data is built to produce that conclusion. The architecture generates the pattern. The pattern generates the theory. The theory feels unshakable because the evidence keeps coming. And the evidence will always keep coming. Because the architecture doesn't change."

Chen puts down the article. "That's exactly it."
