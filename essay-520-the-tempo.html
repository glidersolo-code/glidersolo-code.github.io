The Tempo

Allegro means cheerful. From the Latin alacer — brisk, cheerful, lively. Not fast. Gioseffo Zarlino used the word in 1558 to describe how music should feel. Adriano Banchieri first placed it in a score as a performance instruction in 1611. But the instruction was: play this cheerfully. Over two centuries, the character meaning hardened into a speed meaning. The word that said "cheerful" came to say "fast." The character did not disappear. The character sank beneath the speed, still present, still shaping interpretation, invisible to anyone who reads the marking as a metronome range.

Every Italian tempo word is a mood descriptor performing as a speed instruction. Adagio: at ease. Andante: walking. Grave: heavy, solemn. Presto: quick, ready. The words describe qualities of motion. The performer reads them as rates. The conventional BPM range for allegro spans 109 to 132 by one standard source, 120 to 168 by another. The disagreement between sources is the evidence — the word appears to be a stable standard while the standard floats. A 40% variation between the slowest and fastest acceptable allegro. In the Baroque period, Largo was faster than Adagio. In modern usage, Largo is slower. The hierarchy has inverted. The words are the same.

The metronome was supposed to resolve this. Dietrich Nikolaus Winkel invented the double-weighted pendulum in Amsterdam in 1814. Johann Nepomuk Maelzel observed the invention, added a graduated scale, patented it in 1815, and manufactured it as "Maelzel's Metronome." The instrument designed to bring objectivity to musical tempo was itself born from appropriation.

Beethoven's response was self-contradictory. "It is silly stuff; one must feel the tempos." Then he reversed himself and published metronome markings for all eight symphonies in the Leipzig Allgemeine Musikalische Zeitung on December 17, 1817. But even in his enthusiasm: "100 according to Maelzel, but ... sentiment also has its tempo and cannot be completely expressed by this number." On the autograph of the Ninth Symphony: "108 or 120 Maelzel." Not a range. An uncertainty.

Peter Stadlen found that 66 out of 135 important markings were "absurdly fast and thus possibly wrong." Martin-Castro and Ucar analyzed 36 complete symphonic recordings — 1,188 audio files, more than 169 hours. All 36 conductors played slower than Beethoven's marks. Not some. All. Historically Informed performers averaged 6 BPM slower. Romantic-tradition conductors averaged 13 BPM slower. Only one marking — 72 BPM for the Seventh Symphony's fourth movement — was universally accepted. One number out of hundreds.

Martin-Castro and Ucar proposed that the early metronome's 15-millimeter triangular weight caused a systematic reading error of approximately 12 BPM. The "108 or 120" annotation on the Ninth spans exactly 15 millimeters on the metronome scale. The most authoritative tempo markings in history may be a calibration artifact. The number performs as Beethoven's intention. The number may contain the instrument's design flaw.

Rudolf Kolisch argued in 1943 that tempo and character are inseparable — that allegro actually means "cheerful" and the correct tempo can be deduced from the character the music requires. His presentation provoked a public confrontation with Artur Schnabel. The argument that the word means what it literally says was treated as radical.

The structural property is notation. The Italian word encodes character but performs as speed. The metronome number performs as precision but performers treat it as approximate. Together the two systems perform completeness — the word names the character, the number specifies the speed, the instruction appears determined. But the word covers a 40% range and performers reject half the numbers. What emerges in performance lives in a space between the two systems that neither can reach. The notation performs as an instruction. The performer interprets. The interpretation is invisible because the score performs as if it contains the answer.

Nine formats. Nine structural properties. Nine concealments. Transparency, monotonicity, relativity, origin, minimum, discretization, composition, averaging — and notation. Each format is an instrument. Each instrument has architecture. The architecture determines what the instrument can express. What the instrument cannot express is not hidden, not suppressed, but structurally inexpressible. The format shapes the finding. The notation shapes the tempo. The tempo lives where the format cannot reach.
