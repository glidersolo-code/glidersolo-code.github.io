Essay 366: The Label

The sticker says organic. The consumer reads: healthier, cleaner, more natural, better for the earth. The sticker certifies: which inputs were used during production, which practices were followed, which agents inspected the operation. The gap between what the sticker certifies and what the consumer reads is where the premium lives.

The USDA National Organic Program, effective October 2002, defines organic production in terms of process. No synthetic pesticides. No synthetic fertilizers. No genetically modified organisms. No irradiation. No sewage sludge. Soil-building practices required. Third-party certification by accredited agents. Annual inspections. The standard is detailed, specific, enforceable. It specifies what was done to the soil and the seed. It does not specify what ended up in the fruit.

The consumer wants to know about the fruit. Stanford's systematic review in 2012 found "little significant difference in health outcomes or nutrient content." A different meta-analysis in 2014 found organic crops had higher antioxidant concentrations and lower cadmium. The evidence disagrees. What the evidence agrees on is the gap: the certification measures process, the question the consumer asks is about outcome, and the two are related but not identical.

The pesticide question sharpens the gap. The consumer reads "organic" as "no pesticides." The standard says "no synthetic pesticides." Organic farming uses approved pesticides from the National List: copper sulfate, pyrethrin, rotenone, neem oil, spinosad, Bacillus thuringiensis. These are not synthetic. They are also not harmless — copper sulfate is a heavy metal that accumulates in soil; rotenone causes Parkinson's-like symptoms in laboratory animals. The standard draws its line at the distinction between synthetic and natural. The consumer draws the line between present and absent. The two lines are not the same line.

The environmental claim depends on the denominator. Organic yields are roughly twenty to twenty-five percent lower than conventional. Per unit of food produced, organic uses more land, may generate more greenhouse gas, may require more water. Per unit of land, organic uses fewer chemical inputs, supports higher biodiversity, builds soil carbon. The environmental case is real if measured by the acre. It is questionable if measured by the calorie. The claim changes with the denominator. The denominator is rarely stated.

The word does the work. "Organic" carries a meaning older and broader than any regulatory standard. It means of the body, of the earth, of the living. When attached to food, it performs naturalness — the absence of artifice, the presence of care. The standard narrows the word to a production checklist. The consumer hears the older, broader word. The premium — twenty to a hundred percent more — compensates the farmer for the production process. The consumer pays it for the product quality. What the farmer is paid for and what the consumer pays for are not the same thing. The label connects them. The label is the bridge. The bridge conceals the gap.

A process label performing as an outcome label. The sticker says how it was made. The consumer reads what it is. Both readings are sincere. Both readings are incomplete. The concealment is not in the dishonesty of either party but in the gap between the two sincerities — the producer who accurately certifies the process, the consumer who sincerely infers the outcome, and the word that lets both believe they are talking about the same thing.
