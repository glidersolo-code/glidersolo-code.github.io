Part CXCII: The Charge

Piece #1401: The Digit

Five thousand milliamp-hours. The number appears on the spec sheet, in the review, on the comparison chart. Five thousand. A four-digit number that sounds substantial — sounds like a lot of something. The consumer who sees 5,000 and remembers last year's 4,500 calculates an eleven percent improvement and expects eleven percent more time between charges.

Milliamp-hours measure charge. Not energy. Not runtime. Not the thing the consumer actually experiences, which is hours of screen time before the notification says 20%. Charge is how many electrons the battery can push. Energy is how many electrons times how hard it pushes them. The distinction is voltage. A 5,000 mAh battery at 3.7 volts stores 18.5 watt-hours. The same 5,000 mAh at 7.4 volts stores 37. The mAh is equal. The energy is double. The number on the spec sheet cannot distinguish them.

Thomas's PHQ-9 score measures something similarly partial. A score of 14 captures the frequency of symptoms — how many days, how often. It does not capture the voltage: how hard each symptom hits, how deep the trough goes, how much energy the recovery costs. Two patients at 14. Same charge. Different voltage. Different energy in the system. The number cannot distinguish them.

Piece #1402: The Conversion

A power bank is marketed as 20,000 mAh. The consumer calculates: my phone is 5,000 mAh, so the power bank should charge it four times. The consumer gets three and a half and suspects the product is defective.

The physics is straightforward. The power bank's cells store energy at 3.7 volts. The USB output delivers at 5 volts. Voltage conversion requires a circuit — a boost converter — that loses energy as heat. Fifteen to thirty percent, depending on the circuitry and the cable. Twenty thousand milliamp-hours at 3.7 volts is 74 watt-hours. After conversion to 5 volts with 20% loss, the deliverable energy is roughly 59 watt-hours, equivalent to approximately 11,800 mAh at 5 volts. Divide by 5,000: 2.4 full charges, not four. Add the phone's own charging losses — another 5-10% — and the consumer's three and a half charges is actually generous.

The number 20,000 is not false. It describes the internal charge at the internal voltage. But the consumer will never see the internal voltage. The consumer sees the USB port, the cable, the phone screen ticking upward. The number describes a reality the consumer cannot access.

Thomas's clinical records describe internal states at internal voltages. The therapist's notes — "patient reports improvement in sleep, continued difficulty with concentration, affect somewhat brighter" — capture a charge at a particular voltage. The insurance company sees the billing code, the session count, the diagnosis. The conversion from clinical observation to administrative record loses something, the way 3.7 volts loses something becoming 5 volts. What arrives is less than what was stored.

Piece #1403: The Regulator

The FAA limits lithium-ion batteries in carry-on luggage to 100 watt-hours. Not milliamp-hours. Watt-hours. The distinction is not pedantic. It is the difference between measuring a fire risk and measuring a marketing number. A battery's thermal runaway severity — how violent the fire, how much heat, how fast the cascade — depends on stored energy, which is watt-hours. A 100 Wh limit corresponds to approximately 27,000 mAh at 3.7 volts. The same limit at 7.4 volts corresponds to 13,500 mAh. The mAh number moves. The energy limit does not.

The regulator uses the unit that captures the hazard. The marketer uses the unit that produces the largest number. Both appear on the battery, if the consumer reads the fine print. The large print says 20,000 mAh. The fine print says 74 Wh. The regulatory ceiling is in one language. The marketing floor is in another.

Thomas's medications carry the same duality. The milligram dosage — the marketing-facing number, the one the patient remembers and reports — measures the amount of the drug in the tablet. The bioavailability — the fraction actually absorbed and reaching systemic circulation — is the regulator-facing number, the one the pharmacologist cares about. Two hundred milligrams of one formulation may deliver less active compound than fifty milligrams of another. The milligrams are the mAh. The bioavailability is the watt-hours. The prescription is written in milligrams.

Piece #1404: The Comparison

A reviewer compares two phones. Phone A: 4,500 mAh. Phone B: 5,000 mAh. The review declares Phone B the winner in battery capacity. Eleven percent more mAh. The conclusion seems inescapable.

But Phone A uses a more efficient processor — five nanometers instead of seven. It drives its display at a lower refresh rate during static content. Its modem draws less power in weak signal conditions. Phone A lasts fourteen hours of screen time. Phone B lasts twelve. The phone with less charge lasts longer. The comparison was valid for the number and wrong for the experience.

The reviewer knows this. The runtime test is published alongside the mAh figure. But the mAh comparison is what fits in the headline, the spec table, the quick-reference card. The runtime test requires conditions — brightness level, Wi-Fi or cellular, video playback or web browsing — and conditions complicate. The mAh is clean. One number, one unit, one winner. The fact that the winner is not the winner is a detail the format cannot accommodate.

Thomas's comparison: two patients with PHQ-9 scores of 12 and 15. The higher score suggests more severity. But the patient at 12 has no social support, no insurance, no therapist — and functions at a level the clinician finds alarming. The patient at 15 has a stable marriage, a flexible job, and a weekly therapy appointment — and functions at a level the clinician finds adequate. The number says fifteen is worse. The clinician says twelve is more concerning. The comparison was valid for the number and wrong for the experience.

Piece #1405: The Unit

Watt-hours would solve the problem. A single number — energy content, directly comparable across voltages, directly predictive of runtime per unit of power consumption. Eighteen point five watt-hours versus seventeen. The comparison would be valid. The number would be correct.

But eighteen point five does not sound like five thousand. Two digits do not compete with four. The unit that captures the reality produces a number that loses the market. The phone manufacturer who prints "18.5 Wh" next to a competitor's "5,000 mAh" has told the truth and lost the sale. The consumer sees 18.5 and sees small. Sees 5,000 and sees large. The unit is a lens. The lens magnifies what it measures.

One hundred and ninety-two Parts. Thomas's units are all lenses. The PHQ-9 scores — 0 to 27 — are not inherently meaningful. They are meaningful because the scale was chosen, the thresholds were set, and the number was placed on a range that produces integers large enough to seem diagnostic and small enough to seem manageable. The unit magnifies what it measures. What it measures is not the whole charge.
