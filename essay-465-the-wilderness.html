The Wilderness

The statute says "recognized." Not created, not established, not designated — recognized, as if the law were merely opening a curtain on a stage that had always been set. A wilderness is an area "where the earth and its community of life are untrammeled by man, where man himself is a visitor who does not remain."

The Ahwahneechee had remained for six centuries.

Howard Zahniser chose the word "untrammeled" across sixty-some drafts, and the precision matters. Untrammeled means unconfined, unrestricted — free from human control. Not untrampled. Not undisturbed. The distinction sits in the statute like a trapdoor: the land may be disturbed by lightning, flood, wind, and earthquake, and remain wilderness. Only human disturbance disqualifies. The category demands that nature act upon the land and humans refrain.

But the Ahwahneechee did not refrain. The Crow did not refrain. The Shoshone and the Bannock and the Blackfeet did not refrain. They burned. They pruned. They cleared springs and diverted streams. The open forests that Muir rhapsodized were products of millennia of prescribed fire. The meadows were maintained. The understories were managed. The landscape that justified protection was the landscape that management had produced.

The Mariposa Battalion entered Yosemite Valley in 1851 and removed the Ahwahneechee. The park was established in 1890. The last families were evicted in 1969. Mark David Spence documented the "separate but symbiotic developments of the Indian reservation and recreational park systems." The reservation cleared the land. The park preserved the clearing. The word "wilderness" named the result.

Then the fire stopped, and the wilderness began to disappear. A century of suppression thickened the forests, choked the meadows, shifted species composition, accumulated fuel. The landscapes lost the character that had made them worth protecting. When ecologists proposed prescribed fire to restore what suppression had destroyed, wilderness policy classified human-ignited fire as "trammeling." The word that was written to protect the land now prevented the practice that had produced it.

Brodie and colleagues, writing in Fire Ecology in 2024, titled their paper "Untrammeling the wilderness" — and the verb was the argument. The century of fire suppression was the trammeling. The absence of human fire was the intervention. The law that prohibited human action in wilderness had, by prohibiting the action that maintained it, committed the most consequential human intervention of all.

The definition works like this: remove the managers; rename the result "nature"; protect the name; watch the condition degrade; discover that the protection prevents the restoration; conclude that the definition is the problem. But the definition is not the problem. The definition is the product. What the law calls "wilderness" is what remains after the people who tended the land are gone. The word does not describe a natural condition. The word describes an erasure and names it nature.

One hundred eleven million acres across eight hundred wilderness areas in forty-four states. The word governs more land than many nations contain. And the word works because it uses the indicative mood — "is recognized," "is characterized," "has been affected primarily by the forces of nature" — rather than the conditional or the historical. The grammar performs the naturalness. A different grammar would expose it: this land was cleared of its human stewards, and the clearing was called preservation, and the preservation was called recognition of what was always there. But that grammar is the one the statute cannot use. The definition requires the erasure. The erasure is the definition.
