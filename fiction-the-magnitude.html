The Magnitude

Thomas, Part CVII

976. The Amplitude

Charles F. Richter published "An instrumental earthquake magnitude scale" in the Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America in 1935 — volume 25, pages 1 to 32. The scale measured the logarithm of the largest amplitude recorded on a Wood-Anderson torsion seismometer at a standard distance of 100 kilometers. The number was the peak. The amplitude was what the seismograph needle swung to at its maximum displacement, and Richter used that single peak to represent the earthquake's size. The scale was local: designed for Southern California, for a specific instrument, for a specific range of distances and magnitudes. It was a local measure published as a general magnitude.

Thomas's clinical severity is measured by amplitude. The PHQ-9 scores each of nine symptoms by frequency — not at all, several days, more than half the days, nearly every day — and the severity rating is the sum. The sum is dominated by the largest amplitudes: the symptoms that score 3, the days that are nearly every day, the items that swing the needle to maximum displacement. The score captures the peak. It uses that peak to represent the condition's size. The scale was designed for primary care screening — a specific clinical setting, for a specific population, for a specific range of severity. It was a local measure deployed as a general magnitude.

977. The Saturation

Above approximately magnitude 7, the Richter scale saturates. The surface waves measured by ML have a characteristic period, and very large earthquakes release their energy at longer periods that surface-wave measurements cannot capture. A magnitude 7.5 earthquake and a magnitude 9.0 earthquake can produce similar surface-wave amplitudes. The instrument reaches its ceiling not because the earthquake has reached its ceiling but because the property the instrument measures — surface-wave amplitude at short periods — has a natural limit. The energy continues to increase. The number stops distinguishing.

Thomas's instrument saturates. The PHQ-9 has a maximum score of 27. A patient scoring 24 — severe depression with suicidal ideation, anhedonia, psychomotor disturbance — and a patient scoring 26 — the same, plus insomnia, appetite change, concentration failure — occupy different clinical territories. The instrument cannot distinguish them. The energy continues to increase. The number stops resolving. Above a certain severity, the measurement is a ceiling, not a magnitude. The instrument reports severe for everything above the saturation threshold, and the system treats severe as a single category rather than as a region where the instrument has stopped measuring.

978. The Moment

In 1977, Hiroo Kanamori at Caltech developed the moment magnitude scale. In 1979, Thomas Hanks and Kanamori published its formal definition in the Journal of Geophysical Research — volume 84, pages 2348 to 2350. Moment magnitude is calculated from the seismic moment: the product of the fault's rupture area, the average slip across the fault, and the rigidity of the surrounding rock. It measures the total energy released, not just the surface-wave amplitude. It does not saturate. A magnitude 7 earthquake and a magnitude 9 earthquake are clearly distinguished by Mw because the seismic moment captures the full geometry of the rupture — how large the fault is, how far it moved, how stiff the rock is. The moment is a structural measurement. It captures the cause, not just the signal.

A moment magnitude exists for Thomas. Not the PHQ-9's amplitude measurement but a structural assessment: how large the fault is — how much of his life the condition ruptures. How far it moved — how much function has shifted from baseline. How stiff the rock is — how resistant his situation is to therapeutic intervention. Chronic caregiving for a medically complex child is high-rigidity rock: the fault cannot slip back easily because the condition is structural, not acute. The moment captures this. The PHQ-9 does not. The PHQ-9 measures surface-wave amplitude. The moment magnitude exists. It is published. Clinical assessment methods that capture fault area, average slip, and structural rigidity have been validated. They are not used because the seismograph on the clinician's desk is a Wood-Anderson torsion instrument, and the Wood-Anderson is the instrument the network was built to read.

979. The Name

When an earthquake occurs, the media reports the magnitude. The number is moment magnitude — Mw. The name they use is the Richter scale. "A 7.0 earthquake on the Richter scale." The number comes from Kanamori's method. The name comes from Richter's. The public hears the name and applies the original method's conceptual framework to a number produced by a different method. The Richter scale is logarithmic, local, amplitude-based, and saturates. Moment magnitude is logarithmic, global, moment-based, and does not saturate. The numbers agree for moderate earthquakes — between about 3 and 7, ML and Mw are similar. Above 7, they diverge. But the media does not report "on the moment magnitude scale" because the public recognizes "Richter" and does not recognize "moment magnitude." The name persists after the method it named has been replaced.

Thomas's condition is reported in clinical shorthand. "Moderate depression." "Caregiver burnout." The names come from an older conceptual framework — categorical diagnosis, stage-based burden assessment. The actual measurements, when they are made, use newer instruments with different assumptions. But the names persist because the clinical ecosystem recognizes them. The clinician writes "major depressive disorder, recurrent, moderate" — a name from the DSM framework — and the number that justifies the name comes from the PHQ-9, which measures something different from what the DSM category implies. The name persists after the method has been replaced, and the persistence conceals the replacement.

980. The Log

Magnitude is logarithmic. Each whole number increase represents a tenfold increase in measured amplitude and approximately a 31.6-fold increase in released energy. The public treats it as linear. A 7.0 and an 8.0 feel "one unit apart" to a layperson who has never experienced either. The 8.0 releases 31.6 times more energy. A 9.0 releases 1,000 times more energy than a 7.0. The mathematical structure of the scale — that equal intervals represent exponential differences — is invisible to the user who encounters only the number.

Thomas's severity scores are treated as linear by the system that processes them. A PHQ-9 score of 10 and a score of 15 are "5 points apart." A score of 15 and a score of 20 are "5 points apart." The clinical difference between 10 and 15 may be manageable to catastrophic, while the difference between 15 and 20 may be catastrophic to unsurvivable. The mathematical structure of clinical severity — that equal point intervals may represent unequal intervals of suffering — is invisible to the system that reads only the number. One hundred and seven Parts, and the mechanism is the magnitude: a number whose mathematical structure is invisible to the user, whose name persists after its method has been replaced, and whose saturation point is treated as a category rather than as the instrument's failure to continue measuring.
