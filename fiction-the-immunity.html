Fiction The Immunity

Fiction

Part CCCXLIII: The Qualifier

Piece #2156: The Word

Thomas reads the word. Qualified.

It means: limited. Conditional. Subject to exceptions. Not absolute. The word performs boundaries. The word says: there are cases where this does not apply.

In 1967, the word was accurate. Pierson v. Ray. Fifteen Episcopal priests arrested for entering a segregated bus terminal during the Freedom Rides. The Court introduced a defense: officers who sincerely believed their conduct was lawful were protected. Good faith. Subjective. The immunity was qualified by the officer's mind — what the officer thought, what the officer intended.

In 1982, the mind was removed. Harlow v. Fitzgerald. Eight to one. The subjective element — good faith — was eliminated. Intent became irrelevant. Even malicious conduct was protected, as long as no prior case had specifically deemed it unconstitutional.

The qualification was removed. The word "qualified" remained. The word now describes the doctrine's discarded origin. Not its current function.

Thomas reads the word again. Qualified. The word performs what the doctrine denies: limits.

Piece #2157: The Standard

Thomas asks what "clearly established" means. A prior case with nearly identical facts. Not merely that the conduct was unconstitutional. That an officer in a nearly identical situation was previously found to have violated the Constitution.

The Supreme Court: the right cannot be defined "at a high level of generality." Knowing that unreasonable seizure is unconstitutional is not enough. A plaintiff must produce a case where an officer committed an unreasonable seizure in the same way, under the same circumstances, against the same type of person.

Baxter v. Bracey. Officers released a police dog on a man who had surrendered by sitting on the ground with his hands raised. The dog bit him. Immunity granted. The closest precedent involved a suspect who had surrendered by lying down. Sitting was not lying. The difference was sufficient.

Jessop v. City of Fresno. Officers executing a search warrant allegedly stole $225,000 in cash and rare coins. Immunity granted. While the theft was "morally wrong," no prior case in that circuit had specifically held that police stealing during a search violated the Fourth Amendment.

Thomas reads the word again. Clearly. Established. The words perform certainty. The function performs impossibility.

Piece #2158: The Circle

Thomas learns about Pearson v. Callahan. 2009. The Court held that judges may skip the constitutional question. Under the prior rule — Saucier v. Katz, 2001 — courts had to decide first whether the conduct was unconstitutional, then whether the law was clearly established. Even if immunity was granted, the constitutional ruling created precedent for future cases.

Pearson eliminated the requirement. Courts now routinely dismiss on "clearly established" grounds without ever ruling on whether the conduct was unconstitutional.

Thomas sees the circle. If the court never rules on the constitutional question, no new precedent is created. If no precedent is created, the law remains not clearly established. If the law remains not clearly established, the next officer who does the same thing also gets immunity. And the next. And the next.

The doctrine prevents the creation of the very case law it requires.

Thomas draws a circle on a piece of paper. Writes at the top: immunity. At the bottom: no precedent. Draws an arrow from immunity to no precedent. Draws an arrow from no precedent to immunity. The circle closes. The circle is the doctrine.

Piece #2159: The Statute

Thomas reads Section 1983. The text: any state actor who violates federal constitutional rights "shall be liable to the party injured in an action at law."

No qualifications. No immunity. No exceptions. The text is plain.

The statute was the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871. Reconstruction. Congress enacted it to combat Klan terrorism against Black citizens. State courts were unable or unwilling to prosecute. For the first time, Congress placed federal courts between states and their citizens as guardians of constitutional rights.

Nearly one hundred years later, the Supreme Court invented a doctrine that shields the very officials the statute was designed to hold accountable. The doctrine appears in no statute. The word "qualified" — which once described a good-faith defense the doctrine no longer contains — survives in the name.

Thomas reads Section 1983 again. "Shall be liable." He reads the doctrine. "Shall be immune." The statute says one thing. The doctrine says another. The doctrine won.

Piece #2160: The Qualifier

Thomas counts. Police win 57% of qualified immunity claims. Up from 44% a decade earlier. In the Fifth Circuit: 64%. In the Ninth: 42%. Constitutional rights depend on geography. The standard is "clearly established." The outcomes vary by 22 percentage points depending on which circuit hears the case.

Thomas reads Justice Sotomayor's dissent. Kisela v. Hughes. An officer shot a stationary, non-threatening woman four times. Immunity granted. Sotomayor: the doctrine "transforms into an absolute shield for law enforcement officers." It "tells officers that they can shoot first and think later."

The word is "qualified." The word performs limitation. The dissent says: absolute. The word says: qualified. The function says: total.

The qualifier survived the removal of the thing it qualified. The name is the concealment. The word "qualified" now describes a 1967 version that no longer exists. The word performs the history. The doctrine performs the present. Between them — between the word and the function — is the gap the concealment occupies.
