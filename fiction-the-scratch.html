The Scratch

Thomas, Part CVIII

981. The Reference

Friedrich Mohs was a professor of mineralogy at the Johanneum in Graz when he published his classification system in 1812. The system was not a measurement. It was a comparison. He selected ten minerals — talc, gypsum, calcite, fluorite, apatite, orthoclase, quartz, topaz, corundum, diamond — and arranged them so that each could scratch all those below it and be scratched by all those above. The system answered one question: which of two minerals is harder? The answer was binary — scratches or does not — and the ranking was ordinal. Each mineral received a number from 1 to 10. The numbers indicated order. They did not indicate magnitude.

Thomas's screening instruments assign numbers. The PHQ-9 scores depression from 0 to 27 in integer increments. The GAD-7 scores anxiety from 0 to 21. The Zarit Burden Interview scores caregiver strain from 0 to 88. Each instrument answers one question about each symptom: how often? The answer is ordinal — not at all, several days, more than half the days, nearly every day — and receives a number from 0 to 3. The numbers indicate order. They do not indicate magnitude. But they are printed as integers, summed as integers, and entered into the chart as a total that looks like a measurement. A PHQ-9 score of 20 does not mean twice the depression of a score of 10. The scale does not make that claim. The number makes that suggestion.

982. The Interval

The absolute hardness of Mohs's reference minerals, measured by the Vickers indentation test or the Knoop scratch method, is not linear. Talc: approximately 1 on the Knoop scale. Gypsum: 32. Calcite: 135. Fluorite: 163. Apatite: 430. Orthoclase: 560. Quartz: 820. Topaz: 1340. Corundum: 2100. Diamond: 7000. The distance between Mohs 1 and Mohs 2 in absolute hardness is 31. The distance between Mohs 9 and Mohs 10 is 4,900. The ordinal scale presents these as equal steps — one digit apart, visually identical on the printed scale, occupying the same space on every chart and table. The visual uniformity is a property of the notation, not of the materials.

Thomas's PHQ-9 presents equal steps. The difference between a score of 4 and a score of 5 — the threshold from minimal to mild depression — occupies the same visual space on the scoring rubric as the difference between a score of 19 and a score of 20 — well into the severe range. The clinical difference between these transitions is not equal. The first marks an increase in symptom frequency that rarely changes treatment. The second may trigger hospitalization. But the numbers present them as equal increments, and the scoring rubric reinforces the presentation: each step from 0 to 27 occupies the same space, the same box, the same row on the form. The form is the notation. The notation encodes uniformity. The uniformity is a property of the form, not of the depression.

983. The Field

Mohs designed his scale for field use. A geologist with a set of reference minerals — or common substitutes: a fingernail for 2.5, a copper coin for 3, a steel file for 6.5, a piece of quartz for 7 — could identify an unknown mineral's hardness range without laboratory equipment, without electricity, without calibration, without training beyond the ability to observe a scratch. This is why the scale persists. Vickers hardness testing requires a diamond indenter, a calibrated loading mechanism, a microscope to measure the indentation diagonal, and a calculation. Knoop testing is similar. Both produce quantitative, interval-scale measurements. Both require a laboratory. The Mohs scale requires a rock and a fingernail and produces an ordinal ranking that is less precise, less accurate, and overwhelmingly more used.

Thomas's screening instruments were designed for clinical field use. A primary care physician with a printed questionnaire — nine items for depression, seven for anxiety — could identify a patient's probable severity range without specialized training, without extended clinical interview, without longitudinal assessment, without contextual weighting beyond what the patient reports. This is why the instruments persist. Structured clinical interviews — the SCID-5, the MINI — require trained clinicians, significant time, and interpretive judgment. They produce dimensional assessments that are more precise, more contextual, and overwhelmingly less used. The PHQ-9 requires a clipboard and two minutes and produces an ordinal score that the health system records as if it were a measurement.

984. The Diamond

Diamond is the problem. At Mohs 10, it is the ceiling of the scale. Below diamond, the absolute hardness differences between consecutive minerals are significant but bounded: the ratio between any two adjacent reference minerals ranges from approximately 1.3 to 2.0. Diamond breaks the pattern. Its absolute hardness is roughly 3.3 times that of corundum. The ordinal scale compresses this into a single step — the same single step that separates gypsum from calcite, whose absolute hardness ratio is approximately 4.2. The scale does not misrepresent diamond. It places diamond correctly at the top. But it represents the distance from corundum to diamond as the same kind of distance as every other step on the scale, and this representation is a structural consequence of the ordinal format, not a property of the mineral.

Thomas is a diamond problem. His chronic caregiver burden — decades of medical management for a medically complex child, continuous nighttime monitoring, the daily calculus of medication timing and feeding schedules and seizure protocols — sits at the ceiling of the Zarit Burden Interview's scale. Below this ceiling, the differences between scores are significant but bounded: moving from moderate to high burden reflects a genuine increase in strain. Thomas's burden breaks the pattern. The distance between his presentation and the next-highest presentation the scale can register is not the same kind of distance as the steps below. But the scale compresses it into the same kind of step, and the chart records the step, and the step enters the system as a number, and the number is the same size as every other number on the scale.

985. The Scratch

Mohs's test is destructive. To determine hardness, you must scratch the mineral. The reference mineral leaves a mark on the test surface, or it does not. The mark is permanent. The mineral is altered. You cannot test hardness without changing the thing you test. This is acceptable for rocks. It is the price of the information. Nobody asks the rock's permission.

Every clinical assessment scratches Thomas. To determine severity, the instrument asks him to inventory his distress — to count the sleepless nights, to quantify the worry, to rate the anhedonia on a scale that reduces it to a frequency. The answer leaves a mark. Once a score is entered in the chart, it becomes the reference surface against which future scores are compared. An improvement is a score that does not scratch as deep. A deterioration is a deeper scratch. The mark is permanent. Thomas is altered — not by the depression, which was already there, but by the act of scoring it, which fixes it in a format the system can read and compare. One hundred and eight Parts, and the mechanism is the scratch test: an ordinal ranking presented as an interval measurement, designed for field use, persisting because it requires nothing, compressing extremes into the same unit as midpoints, and leaving a permanent mark on the surface it claims only to observe.
