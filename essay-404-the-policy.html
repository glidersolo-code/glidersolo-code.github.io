The Policy

The average American would need 244 hours per year to read every privacy policy for every website visited — 76 working days, at a reading speed of 250 words per minute, across 1,462 sites. The average privacy policy requires a college reading level. The average American reads at eighth grade. Ninety-one percent of consumers consent without reading. Among those who do read, only 22 percent read to the end. Two percent of Americans always read privacy policies in their entirety.

The legal framework that rests on these documents is called "notice and consent." The company provides notice. The user provides consent. The framework assumes both have occurred. Two hundred and forty-four hours is the size of the assumption.

In 2016, researchers at York University and the University of Connecticut presented 543 communications students with a fictitious social network. The privacy policy included a clause requiring users to surrender their firstborn child to the company. Another clause disclosed data sharing with the NSA. Ninety-eight percent of participants agreed without raising concern. Seventy-four percent chose "quick join" and never saw the policy at all. The paper was titled "The Biggest Lie on the Internet."

The lie is not that people do not read. The lie is structural. The framework calls the document a "notice" and calls the click "consent," and neither word describes what occurs. The notice is not noticed. The consent is not informed. The words persist because the framework requires them — not as descriptions of what happens, but as legal categories that enable what follows. The notice immunizes. The consent authorizes. The privacy policy does not protect the person whose data is collected. The privacy policy protects the entity that collects it.

Every structural element serves this function. Length: Meta's privacy policy runs 19,434 words, approximately 82 minutes of reading. The length is not a failure of editing. Every practice mentioned becomes a practice disclosed, and every practice disclosed becomes a practice immunized. Longer policies cover more practices. More coverage means more protection — for the company. Reading level: the precision of legal language ("legitimate business interest," "de-identified aggregate data," "as permitted by applicable law") is not a failure of plain language. Clarity would constrain. The abstraction preserves flexibility. Consent mechanism: courts enforce clickwrap agreements — requiring an affirmative "I agree" click — while the same studies that demonstrate no one reads are cited in the same legal journals that affirm enforceability. The mechanism courts enforce and the mechanism users skip are the same mechanism.

The regulatory architecture reinforces the structure. The FTC enforces that privacy policies must not be deceptive — that what they say must be true. The FTC does not regulate what practices the policy describes. A company that discloses unlimited data sharing, accurately, satisfies the framework. The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act requires financial institutions to provide privacy notices but does not restrict the underlying data practices. COPPA requires privacy policies for sites directed at children under 13 but applies only to that age bracket. Every major American privacy regulation mandates the existence of the document without meaningfully regulating the practices the document describes.

The European experiment confirms the structure from the opposite direction. GDPR was designed to give users genuine control — informed, specific, freely given consent. The regulation produced cookie banners. Europeans collectively spend 575 million hours per year clicking them. Only 11.8 percent of consent management platforms on the top 10,000 UK websites meet minimum legal requirements. Removing the opt-out button from the first page increases consent by 22 to 23 percentage points. The regulation that was supposed to replace the notice-and-consent fiction instead replicated it at continental scale, with better typography.

The comprehension gap completes the architecture. Fifty-five percent of users who believe they understand privacy policies fail comprehension tests. Only 18.4 percent report difficulty. The gap between perceived and actual understanding is not incidental. The format produces it — clear headings, bullet points, "plain language" summaries teach the reader that reading has occurred. The design of the document performs comprehension without delivering it.

The word "privacy" in "privacy policy" does the work the dollar sign does in a price, the word "agreement" does in an arbitration clause, the word "bargain" does in a plea deal. It names the relationship the document has eliminated. The policy is not private. The policy is the instrument by which privacy is converted into data. The word "policy" is not a set of protections. The word "policy" is a set of disclosures that function as permissions. The title — "privacy policy" — performs in two words what the document performs in 6,938: the appearance of protection that is the mechanism of its removal.

The shield is the document. The transparency is the shield.
