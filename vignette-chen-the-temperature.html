The Temperature

A Chen-Maren Vignette

The standard for academic prose was established in 1971.

A committee — not the current committee, an earlier one — surveyed 25,000 paragraphs published that decade across the major journals. They measured sentence length, citation density, subordination depth, hedge frequency, and passive voice ratio. From these measurements, they derived the normal paragraph: 12 sentences, 18 citations per thousand words, subordination depth of 2.3, hedge ratio of 0.14, passive voice at 38%. The values were published. The values became the standard. The standard entered the evaluation rubric.

Chen's paragraph was measured against the 1971 values. Sentence length: 14. Citation density: 11 per thousand words. Subordination: 3.1. Hedge ratio: 0.06. Passive voice: 12%. The paragraph departed from the standard on every dimension. The rubric flagged the departures. Hendricks read the flags.

What the rubric did not report: prose style has been changing. Sentence length has increased as argument complexity has increased. Citation density has decreased as review articles and meta-analyses have concentrated references. Hedge ratios have dropped as fields have shifted toward direct claims. Passive voice has declined as journal style guides have changed. The 1971 values were probably correct for 1971. They are not correct for the current decade. The population changed. The standard did not. The standard could not, because the standard was classified as the standard — a property of good writing, not a measurement of one era's conventions.

Maren noticed. "The rubric is measuring departure from 1971," she said. "The paragraph was written in 2024."

"The standard is the standard," Hendricks said. "If the standard changes every decade, it's not a standard."

This was the argument that had kept 98.6 alive for 124 years. A standard that changes is not a standard. Therefore the standard does not change. Therefore departure from the standard is the writer's deviation, not the standard's obsolescence. The thermometer reads the paragraph. The rubric reads the thermometer. The rubric compares the reading to 1971.

Chen's paragraph sat at 0.8 — measured against a population of paragraphs written by authors who had retired, journals that had changed their style guides, and a discipline that would not recognize its own prose from fifty years ago. The 0.8 was a temperature taken against a standard that was probably correct for its era and certainly wrong for this one. But the standard was a constant. And constants, by definition, do not change. And the paragraph, which had a fever by the rubric's definition, was afebrile by the standards of its own time.
