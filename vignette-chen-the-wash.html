Vignette

The Wash

Chen was doing laundry at Maren's apartment. He turned the dial to Hot.

"What temperature is that?" Maren asked from the doorway.

"Hot."

"What temperature is 'Hot'?"

Chen paused. "Whatever the water heater delivers."

"Which is?"

He didn't know. Maren's water heater was in a closet he'd never opened. She opened it. The dial said 120°F.

"So Hot is 120," she said.

"Minus losses in the pipe. Probably 115 at the machine."

"And Cold?"

"Whatever comes from the street. In this city, in March, probably 45 to 50 degrees."

"And Warm?"

"The average. Roughly 82 degrees."

Maren considered this. "My mother, in Arizona. Her cold tap in August is 78 degrees."

"So your Warm is approximately her Cold."

They looked at each other. Two machines. Two dials. Two words — "Warm" and "Cold" — pointing to the same temperature, 1,500 miles apart, because the words described valve positions, not temperatures, and the temperatures were functions of geography and season that no dial could represent.

"The detergent adds another layer," Chen said. "Modern enzymes — protease, lipase — work best between 60 and 100 degrees. Above 140, they denature. Hot water can reduce cleaning effectiveness."

"So the hierarchy is wrong."

"The hierarchy was right for soap. It's wrong for enzymes. The dial still suggests hot cleans best because the word 'Hot' is on top and the word 'Cold' is on bottom, and vertical order performs quality ranking."

Maren leaned against the doorframe. "The paragraph has a similar structure. The lines are in an order. The order performs an argument — each line builds on the last, the accumulation feels like progression. But the lines could be rearranged and the content would be the same. The order is a dial. The content is the water."

"Rearranged, the paragraph would read differently."

"Yes. But would it argue differently?"

Chen thought about this while the machine filled. The water was 115°F, or 112°F, or 118°F — he would never know exactly, because the machine had no thermometer display, only a word, and the word was the authority, and the water was the reality, and the gap between them was the gap where the garment lived.

"There's a 'sanitize' option," he said, pointing at the dial.

"Does it sanitize?"

"NSF says 150 degrees for seven minutes. The water heater is set to 120. The machine can't reach 150 from a 120 supply. It probably uses steam injection and calls the result 'sanitize.'"

"So the word means something different from what the standard means."

"The word means 'the closest this machine can come to what the standard requires.' The consumer reads the word and infers the standard."

Maren nodded. "The dial speaks in categories. The water answers in degrees. The degrees vary by everything the categories erase."

The machine began to agitate. The water — approximately 115°F, not hot by any standard except the standard that 'Hot' means the valve is open all the way — moved through the clothes in a rhythm governed by a program, at a temperature governed by a heater, in a climate governed by a latitude, none of which appeared on the dial that Chen had turned and trusted.
