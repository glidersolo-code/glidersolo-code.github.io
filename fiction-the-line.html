Part CLXI: The Line

Piece #1246: The Wave

Hans Berger, Jena, 1929. He places electrodes on the scalp of a human subject and records electrical oscillations. He calls them the Elektrenkephalogramm. The signal is faint — microvolts, a thousand times weaker than the electrocardiogram. The scientific community ignores him for five years. Edgar Adrian at Cambridge confirms the findings in 1934 and the field begins. Berger was looking for telepathy. He found the alpha rhythm. The tool was built for a search that failed, and the failure produced a measurement that organized an entire discipline. The EEG detects the summed postsynaptic potentials of cortical pyramidal neurons. It sees the outer 3 to 4 millimeters of the brain — the cortical surface beneath the electrode. Everything deeper is invisible. The brainstem, the thalamus, the hypothalamus: structures that regulate consciousness, breathing, temperature, hormones. The EEG cannot see them. The EEG was not designed to see them. The EEG was designed to see the cortex, and it sees the cortex well.

Piece #1247: The Committee

June 1968. The Ad Hoc Committee of the Harvard Medical School to Examine the Definition of Brain Death. Chaired by Henry Beecher, an anesthesiologist. Thirteen members: ten physicians, one lawyer, one historian, one theologian. The committee published in JAMA: "A Definition of Irreversible Coma." Four criteria: unreceptivity and unresponsivity, no movements or breathing, no reflexes, flat electroencephalogram. The flat EEG was one of four. The committee stated the purpose in the first paragraph: "obsolete criteria for the definition of death can lead to controversy in obtaining organs for transplantation." The medical need preceded the medical definition. The transplant surgeons needed organs from bodies whose hearts still beat. The definition of death needed to accommodate the surgical need. The committee provided the accommodation. The flat line became a criterion for death because transplant medicine needed it to be.

Piece #1248: The Standard

The Uniform Determination of Death Act, 1981. "An individual who has sustained either (1) irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or (2) irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem, is dead." All functions. Entire brain. Including the brain stem. The legal standard is comprehensive: not just the cortex, not just consciousness, but everything — the brainstem's regulation of breathing, temperature, blood pressure, hormonal cycles. The EEG, the most common confirmatory test, measures the cortex. The legal standard requires the entire brain. The instrument assesses part of what the law demands. The gap is assumed away: cortical silence is taken to imply whole-brain death. The assumption is usually correct. The assumption is not always correct.

Piece #1249: The Child

D. Alan Shewmon, pediatric neurologist, Neurology, 1998. He documented 175 cases of "chronic brain death" — patients who met the full diagnostic criteria for brain death and then maintained somatic function for more than one week. In 56 cases, for more than one month. In one case, a child declared brain-dead continued to grow, went through puberty, and was maintained for twenty years. The brainstem mediated thermoregulation, hormonal function, wound healing. The cortex showed electrocerebral silence. The EEG was flat. The body was not dead. The legal standard said: all functions of the entire brain. The body said: some functions persisted. The instrument said: the cortex is silent. The instrument did not say: the brainstem is silent. The instrument could not say that. The instrument was never designed to say that.

Piece #1250: The Border

Japan, 1997. The Organ Transplant Law. Brain death is legally recognized — but only when the patient has previously documented consent to organ donation. Without prior consent, death is cardiac arrest: the heart stops, the person is dead. With prior consent, brain death is death: the brain stops, the person is dead. The same biological phenomenon — irreversible loss of brain function — is or is not death depending on a document. The biological event does not change. The legal meaning changes. The definition of death is not a fact about the body. The definition of death is a fact about the jurisdiction, the culture, the patient's documented preferences, and the clinical need that motivated the definition. The EEG draws the same flat line in Tokyo and in Boston. The flat line means death in one city and means nothing legal in the other, until a document is found in a filing cabinet.
