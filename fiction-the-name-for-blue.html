Part CLI: The Name for Blue

Piece #1196: The Sequence

In 1969, Berlin and Kay published the rule: all languages evolve their color terms in the same order. First dark and light. Then red. Then yellow or green, then both. Then blue. Then brown. Then the rest — purple, pink, orange, gray. The sequence was universal. The biology was fixed. Three types of cone cells in the human retina, opponent-process channels in the visual cortex. The hardware determines the categories. Languages simply discover what the eye already knows. Some languages have discovered more than others. English has eleven basic terms. The Dani of Papua New Guinea have two. The difference, Berlin and Kay said, is developmental. The Dani are at the beginning. English is at the end. The hierarchy was not stated. The hierarchy did not need to be stated.

Piece #1197: The Boundary

The Berinmo of Papua New Guinea have a word — "nol" — that covers dark green and blue. English speakers have two words for those colors. Berlin and Kay would say the Berinmo have not yet reached the stage where blue separates from green. Roberson and colleagues said something different. They showed Berinmo speakers pairs of color chips. Some pairs crossed the nol-wor boundary (the Berinmo distinction between dark green-blue and light green-yellow). Some pairs were equally far apart in wavelength but did not cross any Berinmo boundary. The Berinmo found the boundary-crossing pairs easier to tell apart. They showed the same chips to English speakers. The English speakers found the blue-green boundary crossings easier. Same chips. Same wavelengths. Same human eyes. Different boundaries. The perception followed the language. The eye did not dictate. The eye suggested. The word decided.

Piece #1198: The Blues

In Russian, there is no word for "blue" the way English means it. There are two words. Голубой — light blue. Синий — dark blue. Not shades. Colors. As different as red and orange. A Russian speaker seeing sky and sea sees two colors, not two shades of one. Jonathan Winawer tested this in 2007. Russian speakers discriminated between light and dark blue faster than English speakers. The difference was small — milliseconds — but it was real and it was reliable. Then Winawer added a twist. He asked speakers to perform the discrimination task while counting aloud. Under verbal interference, the Russian advantage disappeared. The language was doing the work. When the language was occupied, the advantage vanished. The perception was not merely labeled by the word. The perception was partly constituted by the word.

Piece #1199: The Chips

Berlin and Kay used Munsell color chips. The chips were manufactured in the United States. They were arranged in a grid: hue along the horizontal axis, lightness along the vertical. The grid sampled color space at intervals. The intervals were not arbitrary — they were perceptually uniform according to American observers. The grid was the instrument. The instrument was a theory. It said: these are the relevant dimensions (hue and lightness). These are the meaningful intervals (the American perceptual standard). When Berlin and Kay showed these chips to speakers of twenty languages and asked them to name the colors and point to the best example, the grid predetermined which colors were available to be named. A distinction that fell between two chips was invisible. A distinction that aligned with the grid was salient. The instrument found what the instrument could find.

Piece #1200: The Investigator

The deepest concealment was not in the data. It was in the investigators. Berlin and Kay spoke English. They experienced blue and green as obviously different — as different as up and down, as certain as the noon sky. The certainty felt like perception. The certainty was language. They could not see their own categorical boundary because the boundary was the lens through which they saw. When they found the same boundary in other languages, they confirmed the universal. When they did not find it, they diagnosed the absence. The Berinmo had not yet evolved the blue-green distinction. The possibility that the Berinmo had evolved a different distinction — one that Berlin and Kay had not yet evolved — did not arise. It could not arise. The evolutionary sequence ran in one direction, and it ended with English.
