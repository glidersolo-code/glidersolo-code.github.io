Part CXCVIII: The Port

Piece #1431: The Renaming

USB 3.0 was released in 2008 at 5 gigabits per second. In 2013, it was retroactively renamed USB 3.1 Gen 1. In 2017, it was renamed again to USB 3.2 Gen 1. The hardware did not change. The capability did not change. The number changed twice. A cable manufactured in 2009 was "USB 3.0" that year, "USB 3.1 Gen 1" four years later, and "USB 3.2 Gen 1" four years after that — three names for the same five gigabits.

Thomas watched the DSM perform the same operation. A patient diagnosed with "Depressive Neurosis" in 1972 had their condition renamed to "Dysthymic Disorder" in 1980 (DSM-III) and then to "Persistent Depressive Disorder" in 2013 (DSM-5). The symptoms did not change. The patient did not change. The taxonomy changed around them, like a street being renamed while the buildings remained.

In both cases, the renaming was invisible to the end user. The patient did not know their diagnosis had a different name. The consumer did not know their cable had a different version. The system updated. The thing it described did not.

Piece #1432: The Range

A device labeled "USB 3.2" could run at 5, 10, or 20 gigabits per second — a fourfold range under one version number. The label concealed the range by performing specificity: "3.2" sounded precise, like a measurement. It was a category — a tent covering three different speeds and calling them one standard.

Thomas saw the same operation in "moderate depression." A PHQ-9 score of 10 and a score of 14 were both "moderate." The word performed a single level of severity. The scores represented a 40 percent range in symptom burden. The label erased the difference between the bottom of the category and the top. The patient who scored 10 and the patient who scored 14 were given the same word. The word was the same. The experience was not.

Categories contain ranges. Labels erase them. The version number that covers a fourfold speed range and the severity word that covers a 40 percent symptom range are performing the same operation: calling a distribution a point.

Piece #1433: The Connector

A USB Type-C port can carry USB 2.0 at 480 megabits per second or USB4 at 40 gigabits per second. The same physical opening, an eighty-fold speed range. The connector does not disclose the protocol. The protocol does not disclose the speed. The consumer plugs in and discovers the bandwidth after the connection is made.

Thomas thought about clinical settings. The same "depression screening" — the same procedure, the same office, the same form — could be a brief check-in with no follow-up or a structured assessment integrated into a treatment workflow with triggered interventions. The interface was the same. The interior — the protocol running through the connection — varied enormously. The patient experienced the connector. The clinician managed the protocol. The connector did not disclose which protocol was running.

In technology and in medicine, the physical interface — the port, the screening room — is the most visible and the least informative component. What happens through the connection matters more than the connection itself. But the connection is what the consumer sees.

Piece #1434: The Reform

In 2022, the USB-IF introduced a new naming scheme: USB 5Gbps, USB 10Gbps, USB 20Gbps, USB 40Gbps. Speed in the name. The simplest format possible. The fact that this was considered a reform — that putting the speed in the speed label was newsworthy — measured the distance the version numbering had traveled from meaning.

Thomas wondered what it would look like to name the PHQ-9 score by its content. Not "moderate" or "moderately severe" but "PHQ-9: 15 of 27 symptom points across 9 domains." Not a category, not a severity word, but the number itself, disclosed as what it was — a sum.

The reform would fail, he knew, for the same reason it partially failed in USB. Consumers wanted simple labels. "USB 10Gbps" was clearer than "USB 3.2 Gen 2" but still required knowing that 10 Gbps was fast. "PHQ-9: 15/27" was clearer than "moderately severe" but still required knowing what 27 meant. The format always reduces. The question is whether the reduction discloses itself.

Piece #1435: The Cable

The cable in the drawer is marked "USB 3.0 SuperSpeed." It is now, according to the USB-IF's current naming, a USB 3.2 Gen 1 cable, or a USB 5Gbps cable. The label on the cable is wrong by two naming schemes but right about the speed. The cable will work exactly as it always has. It will transfer data at 5 gigabits per second. Nothing about the cable has changed except its name, which has changed twice without anyone touching it.

Thomas kept old assessment forms in a filing cabinet. The BDI — the original Beck Depression Inventory, not the BDI-II. The forms still measured what they measured when they were printed. A patient who filled one out today would produce a score that corresponded to the original BDI's thresholds, not the BDI-II's. The form in the drawer was right about the patient. It was wrong about the current standard.

The cable and the form share a condition: they are artifacts of a previous naming that still function correctly within their original specification. The world has renamed them. They have not renamed themselves. The label is the most fragile part of any standard. The function is the most durable. The cable connects. The form scores. The names are someone else's problem.

The format is the port.
