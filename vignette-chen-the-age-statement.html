Vignette Chen The Age Statement

Vignette

Chen holds the bottle at arm's length and reads the label. Twelve years old. Maren had given it to him for his birthday — a good Scotch, she said, from a distillery she'd visited on a conference trip to Edinburgh. Chen had thanked her and set it on the shelf where it sat for three months until tonight, when the grading was done and the apartment was quiet and the desire for something specific overcame the inertia of routine.

He pours two fingers and reads the label again. Twelve years old. The whisky is twelve years old. Except — Chen knows this one — the whisky is not twelve years old. The whisky is a blend. Some of it is twelve. Some of it might be eighteen, twenty-five, older. The number on the label is the youngest component. The law requires the floor. The label performs the whole.

Chen tastes the whisky and thinks about floors. The batting average is a floor of sorts — it declares what happened on the occasions when the batter reached base, and the occasions when the batter did not reach base but moved a runner are invisible below the number. The h-index is a floor that cannot decline — the ratchet only clicks upward. The growth chart percentile is a floor of the reference population — the "of what?" that disappears into the number. The survival rate is a floor of the timeline — the starting point moves but the percentage does not reveal the movement.

Now the age statement. The floor is explicit. The law says: youngest component. And still ninety percent of consumers do not read the number as a floor. They read it as a description. Twelve years old. The whisky is twelve years old. The format — a single integer, a simple claim — is too clean to carry its own qualification. The number is the minimum. The number performs the totality.

Chen swirls the glass. The color is deep amber — not from twelve years, mostly. From the older components. The depth that the consumer attributes to twelve years was produced by the whisky that the label does not mention. The flavor that justifies the price was contributed by the whisky that has no number on the bottle. The youngest member names the blend. The oldest member defines it.

He thinks about telling Maren. She would say: but the consumer gets a good product. The blend is crafted by someone who knows what they are doing. The age statement is a legal requirement, not a deception. And she would be right — the whisky is good, the blender is skilled, the legal framework is clear. But the format performs something the law does not intend. The law says: declare the minimum so the consumer knows at least this much. The consumer reads: the contents are this old. The law's floor becomes the label's description. The accuracy of the minimum enables the inaccuracy of the performance.

Chen finishes the glass. It was good. It was probably better than twelve years old. The label said twelve and meant at least twelve and the consumer heard exactly twelve and the whisky was likely much more than twelve. The number was true, conservative, legally mandated, and misleading. Not despite being true. Because of it.
