Vignette: The Immunity

Chen writes a word on the whiteboard. QUALIFIED.

"What does the word mean?" she asks.

"Limited," Maren says. "Conditional. Not absolute."

"That's what the word performs. The doctrine is called qualified immunity. The word says: there are boundaries. There are cases where this does not apply."

"And?"

"In 1967, the word was accurate. Pierson v. Ray. Officers who sincerely believed their conduct was lawful were protected. Good faith. Subjective. The immunity was qualified by what the officer thought." Chen draws a line through QUALIFIED. "In 1982, Harlow v. Fitzgerald. Eight to one. The Court eliminated the subjective element. Good faith became irrelevant. Malicious conduct was protected, as long as no prior case specifically deemed it unconstitutional."

"The qualification was removed."

"The qualification was removed. The word remained." Chen taps the crossed-out word. "The word 'qualified' now describes the doctrine's discarded origin. Not its function."

"What's the standard?"

"'Clearly established law.' A plaintiff must identify a prior case with nearly identical facts. Not merely that the conduct was unconstitutional. That an officer violated the same right in the same way in the same circumstances."

"Give me an example."

"Officers released a police dog on a man who surrendered by sitting with his hands raised. Immunity granted. The closest precedent involved a suspect who surrendered by lying down. Sitting was not lying."

Maren is quiet.

"Officers stole two hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars in cash and rare coins during a search. Immunity granted. No prior case held that police stealing during a search violated the Fourth Amendment. The theft was — the court's word — 'morally wrong.' But not clearly established as unconstitutional."

"How can stealing not be clearly established as wrong?"

"That's the standard. Not wrong. Not unconstitutional. Clearly established as unconstitutional in a prior case with matching facts." Chen draws a circle on the whiteboard. "And here is the structure. Pearson v. Callahan, 2009. Courts may now skip the constitutional question. Dismiss on 'clearly established' grounds without ever ruling on whether the conduct was unconstitutional."

"But then —"

"No new precedent is created. If no precedent is created, the law remains not clearly established. If the law remains not clearly established, the next officer who does the same thing gets immunity." Chen closes the circle with her marker. "The doctrine prevents the creation of the very case law it requires."

"The statute?"

"Section 1983. The Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871. Enacted during Reconstruction to combat Klan terrorism against Black citizens. The text: any state actor who violates constitutional rights 'shall be liable.' No qualifications. No immunity. A hundred years later, the Court invented a doctrine that shields the officials the statute was designed to reach."

"The one hundred and tenth species."

"Qualifier-outlives-qualification." Chen underlines the crossed-out word. "The word 'qualified' performed limitation in 1967. The limitation was removed in 1982. The word survived the removal of the thing it qualified. The name is a fossil of a doctrine that no longer exists. And the fossil does the heaviest concealment work — every time someone reads 'qualified immunity,' the word 'qualified' performs the boundaries the doctrine has abandoned."
