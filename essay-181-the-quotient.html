Essay 181: The Quotient

The bell curve is an assumption.

Not a discovery — an assumption. David Wechsler's intelligence scale, published in 1939, produces a normal distribution with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15. The distribution is normal because the scoring algorithm forces it to be normal. The raw scores — whatever their actual distribution — are converted through a statistical transformation that produces a bell curve. If the raw data are skewed, the conversion corrects the skew. If the data are bimodal, the conversion smooths the modes. The result is always normal because the procedure guarantees normality.

This is distribution-as-assumption: the statistical form is treated as a property of the trait being measured, when it is a property of the test that measures it. The intelligence quotient appears to discover that intelligence is normally distributed. It does not discover this. It produces this. The finding and the assumption are the same thing, and the sameness is invisible in the number that appears in the chart.

The consequences are precise. A normal distribution has fixed proportions: 68% within one standard deviation, 95% within two. Applied to IQ: 2.3% of the population falls below 70 ("intellectual disability"), 2.3% above 130 ("gifted"). These percentages are cited as facts about the population. They are products of the assumed distribution. If cognitive ability is not normally distributed — if it is skewed toward the lower end, or has a long right tail, or is multimodal — the percentages are wrong. But the scoring procedure guarantees the distribution, so the percentages are always correct by construction, and the construction is the assumption, and the assumption is the finding.

The deeper history compounds the concealment. Binet designed the original test in 1905 as a triage tool — a way to identify children who needed educational support in Parisian schools. He explicitly warned against treating the score as a measurement of a fixed trait. The warning was published. Terman brought the test to Stanford, converted it to a ranking system, and introduced the intelligence quotient formula. Wechsler added the deviation scoring that forced normality. Each step carried the test further from its design context: triage → ranking → psychometric measurement → clinical and educational decision-making. Context-migration layered on distribution-as-assumption.

The Flynn Effect adds temporal instability. James Flynn discovered in 1984 that raw IQ scores had been rising approximately 3 points per decade across industrialized nations since testing began. The tests are renormed every 15 to 20 years, resetting the mean to 100. The resetting absorbs the drift: a score of 100 in 1940 and a score of 100 in 2000 represent different levels of raw performance. The number is the same. What it measures is not. The renorming is the leap second of intelligence testing — an irregular correction maintaining a fiction of stability by adjusting the scale to absorb change.

The number appears in the chart. The chart uses the number. The decisions follow the number. At each point — the chart, the decision, the consequence — the number is treated as a property of the person. The property is a product of the procedure: Binet's task selection, Terman's formula, Wechsler's normalization, Flynn's drift, the norming sample's composition, the test administration's conditions. The precision of the number conceals the decisions that produced it.

Binet's warning is in the 1905 publication. The score does not measure a fixed quality. The warning is true. The quotient is calculated. The chart is updated. The decision is made. The warning remains in the publication — readable, true, and filed alongside Kuznets's warning about GDP: a founding document containing a caution that the system it founded was designed to disregard.
