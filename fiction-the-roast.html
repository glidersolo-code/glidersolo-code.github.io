Part CCXLII: The Roast

Piece #1651: The Name

French Roast is not from France. Italian Roast is not from Italy. Vienna Roast is not from Vienna. City Roast is not from a city. The names entered English-language coffee commerce in the nineteenth century as descriptions of regional preferences — French consumers preferred darker roasts, Italians darker still — and survived as labels after the geography became invisible. French Roast designates a roast level: approximately 28-35 on the Agtron near-infrared reflectance scale, where the instrument measures the color of ground coffee and a lower number means darker. Italian Roast is lower. Vienna Roast is between them. The names attach national identity to degrees Fahrenheit. The consumer reads the bag and infers provenance. The bag describes combustion.

Piece #1652: The Erasure

A coffee bean carries its origin in its chemistry: the volatile aromatics from altitude and soil, the fruit acids from cherry ripeness, the floral notes from varietal genetics, the processing signature from washed or natural or honey method. These are the dimensions that distinguish Ethiopian Yirgacheffe from Guatemalan Huehuetenango, that make Kenyan AA taste different from Sumatran Mandheling. As roast temperature rises past first crack — approximately 385 degrees Fahrenheit, the point where moisture pressure fractures the bean's cellulose structure — these origin characteristics begin to carbonize. Past second crack — approximately 435 degrees — the bean's surface oils migrate outward and the dominant flavors are carbon, caramelized sugar, and the bitter compounds of pyrolysis. Dark roast tastes like dark roast. The origin is still printed on the bag. The origin is no longer in the cup. The roast performed the erasure. The label preserved the name.

Piece #1653: The Consistency

Starbucks roasts dark. This is a business decision, not a flavor decision. A global supply chain draws beans from dozens of origins, across harvests, across years. Light-roasted, these beans would taste different from each other — the customer in Seattle would taste a different cup than the customer in Shanghai. Dark roasting eliminates the variation. The consistency is the product. The consistency is achieved by destruction: burning away the characteristics that would make each origin distinguishable. The consumer reads "bold" on the bag. Bold means the roaster achieved uniformity by carbonizing the evidence of diversity. The word that performs strength describes the absence of difference.

Piece #1654: The Caffeine

The consumer believes dark roast is stronger, and stronger means more caffeine. The relationship is directionally wrong. Caffeine is thermally stable through normal roasting temperatures — it degrades minimally. But dark-roasted beans expand in the drum, losing moisture and density. A scoop of dark roast contains fewer beans than a scoop of light roast. Fewer beans means slightly less caffeine — five to ten percent less per volume measure. By weight, the difference is negligible. "Strength" conflates two dimensions: caffeine content, which is roughly constant, and flavor intensity, which increases with darkness because bitterness from carbonization is perceived as potency. The consumer who orders dark roast for the caffeine is drinking slightly less of it. The consumer who orders light roast for mildness is drinking slightly more. The word "strong" collapsed both dimensions into one axis and pointed it in the wrong direction.

Piece #1655: The Grade

Two hundred and forty-two Parts. The SCA cupping protocol grades specialty coffee on attributes — acidity, sweetness, body, flavor, aftertaste, balance, uniformity, clean cup, defects. These are the dimensions the grading system was designed to reward. Dark roasting diminishes or eliminates every one. Acidity — carbonized. Sweetness — caramelized past nuance. Floral and fruit notes — pyrolyzed. The grading system rewards what the roast level erases. The Agtron scale — the industry's objective colorimetric measurement of roast level — appears nowhere on consumer packaging. No US regulatory definition of "light," "medium," or "dark" exists. The third wave — Counter Culture, Intelligentsia, Stumptown — inverted the hierarchy, marketing light roast and origin to preserve what the mass market burned away. But the mass market is ninety percent of consumption, and the mass market positions dark as premium. Thomas ordered a French Roast. It was not from France. It was strong, meaning bitter, meaning the origin had been removed at four hundred and fifty degrees, meaning the bag showed a country the cup could no longer taste. He drank it. It tasted like every other dark roast he had ever had. That was the point.
