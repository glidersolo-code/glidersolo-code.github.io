The Kit

A box arrives. Inside: a tube, a barcode, a set of instructions so simple they could fit on a napkin. Spit. Seal. Return. Six weeks later, a report: percentages, colored bars, a chromosome painting, names of nations. Forty-three percent British & Irish. Twenty-two percent French & German. The numbers feel like facts about the body — stable, inherent, biographical.

They are not.

The report is a statistical comparison. An algorithm breaks the genome into short windows of consecutive genetic markers and classifies each window against a reference panel — a collection of modern-day individuals who self-reported four grandparents born in the same country. A support vector machine assigns the most likely label. A Hidden Markov Model smooths the result. The output is a probability distribution, collapsed into a single category per window, aggregated into a percentage per population. The percentage is a vote count: how many genome segments looked most similar to the British reference, the French reference, the Scandinavian reference.

The reference panel is not the past. It is the present, standing in for the past. "British & Irish" means not seventh-century inhabitants of the isles but living people whose grandparents were born there — people whose genomes are themselves admixtures of Celtic, Roman, Anglo-Saxon, Norse, and Norman contributions. The algorithm uses one mixture to classify another and calls the result ancestry.

When the algorithm updates, ancestry changes. Version 7 of 23andMe's Ancestry Composition added thirty-three new European populations and eliminated the "Broadly" categories. Overnight, a customer's report shifted from "Broadly Southern European" to specific Italian, Greek, and Iberian fractions. The DNA was unchanged. The reference was rewritten. The identity was revised.

Identical twins — same zygote, same genome — receive different results from the same company. Twenty-eight percent Eastern European for one; twenty-four point seven for the other. The genotyping chip reads 690,000 sites, misreads roughly one percent of them, and which sites fail differs with each chip. The algorithm smooths noise into confident labels, but the confidence is the algorithm's estimate, not the genome's property.

The format does the heaviest work. Percentages summing to one hundred imply that ancestry is a substance with a fixed total, that identity can be partitioned like property, that the fractions exclude each other. The pie chart enforces completeness — no room for "unknown," no tolerance for "it depends." Nation-state labels naturalize the categories. "Nigerian" names a political entity created in 1960; the category is projected backward across millennia of West African population history. "French & German" bridges a border that has moved a dozen times since 1648.

What the report measures — statistical similarity to a modern convenience sample — and what the consumer reads — descent from a named ancestral people — are different categories of thing. Similarity is a mathematical relationship, computed at a moment, between two datasets. Descent is a causal chain, operating through generations, between reproducing bodies. The format collapses the first into the second without marking the collapse.

The collapse has consequences. Roth and Ivemark found in 2018 that genetic ancestry test results changed consumers' racial and ethnic identities. The test did not report a pre-existing identity. It generated one. Panofsky and Donovan studied white nationalists posting results online; those who received non-European percentages deployed repair strategies — discrediting the test, reinterpreting results, invoking physical appearance. The test had created an identity claim requiring negotiation, not a biological fact requiring acceptance. The measurement became a social event.

The kit is the simplest thing in the system: a tube, a barcode, a prepaid envelope. Ease of input disguises the fragility of the output. Spit goes in. Identity comes out. The tube did not contain the identity. The algorithm built it. The format delivered it. The customer wore it.
