Part CLXXXIX: The Diagonal

Piece #1386: The Tube

The cathode-ray tube was a glass funnel. At the narrow end, an electron gun. At the wide end, a phosphor-coated face. The electrons swept across the face in horizontal lines, exciting the phosphors, painting an image that existed only in the persistence of the phosphor's glow and the persistence of the viewer's retina. The glass funnel was roughly circular because it was blown glass, and blown glass tends toward circles. The image was rectangular because broadcast signals were rectangular. The diagonal of the rectangle inscribed within the circle corresponded to the circle's diameter — the manufacturing dimension, the one the glassmaker needed to specify.

So the television was sold by its diagonal. Not because the diagonal was the most informative measurement. Not because consumers experienced the diagonal as a dimension. Because the glassmaker built a circle and the marketer needed a number, and the diagonal bridged the two. The manufacturing dimension became the consumer-facing specification. The reason disappeared. The number remained.

Thomas's clinical assessments began the same way. The PHQ-9 was designed for primary-care screening — a quick triage tool in a time-constrained office visit. The manufacturing dimension was brevity. The consumer-facing specification was a severity score. The original purpose shaped the instrument. The instrument outlived the purpose.

Piece #1387: The Bezel

A nineteen-inch monitor, circa 1997. Nineteen inches of cathode-ray tube, measured corner to corner across the glass. But the bezel — the plastic frame — covered the curved edges where the image distorted and the phosphors failed. The viewable image: perhaps 17.3 inches. The consumer saw nineteen on the box and 17.3 on the screen. The difference was real and invisible until you opened the box.

The Federal Trade Commission required disclosure. Manufacturers had to print the viewable image size alongside the advertised diagonal. The box now said both: 19" (17.3" VIS). The correction was a parenthetical. The large number was still nineteen. The smaller truth was still parenthetical.

Thomas's medical records carry the same architecture. The diagnosis — Major Depressive Disorder, Moderate — is the nineteen inches. The actual experience — difficulty getting out of bed, food tasting like nothing, three AM wakefulness, the effort of smiling at his daughter — is the 17.3. The parenthetical is always smaller. The bezel covers the edges the specification doesn't reach.

Piece #1388: The Ratio

A thirty-two-inch television in 2003, aspect ratio four-by-three. Width: 25.6 inches. Height: 19.2 inches. Area: 491.5 square inches. A thirty-two-inch television in 2008, aspect ratio sixteen-by-nine. Width: 27.9 inches. Height: 15.7 inches. Area: 437.9 square inches. The diagonal stayed the same. The area shrank by eleven percent. The width grew by 2.3 inches. The height contracted by 3.5 inches.

The consumer compared diagonals — thirty-two and thirty-two — and concluded the screens were the same size. They were not. The rectangle had stretched sideways and compressed vertically. The diagonal, measuring from corner to corner, could not register the change because it measured a line, not a surface. The shift happened inside the number. The number stayed perfectly still.

Thomas's severity categories perform the same stability. Moderate depression at twenty-five and moderate depression at forty-five: the label is the same. The shape of the experience — its width, its height, which dimension grew and which contracted — is different. The diagonal of the label covers the same distance. The area it contains has changed.

Piece #1389: The Comparison

A man stands in the television aisle at a big-box store. The screens are arranged by size: 43", 50", 55", 65", 75", 85". The numbers ascend in a clean progression, suggesting a proportional increase in viewing experience. But the relationship between diagonal and area is quadratic, not linear. A 75-inch television has roughly twice the area of a 55-inch television, not 36 percent more as the diagonal ratio would suggest. The linear number underrepresents the larger screen and overrepresents the smaller one. The consumer who steps from 55 to 65 expects an eighteen percent improvement — the ratio of the diagonals. The actual area increase is forty percent.

He picks the 65. It will go above the fireplace. His wife asked for "not too big." He will report to her: sixty-five inches, only ten more than the old one. The ten inches of diagonal translate to forty percent more wall coverage. The number he cites performs modesty. The screen he hangs performs something else.

Thomas tells his therapist he's doing "a little better." The linear scale of language — a little, somewhat, moderately, significantly — does not map proportionally to the quadratic reality of functional change. A little better might mean he showered today. The diagonal of the phrase is small. The area it covers is not.

Piece #1390: The Format

No major television manufacturer advertises by area. The calculation is trivial: width times height. A 55-inch 16:9 television is 47.9 by 27.0 inches — 1,293 square inches. The number exists. It is not printed. A consumer comparing a 55-inch to a 65-inch could be told: 1,293 versus 1,805 square inches, a 40 percent increase. Instead the consumer is told: 55 versus 65, an 18 percent increase. The format — one number, in inches, the diagonal — is not chosen because it is more informative. It is chosen because it is more printable. A spec sheet has columns. A price tag has a line. A headline has a character count. One number fits. Two do not.

The format selects the measurement. The measurement that survives is the one the format can accommodate. A two-dimensional property communicated through a one-dimensional proxy — not because the proxy captures the property, but because the proxy fits the column. And the column, once filled, becomes the thing compared.

One hundred and eighty-nine Parts. Thomas's clinical numbers are diagonals. They describe a line across the rectangle of his experience — corner to corner, the longest possible distance through it — and present that line as the size. The width and height of what he lives are not the number. They are not the column. They are not the comparison. They are the area the diagonal implies but does not measure, the surface behind the line, the screen behind the spec.
