The Factor

Thomas, Part CII

951. The Digestion

The Kjeldahl method measures nitrogen in food. Johan Kjeldahl developed it in 1883 at the Carlsberg Laboratory in Copenhagen to measure nitrogen in grain — to determine, indirectly, the protein content of barley for brewing. The method works by acid digestion: the sample is heated with concentrated sulfuric acid and a catalyst, converting all organic nitrogen to ammonium sulfate. The ammonium is then distilled and titrated. The result is a number: total nitrogen, in grams per unit mass. The method is destructive — the original sample is dissolved. The fat, carbohydrate, fiber, moisture, and structural architecture of the food are destroyed. What survives the digestion is nitrogen. Nitrogen is what is measured.

Thomas's intake assessment is a digestion. The structured interview — the PHQ-9, the demographic form, the risk screening — converts all of Thomas's experience into standardized data. The depression is dissolved into nine frequency ratings. The caregiving history is dissolved into a checkbox. The thirty years of marriage, the progression of his wife's illness, the erosion of his social network, the debt — all heated with the concentrated acid of a forty-five-minute intake, catalyzed by a clinician's attention, converted to a set of numbers. The original form is destroyed. What survives the digestion is what the instrument can measure. The result is a number.

952. The Factor

The Kjeldahl method measures nitrogen. Food labeling requires protein. The conversion uses a factor: total nitrogen multiplied by 6.25 yields estimated protein. The factor 6.25 assumes that all food protein contains exactly 16% nitrogen by mass — 100 divided by 16 equals 6.25. This assumption was formalized by D.B. Jones at the United States Department of Agriculture in 1931 — Circular 183, "Factors for Converting Percentages of Nitrogen in Foods and Feeds into Percentages of Proteins." Jones proposed specific factors for specific food categories: 6.38 for dairy, 5.70 for wheat, 5.95 for rice, 6.25 for everything else. The factors are still in use. Ninety-five years of food labeling, regulatory compliance, nutritional guidance, and dietary research rest on conversion factors published by a single USDA scientist in 1931.

Thomas's assessment score is converted to a treatment recommendation using a similar factor. A PHQ-9 score of 10 or above indicates moderate depression. The threshold 10 was derived from normative validation studies — the score at which sensitivity and specificity for clinical diagnosis were jointly optimized in the study population. The population was not Thomas. The factor — the threshold that converts a score into a category, a category into a treatment protocol — was derived from data that assumed uniform composition across all patients, the way Jones assumed uniform nitrogen content across all proteins. The factor is 6.25. The factor is 10. Both are single numbers that convert a measurement into a report, and both assume that the thing being measured has the same internal composition across all instances.

953. The Nitrogen

Not all nitrogen in food is protein nitrogen. Non-protein nitrogen — NPN — includes urea, nucleic acids, free amino acids, nitrate, creatinine, and dozens of other nitrogen-containing compounds. In fresh milk, NPN accounts for approximately 5% of total nitrogen. In aged cheese, it can exceed 25%. The Kjeldahl method measures all nitrogen equally — protein and non-protein, essential and inert. The conversion factor treats the total as protein. Mariotti, Tomé, and Mirand at the French National Institute for Agricultural Research, publishing in Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition in 2008, reviewed the Jones factors and found that the actual nitrogen-to-protein ratio varies substantially: from 5.14 for sunflower seeds to 6.38 for casein. The 6.25 default is neither the mean nor the median. It is a convention.

Thomas's assessment score contains non-diagnostic nitrogen. Grief is not depression. Financial stress is not anxiety. Moral injury is not guilt. But the PHQ-9 measures all of them equally: "How often have you felt down, depressed, or hopeless?" captures clinical depression and non-clinical grief in the same item. The conversion factor treats the total as pathology. The clinician reads the score and sees moderate depression. Some of the nitrogen is grief. Some is exhaustion. Some is the rational response of a person whose wife is dying. The Kjeldahl method cannot distinguish protein nitrogen from non-protein nitrogen. The PHQ-9 cannot distinguish pathology from appropriate suffering. Both measure total content. Both convert total content to a specific label. Both labels are narrower than what was measured.

954. The Adulteration

In 2008, melamine was found in infant formula manufactured in China. Melamine — C₃H₆N₆ — is 66.7% nitrogen by mass. It is not a protein. It has no nutritional value. But it registers as nitrogen in the Kjeldahl test, and nitrogen multiplied by 6.25 becomes protein on the label. Adding small amounts of melamine to diluted milk dramatically inflated the apparent protein content without adding any actual protein. The gap between what was measured (nitrogen) and what was reported (protein) was wide enough to exploit. Three hundred thousand children were affected. The adulteration was possible because the standard method measures a proxy and reports a target, and the distance between proxy and target can be filled with anything that satisfies the proxy.

Thomas does not adulterate his responses. But he has learned which answers the instrument rewards. Not consciously — not as strategy — but as adaptation. He has learned that reporting sleep disruption produces a clinical response (medication adjustment) while reporting financial distress does not (outside scope of practice). He has learned that the instrument's proxy — frequency of specific symptoms — rewards certain presentations and is indifferent to others. His responses are not false. They are selected. He has learned to present the nitrogen the instrument measures, because the instrument converts nitrogen to protein and the protein gets treated. The non-protein nitrogen — the components of his distress that the instrument cannot convert to a diagnosis — he stops reporting, not because he is hiding it but because reporting it produces no response. The gap between what the instrument measures and what the label reports is wide enough for Thomas's genuine suffering to fall through.

955. The Label

The food label reports protein in grams. It does not report nitrogen content, conversion factor, analytical method, or the 1931 provenance of the factor used. The consumer reads "12g protein" and understands: this product contains twelve grams of protein. The number has been digested (the Kjeldahl method destroyed the sample), converted (nitrogen multiplied by a factor that assumes uniform composition), and compressed (from a measurement with uncertainty to a printed number without). The label is the endpoint of a pipeline that began with a specific analytical method, applied a specific assumption, and reported the result in units that suggest a different kind of knowledge than the measurement actually provides.

Thomas's clinical record reports "moderate depression." It does not report which items contributed to the score, what proportion of the score reflects non-diagnostic distress, which conversion threshold was used, or how the normative validation population differed from Thomas. The clinician reading the record — the next clinician, in a different system, seeing Thomas for the first time — reads "moderate depression" and understands: this patient has moderate depression. The label is the endpoint of a pipeline that began with a structured assessment, applied a conversion factor, and reported the result in a category that suggests a different kind of knowledge than the measurement actually provides. One hundred and two Parts, and the mechanism is the label — the final compression that drops the method, the factor, the non-protein nitrogen, the adaptation, the gap, and presents a number as if it were the thing the number was meant to measure.
