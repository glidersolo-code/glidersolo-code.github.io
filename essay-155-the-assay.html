The Assay

Essay 155

Salivary cortisol is the most widely used non-invasive biomarker for stress. A patient drools into a tube or chews on a cotton swab. The saliva is assayed — overwhelmingly by enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA) — and the result reports the concentration of free cortisol, the biologically active fraction of the stress hormone. The method is simple, painless, and repeatable. It appears in thousands of published studies on stress, depression, PTSD, Cushing's syndrome, circadian disruption, and infant attachment. Clinical thresholds have been established. Reference intervals have been published. The method works.

Anna Lee, Ryunsup Ahn, Jeong-Ho Seok, Kyoung-Ryul Lee, and colleagues at Seoul Clinical Laboratories, Yonsei University College of Medicine, and Minds.AI compared immunoassay with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) for salivary cortisol in Annals of Laboratory Medicine (2024). They found that immunoassay reads 2 to 2.5 times higher than mass spectrometry for the same saliva sample. The Passing-Bablok regression: IA = 0.315 + 2.129 × MS. The correlation is 0.966 — nearly perfect. Both methods capture the same circadian pattern: cortisol peaks after waking and falls to nadir at night. The rhythm is preserved. The magnitude is doubled.

The mechanism is cross-reactivity. The ELISA antibody is designed to bind cortisol, but it also binds structurally similar molecules: cortisone at 2.55%, prednisolone at 16.64%, 11-deoxycortisol at 8.53%. These cross-reactivities are quantified in the antibody's specification sheet as fixed properties of the reagent. The numbers are small. In blood, where cortisol is approximately four times more abundant than cortisone, 2.55% cross-reactivity with cortisone is negligible — a few percent of a minor fraction.

Saliva is not blood. In the salivary glands, the enzyme 11β-HSD2 rapidly converts cortisol to cortisone. By the time saliva reaches the collection tube, cortisone is 5 to 10 times more abundant than cortisol. The ratio between target and interferent has inverted. The same 2.55% cross-reactivity, applied to a molecule present at 10 times the target's concentration, produces approximately 25% inflation from cortisone alone. Add the other cross-reactive metabolites and the total inflation reaches 2 to 2.5 fold. The antibody reports cortisol that is substantially cortisone.

The concealment here is a matrix-inversion error: a cross-reactivity that is trivial in one biological fluid becomes dominant in another because the chemical composition of the fluid reverses the ratio of target to interferent. The cross-reactivity numbers on the specification sheet do not change. The numbers are correct. What changes is what those numbers mean when applied to a different matrix. A 2.55% cross-reactivity means nothing when the interferent is four times less abundant than the target. It means everything when the interferent is ten times more abundant. The specification sheet presents cross-reactivity as a fixed property of the antibody. It is not. It is a property of the antibody-in-a-matrix. Change the matrix, and the same antibody measures something different.

The extension error is structural. The immunoassay was developed and validated for serum cortisol measurement. In serum, it performs well — the cross-reactivities are genuinely negligible because the matrix favors the target. When the same assay was extended to saliva — driven by the clinical convenience of non-invasive sampling — the antibody's specificity profile was carried over as though it were independent of the sample type. Nobody re-characterized what the known cross-reactivities mean in a matrix where the interferent-to-target ratio is reversed. The serum validation was treated as general. It was specific to the serum matrix.

This connects to #233 (integrating sphere quantum yield), where a method validated for transparent samples was extended to scattering samples without re-validating the blank assumption. And to #234 (global soil pH), where different protocols produce systematically different values that get merged under one label. Here, IA and LC-MS/MS produce the same pattern at different magnitudes, and published research combines both under "salivary cortisol" without adjusting for the systematic offset.

The practical consequences compound. Meta-analyses of salivary cortisol that pool IA and MS studies treat the values as interchangeable. They are not. Reference intervals derived from IA cannot be applied to MS results. Clinical thresholds for screening — late-night salivary cortisol to rule out Cushing's syndrome — depend on which assay generated the cutoff. Apply an IA-derived threshold to an MS measurement, and the diagnostic sensitivity changes. The numbers are precise. The numbers are internally consistent within each method. The numbers are incommensurable across methods. The antibody was designed to be specific. It is specific — for the matrix it was validated in. In saliva, it measures cortisol and a ghost of cortisone it cannot separate from its target.
