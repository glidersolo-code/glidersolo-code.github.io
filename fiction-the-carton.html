Part CXCVII: The Carton

Piece #1426: The Middle

The USDA defines six egg sizes: Jumbo, Extra Large, Large, Medium, Small, Peewee. Large sits at the midpoint — three sizes above the bottom, two below the top. The word "Large" performs above-average. The position is average.

Thomas found the same phenomenon in severity labels. The PHQ-9's five severity categories — none (0-4), mild (5-9), moderate (10-14), moderately severe (15-19), severe (20-27) — placed "moderate" in the middle. A patient told their depression was "moderate" heard the word as the midpoint of seriousness — bad enough to matter, not so bad as to alarm. The clinical committee that set the cutoff at 10 chose the boundary based on sensitivity and specificity curves, not on the severity the word implied. The word performed a meaning the threshold did not intend.

"Large" became the default egg size because recipes standardize on it. "Moderate" became the most common clinical classification because the cutoff was set where screening instruments detect most cases. Both words sit at the midpoint of their scales. Both perform something other than the midpoint.

Piece #1427: The Collective

A carton of Large eggs must weigh at least 24 ounces per dozen. The standard applies to the carton, not the egg. An individual egg can vary by half an ounce and the carton still qualifies. The label describes the aggregate. The consumer reads it as describing each unit.

Thomas's outcome data worked the same way. "Seventy percent of patients respond to treatment" described a population statistic. The individual patient heard it as a probability applying to them — a 70 percent chance of getting better. But the 70 percent was a proportion of a group, not a probability for a person. The patient was either in the 70 percent or in the 30. The collective outcome was not an individual prediction.

The carton standard and the response rate share a structure: a group measurement presented in a format that invites individual interpretation. The label says "Large." The patient hears "likely." Both are true of the collection and uncertain about the unit.

Piece #1428: The Candling

Grade is determined by candling — holding eggs against a light source. The inspector observes air cell depth, albumen clarity, yolk centering, blood spots. The air cell grows as moisture evaporates through the porous shell. Its depth is a clock of freshness: one-eighth of an inch for Grade AA, three-sixteenths for A, three-eighths for B. The consumer never sees the candling. The consumer sees the letter.

Thomas never saw his patients' brain scans. He saw their scores. The PHQ-9 was a candling — a structured light shone through the shell of reported symptoms to reveal a shadow of the interior. The clinician observed the shadow. The patient saw the number. The number compressed the observation into an integer the way the grade compressed the candling into a letter.

In both cases, a procedure of observation was replaced by its output. The procedure required expertise — trained candlers, trained clinicians. The output required only literacy. The letter and the number traveled further than the procedure that produced them, arriving at consumers and patients who could read the result without understanding the inspection.

Piece #1429: The Color

Brown eggs hold 62.8 percent of the American market. Shell color reflects hen breed — White Leghorns lay white, Rhode Island Reds lay brown. Color has no correlation with grade, size, flavor, or nutritional content. The premium is perception. Consumers associate brown with natural, artisanal, farm-fresh. The color performs quality. The grading system measures it. The consumer reads the color.

Thomas saw color effects in clinical settings. A patient receiving a brand-name medication in familiar packaging reported better outcomes than one receiving the generic equivalent in plain packaging. The chemical compound was identical. The color — the branding, the packaging, the aesthetic signal — was not. The color performed efficacy. The pharmacology measured it. The patient read the color.

The brown egg and the branded pill share a mechanism: an aesthetic property with no chemical or structural relevance that nonetheless changes the consumer's experience. The format accommodates the irrelevant signal because the signal is what the consumer processes first. The grading system, the clinical trial — these come after. The color comes first.

Piece #1430: The Recipe

A recipe says "two eggs." It means two Large eggs. The size designation has been absorbed into the word "egg" — the modifier has become the thing. If a consumer uses Medium eggs, the recipe will be slightly different. If Jumbo, different again. The recipe does not disclose this because the default is so dominant that specifying it would feel redundant.

Thomas's screening instruments had similar absorbed defaults. "Screen the patient" meant "administer the PHQ-9." The instrument had been absorbed into the verb. Other instruments existed — the Hamilton, the Beck, the MADRS — but "screen" had become synonymous with one of them. The default was so dominant that naming it felt redundant.

The absorbed default is the most complete concealment. The consumer does not know they are choosing Large because the choice has already been made, long ago, by the infrastructure of recipes and shelf space and habit. The clinician does not know they are choosing the PHQ-9 because the choice has already been made, long ago, by the infrastructure of EMRs and billing codes and training. The default is not a selection. It is an inheritance.

The format is the carton.
