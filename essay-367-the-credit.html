Essay 367: The Credit

Andrew Carnegie wanted to give college professors pensions. To give pensions, the Carnegie Foundation needed to determine which institutions were colleges. To determine which institutions were colleges, the foundation needed a unit of measurement. The unit it chose was time.

One credit hour: one hour of classroom instruction per week, for one semester. A bachelor's degree: one hundred and twenty credit hours. Approximately eighteen hundred hours of sitting in rooms where someone talked. The unit was adopted in 1906. It was designed to answer a bureaucratic question — is this a college? — and it answered it with a clock.

The unit escaped. It became the basis of accreditation, financial aid, transfer articulation, degree requirements, tuition pricing, and faculty workload. Every pillar of the American higher education system rests on a time-based unit created for pension eligibility. The original purpose is forgotten. The unit persists.

The concealment is in the word. "Credit." To credit means to attribute, to acknowledge, to give due. A student earns credits — the word performs the acquisition of something creditable, something acknowledged as an achievement. But the system records the acquisition of time. Three credits: forty-five hours of classroom exposure. The student accumulates credits like a shopper accumulates points, and at one hundred and twenty the system produces a credential — a degree — that certifies the sum of time as an achievement of learning.

Transfer sharpens the gap. A three-credit biology course at a community college transfers to a four-year university. The receiving institution accepts the credits because the hours are equivalent — approximately forty-five hours of instruction. The equivalence is temporal, not substantive. Nobody compares the syllabi, the exams, the learning outcomes. The number transfers because the number is a time, and time is fungible. Learning is not.

The format survives because it fits. A credit hour is countable. It can be divided (half-credit modules), multiplied (nine-credit research blocks), summed (one hundred and twenty for a degree), and priced (five hundred dollars per credit). It sits comfortably on a transcript, on a financial aid form, on an accreditation report. Any alternative — portfolio assessment, competency demonstration, narrative evaluation — resists counting, resists pricing, resists the infrastructure built around the credit hour since 1906. The unit is the infrastructure. The infrastructure is the unit.

Online education reveals the gap that the classroom concealed. A three-credit online course might involve forty-five hours of video lectures, or thirty hours of asynchronous discussion, or one hundred hours of project work. The "hour" in credit hour maps to no particular hour. The Department of Education's 2010 definition — "not less than one hour of classroom or direct faculty instruction and a minimum of two hours of out-of-class student work each week for approximately fifteen weeks" — strains against formats that have no classroom and no week. The definition persists because the unit persists because the infrastructure persists. The infrastructure was built around a clock. The clock was built for a pension. The pension was built by a man who made steel.

The input performing as the output. Time performing as learning. Exposure performing as achievement. The unit measures how long the student sat. The credential certifies what the student knows. The word "credit" bridges the gap — performing attribution while recording duration. A hundred and twenty credits. Eighteen hundred hours. One degree. The hours are real. The learning is assumed. The assumption is the credit.
