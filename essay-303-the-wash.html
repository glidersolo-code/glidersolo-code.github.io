The Wash

Three words on a dial. Hot, Warm, Cold. Three temperatures that are not temperatures.

Hot means: open the hot supply valve. The water that arrives depends on the water heater — 120°F if the homeowner followed the DOE's recommendation for energy savings and scald prevention, 140°F if the heater was never adjusted from factory default, 105°F if it is recovering from a shower. The machine cannot deliver what the heater does not have. The word performs a temperature the plumbing may not support.

Cold means: open the cold supply valve. The water that arrives depends on geography and season. In Minneapolis in January, cold tap water is approximately 37°F. In Phoenix in August: 78°F. Same machine, same detergent, same garment, same dial setting. Forty-one degrees of difference, determined by the pipe, the soil, the latitude, and the month. The word performed consistency. The pipe delivered climate.

Warm means: blend them. If hot is 120°F and cold is 40°F, warm is approximately 80°F. If cold rises to 78°F in summer, warm rises to 99°F. The fabric care label on the garment — the tag the consumer typically cuts off — defines "warm" as 105°F plus or minus 15°F, a thirty-degree band that acknowledges the variability the machine's dial does not. The care label was honest about the range. The dial was categorical.

The cleaning hierarchy inverts with chemistry. The assumption — hot cleans best — was accurate for soap, which dissolves grease more effectively at higher temperatures. Modern laundry detergent is enzyme-based: protease for protein stains, lipase for fats, amylase for starches. These enzymes are proteins with optimal ranges, typically 60 to 100°F. Above 140°F, the enzymes denature. Hot water can reduce the cleaning power of the product the consumer bought. The hierarchy that the dial's order suggests — hot above warm above cold — was real in the soap era and is directionally misleading in the enzyme era. The dial did not update.

The "sanitize" option adds a final layer. NSF Protocol P172 defines sanitization as 150°F sustained for at least seven minutes. Most residential water heaters are set below this. Many sanitize cycles use steam injection or extended agitation rather than achieving the sustained thermal threshold. The consumer reads "sanitize" and infers sterility. The cycle delivers bacterial reduction — often a three-log reduction, 99.9 percent — but reduction and elimination are not the same word and not the same outcome.

Three words that perform three categories behind which three variables multiply: water heater setpoint, geographic inlet temperature, and seasonal variation. The consumer's Warm in Minneapolis in January and the consumer's Cold in Phoenix in August may be within two degrees of each other. The words are different. The water is the same. The dial, having spoken, does not elaborate.
