The Keyboard

The typebars on the Sholes & Glidden Type-Writer hung beneath the platen on metal levers. When adjacent bars were struck in rapid succession, they collided and jammed. Christopher Latham Sholes rearranged the keys between 1868 and 1873 to separate frequently co-occurring letter pairs, reducing the jamming rate. The arrangement was a mechanical solution to a mechanical problem. The problem was not about hands. The problem was about bars.

Remington bought the rights in 1873 and shipped the Remington No. 2 in 1878. By 1893, the Union Typewriter Company — a merger of the five largest manufacturers — adopted the layout as a universal standard. The standardization was about interchangeable labor: a typist trained on one machine could sit at another. Business schools, secretarial programs, and public schools trained millions on the arrangement. The motor patterns took months to develop and became automatic — fingers moving to letters without conscious navigation, the way practiced musicians find notes without looking at the instrument.

The IBM Selectric replaced typebars with a rotating type ball in 1961. Electronic typewriters eliminated mechanical linkage in the 1970s. Computer keyboards translated keystrokes into electrical signals. Touchscreen phones eliminated keys entirely — the "keyboard" became a flat image on glass, responsive to fingertip capacitance. At each stage, the constraint that produced the arrangement disappeared. The jamming problem was solved, then the mechanism that could jam was removed, then the physical key was removed. The arrangement remained.

August Dvorak patented an alternative layout in 1936, placing vowels on the home row. The US Navy conducted wartime studies showing faster learning curves, though Liebowitz and Margolis demonstrated in 1990 that the Navy studies were conducted by Dvorak himself and that independent research showed negligible speed differences between layouts for trained typists. The question of which layout is "better" has never been conclusively answered — and has never needed to be. The layout persists not because it won a competition but because the competition was never held. The training was already in the fingers by the time anyone thought to compare.

Four billion people use QWERTY. Each has spent months or years building motor patterns specific to the arrangement. The patterns reside in the motor cortex, in the basal ganglia, in the automatic sequences that constitute typing fluency. The fluency produces a subjective experience: the letters feel like they belong where they are. The feeling is indistinguishable from correctness. A person who has typed QWERTY for twenty years does not experience the layout as "a solution to typebar jamming in 1873." The person experiences the layout as the keyboard — as the natural and obvious way to arrange twenty-six letters. The history is invisible because the fluency overwrites it. The practice erases the origin. What remains is the feeling that the arbitrary arrangement is the correct one, held in place not by any machine but by four billion trained hands on four billion glass screens, typing on the ghost of a typewriter that no longer exists.
