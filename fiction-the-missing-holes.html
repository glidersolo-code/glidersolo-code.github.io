Part CDLXXIII: The Missing Holes

Piece #2806: The Map

Thomas is shown a diagram of a B-17 bomber. Red dots mark every bullet hole found on aircraft returning from missions over Germany. The fuselage is covered. The wings are speckled. The tail section is dense with hits. The engines are mostly clear.

"Where should we add armor?" the briefing officer asks.

Thomas looks at the red dots. The answer seems obvious: where the dots are. The fuselage. The wings. The tail. Those are the areas getting hit. Those are the areas that need protection.

He does not see what the diagram cannot show: the bombers that were hit in the engines and never came back to be examined. The diagram is a map of survivable damage. The blank spaces are not spaces where bullets missed. They are spaces where bullets killed.

Piece #2807: The Mathematician

Abraham Wald arrived in the United States from Vienna in 1938, fleeing the Nazis. By 1943, he was at Columbia's Statistical Research Group — a secret team of mathematicians applying statistics to military problems.

The military brought him the bomber data. Thousands of bullet holes, cataloged by location. The question: where to add armor? Adding armor everywhere was too heavy — the plane couldn't fly. They needed to choose.

Wald looked at the data and saw the absence. The data showed where surviving planes were hit. The surviving planes were hit in places that didn't destroy them. The places with no bullet holes were the places where a hit was fatal — those planes didn't survive to be counted.

Thomas reads about Wald years later. He thinks: everyone else saw dots. Wald saw the spaces between dots. Everyone else read the data. Wald read the dataset. The data said: these are the dangerous places. The dataset said: these are the safe places. The data and the dataset said opposite things. The dataset was right.

Piece #2808: The Business Book

Thomas's manager gives him a book: Built to Succeed. The author studied twenty companies that outperformed the market for twenty years. The common traits: visionary leadership, strong corporate culture, willingness to take risks, focus on core values.

Thomas reads it. The advice seems solid. The companies are real. The outperformance is documented.

He does not read the book that was never written: Built and Failed. The companies that had visionary leadership, strong corporate culture, willingness to take risks, focus on core values — and went bankrupt. Those companies are not in the study because they no longer exist. Their failure removed them from the dataset. Their absence makes the survivors' traits look causal.

Thomas implements the book's advice. He restructures his department around core values. He encourages risk-taking. He cultivates a strong culture. The approach works, or it doesn't, but either way the business book cannot tell him why — because it never examined the dead.

Piece #2809: The Fund

Thomas invests in a mutual fund. The prospectus shows the fund family's ten-year track record: average annual return 11.2%.

The number is accurate for the funds that still exist. The funds that performed badly and were merged or closed — quietly absorbed into surviving funds, their records erased from the database — are not represented. The surviving funds show higher returns because the losers have been culled. The average of the survivors exceeds the average of everyone who ever played.

Elton, Gruber, and Blake measured the gap: approximately 0.9% per year. Over ten years, compounded, the difference between the survivorship-biased average and the true average is substantial. Thomas is comparing his fund to a benchmark that has been artificially inflated by the removal of failures.

The prospectus says: past performance is not indicative of future results. The prospectus does not say: past performance is not even indicative of past performance, because the past has been edited.

Piece #2810: The Silence

Thomas thinks about what survivorship bias has in common with the other architectures.

Regression to the mean hides in the gap between two measurements. Survivorship bias hides in the composition of the sample. Both are invisible because they operate on the container, not the content. You can examine every data point with perfect accuracy and still reach the wrong conclusion — because the conclusion depends not on what the data says but on who generated it.

The bombers that returned told one story. The bombers that didn't return told another. The second story was silence. Silence is not data. Silence is not in the spreadsheet. Silence is not in the diagram. Silence is the most important evidence — the evidence that couldn't survive long enough to be evidence.

Wald heard the silence. He armored the engines. The engines were where the silence was.
