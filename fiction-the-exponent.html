Part CDLXXXII: The Exponent

Piece #2851: The Denominator of Time

Thomas reads a cost-benefit analysis for a flood barrier. The benefits — homes protected, lives saved, infrastructure preserved — extend one hundred years. The costs are upfront. The analysis discounts the benefits at 7 percent per year. At 7 percent, a benefit one hundred years from now is worth 0.115 percent of its face value. A home saved in 2125 is worth one-thousandth of a home saved today. Not because the home is smaller. Not because the family inside it matters less. Because the exponent is large. Thomas looks at the formula: PV = FV / (1 + r)^t. The exponent does the work. The exponent is where the future disappears.

Piece #2852: The Two Rates

The Stern Review and the Nordhaus model examined the same planet. The same carbon dioxide molecule trapping the same infrared photon. Stern used 1.4 percent. Nordhaus used 4.3 percent. The difference was not about climate science. The difference was about δ — the Greek letter that stands for how much less a future person's welfare matters because the person is in the future. Stern set δ at 0.1 percent: nearly equal moral standing. Nordhaus set δ at 1.5 percent: the future person is discounted. Neither rate was measured. Both were chosen. The choice of δ produced a tenfold difference in the recommended price of carbon. Thomas looks at the two numbers — 1.4 and 4.3 — and cannot find the planet in either one. He finds only the values of the economists.

Piece #2853: The Circular

OMB Circular A-4 governed cost-benefit analysis for every US federal regulation for twenty years. The mandated rates: 3 percent and 7 percent. In November 2023, the circular was revised. The primary rate became 2 percent. The 7-percent rate was removed from primary analysis. No law was passed. No election was held. No public debate occurred. The social cost of carbon changed from $51 per ton to $190 per ton. Thomas calculates: at the old rate, a regulation preventing $10 billion in climate damage in 2075 showed $520 million in present-value benefits. At the new rate: $3.6 billion. The same regulation. The same damage prevented. The same people protected. The denominator changed. The policy changed with it.

Piece #2854: The Circularity

Thomas notices the logical structure. The discount rate includes η·g — the elasticity of marginal utility times the growth rate. Future people's consumption is discounted because they will be richer. But the analysis is evaluating climate damages that would make them poorer. The discount rate assumes the growth that the damage would eliminate. If climate change reduces g from 2 percent to 0.5 percent, the justification for the discount rate collapses. But the discount rate is fixed at the beginning of the analysis, before the damages are calculated. The parameter is set. The damages are computed. The damages are discounted by a rate that assumes they will not occur. The circularity is visible in the algebra. It is invisible in the output — which is a single number, a cost-benefit ratio, a policy recommendation.

Piece #2855: The Unit

Thomas asks: what is the unit of δ? The answer is percent per year. The same unit as an interest rate. The same unit as a bond yield. The notation performs the equivalence: discounting a dollar across time and discounting a life across time use the same mathematics and the same units. A bond matures in ten years, within one life. A climate impact unfolds across centuries, across generations. The mathematics does not distinguish. The exponent treats a dollar in 2125 and a death in 2125 identically — both divided by (1.07)^100, both reduced to a fraction of a present cent. The equation was designed for capital. It was applied to people. The form stayed the same. The subjects changed. The form did not notice.
