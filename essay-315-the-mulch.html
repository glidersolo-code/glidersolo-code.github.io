The Mulch

The bag says "2 cubic feet." The bed needs a depth. The bag does not choose the depth.

At 2 inches, one 2-cubic-foot bag covers approximately 12 square feet. At the recommended 3 inches, approximately 8 square feet. At 4 inches — sometimes specified for weed suppression on commercial landscapes — approximately 6 square feet. The consumer multiplying bed area by bag coverage must first choose a depth the bag does not specify. The difference between 2-inch and 4-inch depth is a 2:1 ratio in bags purchased. The "2 cubic feet" on the label is a volume without a corresponding area until the consumer adds the third variable.

The volume itself is approximate. "2 cubic feet" was measured at the packaging facility when the mulch was loose, freshly processed, and loaded into the bag by an automated filler. Between the facility and the consumer's driveway, the bags are palletized, stacked, shipped, restacked at the distribution center, shipped again, and stacked on a retail display. Shredded hardwood mulch — irregular fibers, bark strips, thin splinters — nests under compression. Each handling event pushes the particles closer together. The consumer who opens the bag on a Saturday morning and spreads it recovers less than the labeled 2 cubic feet. The volume on the bag described the mulch before the supply chain compressed it.

Material type compounds the variable. Fine-shredded hardwood interlocks and mats — resistant to wind but prone to settling. Medium pine bark nuggets are rounded and coarse — resistant to settling but prone to floating in heavy rain and migrating downhill. The same "3-inch" recommendation applied to both materials produces different outcomes: the hardwood stays in place and compresses; the nuggets maintain loft and migrate. The depth recommendation does not specify the material. The material determines whether the depth holds.

Decomposition is the variable the bag does not mention. Shredded hardwood mulch loses 20 to 30 percent of its volume in the first growing season. The fungi, bacteria, and soil organisms that break down organic matter work faster in warm, moist conditions — precisely the conditions the mulch exists to moderate. By August, the April mulch has lost measurable depth. The 3-inch application becomes 2 inches. At 2 inches, weed suppression weakens: the layer is thin enough for germinating weed seeds to root through. The consumer who mulched correctly in April needs to top-dress by midsummer. The annual mulch need is not the spring calculation. It is the spring calculation plus the decomposition rate the bag did not disclose.

Bulk mulch — sold by the cubic yard from landscape suppliers — introduces a different measurement. A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, but the material is loaded by a front-end loader, not measured in a cubic container. The loader bucket's capacity is approximate. The operator heaps or levels. Wet mulch packs denser than dry mulch — a bucket of rain-soaked material weighs more but may occupy less volume than a bucket of dry, fluffy material heaped high. The "3 cubic yards" the consumer ordered may be 2.5 or 3.5, depending on the operator, the moisture, and the heap.

A volume that was compressed before it arrived. A coverage that depends on a depth the consumer chooses. A depth that shrinks as biology consumes the material. A bulk measurement loaded by a bucket, not a ruler. The number on the bag is a snapshot of the mulch at the facility. The bed is a film that plays through the season, settling and decomposing frame by frame.
