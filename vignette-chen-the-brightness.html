The Brightness

They were looking at a star catalog. A number beside the name. 4.83.

"Absolute magnitude," Maren said. "How bright the Sun actually is."

"What does that mean?"

"How bright it would look from 10 parsecs away."

"No one is 10 parsecs from the Sun. The number describes a brightness observed from a position no observer occupies. And the number runs backwards — brighter objects get smaller numbers, because Hipparchus of Nicaea, around 129 BC, called the brightest stars 'first magnitude,' meaning first rank. Every measurement scale invented since runs the other direction. This one never reversed."

Chen's paragraph was about the convention. Pogson formalized the six-class perceptual system in 1856 — each magnitude step a factor of 2.512, five steps a factor of 100. The logarithmic scale extended Hipparchus's naked-eye categories into negative numbers for bright objects (the Sun at -26.74) and past 30 for objects detectable only by space telescopes. The scale designed for what one man could see from a Greek island now measured what no human eye would ever see, using the same inverted units, the same perceptual origin.

"And the zero-point —"

"Vega. Defined as magnitude 0.00 by Johnson and Morgan in 1953. A rapidly rotating star viewed nearly pole-on — within about 5 degrees of its axis — surrounded by a debris disk that Aumann discovered in 1984. The zero of the brightness scale is a peculiar star seen from an unusual angle. Every magnitude in every catalog is measured relative to this imperfect standard."

"But different filters give different magnitudes."

"The same star has different brightness numbers depending on which glass you look through. The Johnson-Cousins V-band, the Sloan g'-band, the 2MASS J-band — different numbers for the same object. The catalog prints one. The reader sees a property of the star. The number is a property of the filter, the atmosphere, the detector, the standard-star network, and a 2,200-year-old convention about which direction numbers should run."

The paragraph sat at 0.8. It was, like a magnitude, a number that appeared to describe a property of the object while encoding the observer's position, the observer's instrument, the observer's zero-point, and a convention so old it had become invisible — running backwards through specific glass, anchored to a peculiar star, extending far beyond the perception it was designed to report, the format performing objectivity while the content remained particular to an island, a century, an eye.
