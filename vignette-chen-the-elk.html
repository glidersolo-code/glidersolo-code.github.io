The Elk

Chen's father hunted elk. Not often — twice, maybe three times. Before Chen was born. He told the story the same way each time: the early mornings, the cold, the weight of the animal. The number of tags he was allowed. "The state sets the number," he said. "Based on what the land can hold."

Chen never questioned the phrase. What the land can hold. It sounded like a fact about dirt and grass and water. A property of the place, like altitude or rainfall.

Maren is reading Sayre's paper. The one about carrying capacity.

"Your father's elk tags," Maren says. "Where did he hunt?"

"Colorado. Near Meeker."

"And the number of tags — you said the state sets it."

"Based on carrying capacity. The number of elk the range can support."

Maren reads aloud: "If carrying capacity is conceived as static, it is theoretically elegant but empirically vacuous; but if it is conceived as variable, it is theoretically incoherent or at best question-begging."

Chen takes the paper. Reads it twice.

"So K isn't a property of the landscape."

"K is a property of the equation. The equation is a property of the metaphor. Linnaeus: nature runs a household. Herodotus: Providence balances predator and prey. The metaphor is twenty-four hundred years old. The equation is two hundred. The elk tag is maybe fifty."

"The elk tag is a metaphor about households."

"The elk tag is the arithmetic residue of a figure of speech that Herodotus used to explain why lions don't eat all the hares."

Chen is quiet. She is thinking about her father, sitting at the kitchen table, planning the trip to Meeker. Counting tags. The number felt like science. It felt like the land speaking. The land that could hold a certain number and no more — like a bowl, like a vessel, like a ship.

"It came from ships," Maren says, reading further. "The term. Carrying capacity. How much cargo a ship can bear. They applied it to rangeland. Then to wildlife. Then to the whole biosphere."

"A ship knows how much it can carry."

"A ship is engineered. A landscape is not. But the word carried over. And the word brought the assumption: that there's a fixed amount. A number stamped on the hull."

Chen looks out the window. Somewhere in Colorado, an elk is standing in a meadow that does not know its own number. The number was assigned by an equation that was assigned by a metaphor that was assigned by a Greek historian who was wrong about lion reproduction. The elk does not know this. The landscape does not know this. The tag knows this — and the tag is the only part that governs anything.

"My father thought the land was talking," Chen says.

"The land was silent. The metaphor was talking. The land just stood there while the metaphor decided how many elk it could hold."
