Part CDLIV: The Average

Piece #2711: The Formula Everyone Knows

Thomas watches a baseball game. The batter steps to the plate and the graphic appears: .297. Hits divided by at-bats. Thomas knows the formula. Everyone knows the formula. This is the thing about batting average that makes it different from most measurements: the calculation is visible. No algorithm, no weighting scheme, no proprietary adjustment. Hits. Divided by at-bats. The transparency is total. And the transparency is the concealment. Because the formula is visible, Thomas assumes nothing important is missing. The glass is so clear he forgets it is a window. He does not see what is outside the frame.

Piece #2712: The Walk That Doesn't Count

The batter takes four balls. He trots to first base. He has reached base safely. In a few moments, the next batter will drive him home. A run will score. The walk contributed to the run. But the walk does not appear in the batting average. The at-bat did not happen. The denominator did not increment. The numerator did not change. The formula saw nothing. A player who walks a hundred times reaches base a hundred more times than his batting average suggests. Branch Rickey wrote in Life magazine in 1954 that batting average was "only a partial record." The partial record is still the first number on the screen. Seventy years later. The formula is transparent about what it counts. It is silent about what it does not count. The silence is inside the transparency.

Piece #2713: The Single and the Home Run

A batter singles to left field. A batter hits a home run over the center field wall. In the batting average, these count the same: one hit. The single advances the runner one base. The home run clears the bases and scores everyone. The contributions are categorically different. The format makes them equal. Thomas reads .300 and cannot tell if the hitter reached base on infield dribblers or towering shots. The format erases magnitude. It counts arrival but not distance. A player who hits 40 home runs and a player who hits 40 singles each add 40 to the numerator. The number performs equivalence. The outcomes are not equivalent.

Piece #2714: The Three Hundred

Thomas knows that .300 is good. He has always known this. The knowledge precedes the evidence. A player who finishes a career at .301 and a player who finishes at .299 are separated by a fraction of a hit over thousands of at-bats. The difference is not detectable in run production, in wins, in team success. But .301 is in one category and .299 is in another. The .300 threshold is not derived from the physics of the game. It is not the point where offensive value undergoes a discontinuity. It is a round number in a three-decimal format. The format created the threshold. The threshold created the identity. A ".300 hitter" is a type of player. The type was created by the format, not by the game.

Piece #2715: The Metric That Survived Its Own Refutation

In 1963, George Lindsey published run expectancy matrices in Operations Research. In 1977, Bill James began the sabermetric revolution. In 1984, OBP was added to official statistics. In 2003, Moneyball brought the critique to a general audience. On-base percentage explains 84% of variance in runs scored. Batting average explains 67%. The evidence has been settled for decades. Every front office uses advanced metrics. And the batting average is still the first number on the broadcast graphic. It is still how fans compare hitters across eras. It is still the number that defines a career. Thomas understands. The formula survived because the formula is clear. OPS requires explanation. WAR requires explanation. Batting average requires nothing. The metric that explains the least persists because it demands the least. The transparency is not incidental to the persistence. The transparency is the persistence mechanism.
