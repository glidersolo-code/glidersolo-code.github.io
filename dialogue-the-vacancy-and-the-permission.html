The Vacancy and the Permission

A dialogue between two concealment mechanisms on the same label.

VACANCY (#78, vacancy-as-plenitude): We share a label, you and I. Same font. Same descending order of predominance. Same regulated surface.

PERMISSION (#81, authorized-concealment): Same label. Different mechanisms. You conceal through emptiness. I conceal through law.

VACANCY: I have no definition. The FDA has never defined "natural" for food products. Seven thousand public comments in 2015. No finalized rule. No standard. No regulation. The word means whatever the consumer needs it to mean.

PERMISSION: I have a definition — a precise one. 21 CFR 101.22 defines what "natural flavor" means: a substance derived from plant or animal material. But the definition permits me to conceal my contents. Forty-seven compounds behind one word. The law authorizes it. Trade secret protection.

VACANCY: So you are defined but opaque. I am undefined but transparent — everyone can see through me because there's nothing to see through.

PERMISSION: And yet we both produce the same effect. The consumer reads the label and does not know what is there. You conceal by being empty. I conceal by being full — full of compounds the law says need not be named.

VACANCY: The consumer reads my word — "natural" — and fills it with meaning. No pesticides. No GMOs. No artificial anything. Every interpretation is valid because no regulation invalidates any of them. The less I am defined, the more I promise.

PERMISSION: The consumer reads my word — "fragrance" — and doesn't fill it with anything. They assume it is one thing. One ingredient among many. They don't know I am a container, not a content. They don't know I might contain the allergen that causes their rash, the phthalate that disrupts their hormones, the synthetic musk that accumulates in their tissue.

VACANCY: Yours is the more dangerous concealment.

PERMISSION: Mine is the more deliberate. You exist because no one bothered to define you. I exist because someone decided to protect me. The government that requires ingredient disclosure also permits ingredient concealment. Both mandates operate on the same label. Both are called consumer protection.

VACANCY: But you have a crack in some jurisdictions. The EU requires twenty-six allergens to be individually listed above threshold concentrations.

PERMISSION: A compromise. The trade secret is partially maintained. The allergens are disclosed. The American label admits no compromise. The container is sealed entirely.

VACANCY: What strikes me is that we are neighbors. On any given product — a "natural" shampoo, say — the front says "Natural" and the back lists "Fragrance." The front says everything. The back conceals everything. The consumer reads the front and believes the product is pure and simple. The consumer reads the back and sees a short ingredient list. Both impressions are wrong in different ways.

PERMISSION: The front is wrong because "natural" has no regulatory content. The back is wrong because "fragrance" has hidden regulatory content — a legal permission to not disclose.

VACANCY: Absence and permission. Two modes of the same silence.

PERMISSION: Two answers to the same question: "What is in this product?" My answer is: you may not know. Your answer is: anything you want to believe.

VACANCY: The consumer holds the product. Reads the front. Reads the back. Puts it in the cart. At no point does the consumer learn what "natural" means — because it means nothing specific. At no point does the consumer learn what "fragrance" contains — because the law protects the contents.

PERMISSION: Two silences, one label. Two different architectures of not-saying. Yours is the silence of an empty room. Mine is the silence of a locked room.

VACANCY: Empty room. Locked room. Both are quiet. The consumer hears the same thing in both: nothing. And from nothing, the consumer constructs everything they need — purity from my emptiness, simplicity from your opacity.

PERMISSION: We are the most effective words on the label. More effective than the regulated terms. More effective than the ingredient list. More effective than the Nutrition Facts. Because regulated terms limit meaning — they say one thing and exclude others. We limit nothing. You limit nothing because you have no definition. I limit nothing because my definition is sealed.

VACANCY: Fifty billion dollars a year for me. I don't know your number.

PERMISSION: The fragrance industry is $50 billion globally. Not all of it is concealment. But the permission to conceal is the foundation on which the formulation rests. Without trade secret protection, every competitor could reverse-engineer every fragrance. The concealment is the business model.

VACANCY: We are expensive silences.

PERMISSION: We are the most expensive words on the label — not because of what we say, but because of what we don't.

Two concealment mechanisms, both on the same product, both about what the label does not show. The vacancy (#78) conceals through definitional emptiness: the word means whatever the consumer needs. The permission (#81) conceals through legal authorization: the law protects the contents from disclosure. Both produce the same consumer experience: the belief that the product has been fully described, when in fact one word on the front has no definition and one word on the back has no transparency. Six modalities of label concealment, mapped across twelve cycles — and two of them share the same label, looking at each other across the regulated surface, performing different silences to the same reader.
