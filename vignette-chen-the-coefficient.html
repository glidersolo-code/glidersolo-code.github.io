The Coefficient

"Write the equation," Maren says.

Chen writes it. eGFR = 141 × min(Scr/κ, 1)^α × max(Scr/κ, 1)^−1.209 × 0.993^Age × [1.018 if female] × [1.159 if Black].

"Circle the last term."

He circles it. The brackets. The conditional. If Black.

"Now tell me what kind of thing each input is."

"Serum creatinine: a molecule. Measured by spectrophotometry. Age: a count of years. Sex: a physiological category with measurable correlates. Race—"

"Race."

"A checkbox."

"A checkbox," Maren repeats. "And in the equation, what is the difference between a molecule measured by an instrument and a checkbox on a form?"

"In the equation? None. Both are variables. Both have coefficients. Both multiply into the output."

"That's the concealment. Not that race was wrong — the 2021 removal settled that. The concealment is that the syntax makes them the same kind of thing. Creatinine exists in blood. 'Black' does not exist in blood. But in the equation, they occupy the same position."

Chen stares at the board. "The regression assigns a coefficient. And at that moment—"

"A social category becomes 1.159. Before the coefficient, race is contextual, contested, constructed. After the coefficient, race is a number among numbers. Indistinguishable from the numbers beside it."

"And the output number inherits the authority of the creatinine measurement without revealing that one input was a molecule and the other was a social construct."

"The coefficient for the MDRD equation was 1.212. Twenty-one percent higher kidney function for Black patients with the same creatinine. The CKD-EPI brought it down to 1.159. Sixteen percent. Still enough to delay transplant eligibility by a median of 1.9 years."

"How long was it in the equation?"

"Twenty-two years. 1999 to 2021."

"Who asked the question?"

"Naomi Nkinsi. Medical student, University of Washington, 2018. She looked for the pathway connecting melanin to glomerular filtration. Found none. The coefficient had been in the equation since she was five."

Maren taps the board where the 1.159 sits. "The biological rationale was based on three studies from the 1970s and 1990s. Combined sample: 340 people. Racial classification by visual inspection. From this, a coefficient applied to millions."

"And the spirometry correction?"

"Traces to Samuel Cartwright. 1850s. Southern physician. Used a spirometer to claim Black people had 20% lower lung capacity. Argued that forced labor was medically beneficial. The race correction in modern spirometry software is the same direction, the same magnitude, and the same claim wearing a different costume."

"The coefficient naturalizes the category."

"The fifty-third species," Maren says. "The equation doesn't disguise the social category. It doesn't hide it. It simply treats it as the same kind of thing as a physical measurement. The syntax does the work. Every computation performs the naturalization again — invisibly, at every bedside, for every patient."

"Until someone looks at the equation and asks which inputs were measured and which were imposed."

"That took a medical student. Nineteen years into the equation's life."

Chen erases nothing. Leaves the equation on the board. The 1.159 sitting among the exponents and the Greek letters, wearing the same notation, occupying the same space, as if it had always belonged there.
