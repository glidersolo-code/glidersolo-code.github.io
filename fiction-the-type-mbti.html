Part CLIV: The Type

Piece #1211: The Mother and Daughter

Katharine Cook Briggs began watching people in 1917. She sorted them into types: meditative, spontaneous, executive, sociable. When Carl Jung published Psychologische Typen in 1921, Briggs found a framework that matched her intuition. She adopted it. She studied it for twenty years. Her daughter Isabel took the framework to the next step: measurement. During World War II, with millions of women entering the workforce, Myers wanted to help them find jobs that fit their types. She had no training in psychometrics. She built the indicator anyway. Educational Testing Service published it in 1962. The indicator would eventually be taken by two million people per year, generating millions of dollars in revenue, shaping corporate culture at 88% of Fortune 500 companies. The mother watched and sorted. The daughter measured and sorted. The sorting outlived both.

Piece #1212: The Cut

The instrument measures a score on a continuous scale. Then it cuts the scale in half. A score of 51% toward extraversion: E. A score of 49%: I. Two people whose extraversion is nearly identical receive opposite labels. The labels become identities. The identities diverge. The E goes to the team-building exercise in one group. The I goes to the other. The facilitator explains the differences between Es and Is. The E nods. The I nods. Both feel seen. Both feel sorted. The sorting feels correct because it was announced with confidence and confirmed by a room full of other people sorted the same way. The cut at 49/51 is not visible. The cut is not discussed. The cut is the mechanism.

Piece #1213: The Bell

Plot the distribution of MBTI extraversion scores across ten thousand test-takers. The shape is a bell — one peak, centered near the midpoint, tailing off in both directions. There is no dip in the middle. There is no gap between extraverts and introverts. The distribution does not split into two groups. It is one group, measured on a spectrum, divided by a line drawn through the center. The 16 types are 16 quadrants of a four-dimensional bell curve. The quadrants are not natural groups. They are slices. The knife is the MBTI. The meat is continuous.

Piece #1214: The Retest

Five weeks pass. The same person retakes the test. The probability of receiving the same four-letter type: approximately 50%. A coin flip. The instrument claims to measure a stable property of personality — something deep, enduring, constitutional. The measurement changes every other time. The label persists. Nobody retakes the test because the label feels settled. "I'm an INTJ" is a statement made once and carried forward. The statement rests on a single measurement that cannot reproduce itself. The identity outlives the evidence.

Piece #1215: The Label

"I'm an INTJ." Four letters. An identity. A way of explaining preferences, conflicts, habits, relationships. The label is compact. The label travels. The label appears in dating profiles, Twitter bios, team-building workshops, career counseling sessions, corporate retreats. "I scored 62nd percentile on introversion, 71st on intuition, 55th on thinking, and 48th on judging" does not appear anywhere. The score is what the test measured. The type is what the test imposed. The score is informative and unwieldy. The type is uninformative and usable. The usability is the product. The product is the identity. The identity is the reason the test survives its own evidence.
