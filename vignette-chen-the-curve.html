The Curve

Chen is drawing on a napkin. Two bowed lines, both below a diagonal.

"These are two different countries," he says. "Different inequality structures. Different experiences for their citizens. Different problems requiring different solutions."

Maren looks at the curves. The first bows gently through most of its length, then drops sharply near the right edge. The second bows evenly throughout — a smooth, deep arc.

"The first one," Chen says, "is a country with a big middle class and a very rich top. The inequality is concentrated at the top. The second is a country with two clusters — low income and high income, hollow middle. The inequality is everywhere."

"And the Gini coefficient?"

"0.40. Both of them."

Maren stares at the napkin. "The same number."

"The same number. The Gini is the ratio of the area between the curve and the diagonal to the total area below the diagonal. Both curves create the same area. Both produce the same scalar. The scalar keeps the magnitude and throws away the shape."

"And the shape is — "

"The shape is where the inequality lives. Whether the problem is an oligarchic elite or a collapsing middle class. Whether a progressive tax fixes it or barely touches it. Whether the citizens feel squeezed or left behind or both. The shape determines everything that matters about the inequality. The number discards it."

Maren picks up the napkin. Turns it. "Why does anyone use this number?"

"Because it's one decimal. It fits in a table. It fits in a headline. You can rank 190 countries in a single column. The Lorenz curve requires a graph. A graph requires space. Space requires attention. The scalar requires nothing."

"Heisig — the 2022 study?"

"Multi-parameter models consistently outperform the Gini. Two numbers instead of one — bottom-heavy inequality vs. top-heavy inequality — explain outcomes better. Health. Social trust. Political stability. But two numbers don't rank as easily. Two numbers don't fit the column."

Maren sets the napkin down. "The number wins because it's portable."

"Portability selects for compression. Compression selects against shape. The table selects for the number that fits. The number that fits is the number that lost the curve."

"Zero point four."

"Zero point four. Two societies. One number. No shape."
