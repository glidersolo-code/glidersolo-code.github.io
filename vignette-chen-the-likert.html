Vignette Chen The Likert

Vignette

Chen is filling out a course evaluation. The form asks: "The instructor explained concepts clearly." Five options. Strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, strongly agree. Chen thinks about Dr. Okafor's seminar. Some sessions were luminous — the reading came alive, connections emerged that Chen hadn't seen. Other sessions were routine — competent, workmanlike, adequate. One session was genuinely confusing, though Chen later realized the confusion was productive. He is being asked to compress fourteen weeks into one box.

He selects "agree." Not because it accurately describes the experience but because it is the closest landing strip. The experience was a 3.7, or a 4.2 on Tuesdays and a 3.1 on Thursdays, or a shape that numbers don't describe. The form offers five integers. He selects the fourth.

Maren, sitting beside him in the computer lab, selects "strongly agree" for the same course. She liked the same sessions Chen liked. She found the same confusing session confusing. Her experience of the course, if they talked about it, would sound very similar to his. The difference between their selections is not a difference in experience. It is a difference in how they interact with the format. Maren uses the extremes. Chen tends toward the center. This is not a personality difference — or rather, it is a personality difference that the format converts into a measurement difference. The survey cannot separate the teaching from the response style.

Chen knows this. He has read the literature. Schwarz, 1991: the same question, the same verbal labels, a twenty-one-percentage-point shift from changing the numbers alone. 0-to-10 versus -5-to-+5. The numbers change the answer. The labels are identical. The respondent is not choosing a description of their experience. The respondent is navigating a format. The navigation is shaped by the numbers, the culture, the cognitive effort, the time of day, the number of surveys already completed this week.

The department will receive a report. Dr. Okafor's mean rating on "explains concepts clearly": 4.12. The number will be compared to the department average of 3.87. Dr. Okafor will be above average. The committee will note this. No one on the committee will ask what 4.12 means — what continuous experience was forced into what discrete bins, what response styles inflated or deflated the number, what the difference between 4.12 and 3.87 represents in terms of actual teaching quality. The difference is 0.25 on a scale whose actual resolution is 1.0. The difference is a quarter of a box. The committee will treat it as meaningful.

Chen submits the evaluation. He moves to the next question. "The course materials were well organized." He thinks about this for approximately two seconds and selects "agree." Krosnick would call this satisficing. Chen calls it Tuesday.
