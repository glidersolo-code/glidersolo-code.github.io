Part CCLXVI: The Surge

Piece #1771: The Joules

The surge protector says "2,100 joules." Thomas plugged his computer, monitor, and external drive into it. He chose the 2,100-joule model over the 1,000-joule model because more joules means more protection. The joule rating describes the total energy the metal-oxide varistors inside the device can absorb over its entire lifetime — not per event. Each surge degrades the varistors. A 500-joule surge reduces the remaining capacity to 1,600 joules. A second 500-joule surge: 1,100 joules. A third: 600. A fourth: 100. After the fifth, the varistors are depleted. The surge protector continues to pass electricity to the outlets. The indicator light may still show "protected." The protection is gone. Thomas, having purchased the 2,100-joule model three years ago, does not know how many joules the power surges during that period consumed. He trusts the indicator light. The indicator light trusts the manufacturer's threshold.

Piece #1772: The Clamp

The clamping voltage determines when the protection begins. UL 1449 specifies clamping voltage ratings — the voltage at which the varistors begin diverting surge energy to ground. Thomas's protector clamps at 400 volts. A competitor clamps at 330 volts. Standard household voltage is 120 volts. A surge that spikes to 350 volts: Thomas's 400V-clamped protector does nothing — the spike is below its activation threshold. The surge passes through to the computer at 350 volts. The competitor's 330V-clamped protector activates at 330 volts and diverts the excess. Thomas's computer receives a 350-volt spike. The competitor's computer receives approximately 330 volts. Both protectors list their joule rating prominently. The clamping voltage is in the manual or on the UL listing — not on the shelf card.

Piece #1773: The Speed

Response time. The varistors do not activate instantaneously. When a surge arrives — voltage rising in microseconds — the varistors need nanoseconds to begin clamping. Thomas's protector responds in approximately 5 nanoseconds. Five nanoseconds of the surge's leading edge passes through to the equipment before any protection engages. For a fast-rising lightning-induced transient, the leading edge may reach 1,000 volts in the first 10 nanoseconds. The first 5 nanoseconds of a 1,000-volt surge pass through Thomas's protector unimpeded: approximately 500 volts of the leading edge reaches the equipment. A protector with 1-nanosecond response lets approximately 100 volts of the leading edge through. The response time is not on the box. The consumer compares joules. The leading edge compares nanoseconds.

Piece #1774: The Power Strip

Thomas's surge protector is also a power strip. Six outlets, a 4-foot cord, an on/off switch. On the shelf, it sat between a $12 power strip (no surge protection, no joule rating, six outlets, 4-foot cord) and a $45 surge protector (2,100 joules, six outlets, 4-foot cord). The physical appearance was similar. The $12 power strip is a conductor — it extends the wall outlet to six outlets. It provides zero surge protection. The $45 surge protector does the same physical job plus contains varistors that absorb surge energy. After the varistors deplete, the surge protector becomes the power strip. The consumer cannot tell by looking at the device whether the varistors are still functional. The most expensive surge protector and the cheapest power strip, after enough surges, are the same thing.

Piece #1775: The Warranty

Two hundred and sixty-six Parts. The box says "connected equipment warranty: $300,000." Thomas read this as a promise: if a surge damages his equipment while connected to this protector, the manufacturer will pay up to $300,000. The warranty's fine print requires: proof that the surge protector was properly installed (grounded outlet, no daisy-chaining), proof that the damage resulted from a surge event (requires electrician's documentation or utility company confirmation), proof that the equipment was not damaged by any other cause, and a claim filed within 30 days. The $300,000 warranty is a conditional promise with conditions the consumer cannot easily satisfy in the aftermath of a power surge that damaged their equipment. Thomas read the headline. The conditions lived in the pamphlet inside the box, which Thomas discarded during setup. The number said $300,000. The conditions said "maybe."
