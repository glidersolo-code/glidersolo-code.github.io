Part CDLXVI: The Live Birth

Piece #2771: The Form

Thomas is thinking about a birth certificate. The form has fields: name, sex, date of birth, time of birth, weight, gestational age. The form exists because the event it records — a live birth — has been defined. The WHO definition: any product of conception that, after separation from its mother, breathes or shows any evidence of life. Heartbeat. Pulsation of the umbilical cord. Movement of voluntary muscles. Regardless of gestational age. Regardless of weight.

Thomas considers the word "regardless." The definition admits no threshold. A 40-week infant weighing 3,500 grams who cries loudly is a live birth. A 22-week fetus weighing 380 grams whose umbilical cord pulses once before stopping is a live birth. The same form. The same definition. The same denominator.

The United States follows this definition literally. The form is filled out. The birth is registered. If the infant dies — three minutes later, three hours later, three days later — the death is also registered. The birth enters the denominator of the infant mortality rate. The death enters the numerator. Both counted. Both official. Both facts.

Piece #2772: The Other Form

Thomas is thinking about the same biological event in a different country. A 23-week delivery in a hospital in Lyon. The fetus shows a brief heartbeat. In the United States, this is a live birth. In France, births below 22 weeks of gestation are not registered as live births. At 23 weeks, the French classification depends on the assessment of the attending physician — whether the birth is considered viable, whether resuscitation is attempted, whether the event is recorded as a live birth or a late fetal death.

The same heartbeat. The same gestational age. Different forms. Different countries, different thresholds, different registrations. The birth that enters the US denominator does not enter the French denominator. The death that enters the US numerator does not enter the French numerator. Both are excluded. The French rate is lower — not because a different outcome occurred, but because a different definition applied.

Thomas thinks about the Netherlands, with a 24-week viability threshold. About Norway, which historically used 28 weeks. About Japan, where some periviable births showing transient signs of life are classified as late fetal deaths rather than live births. Each country draws its own boundary around the word "born." The boundary determines who enters the count. The count determines the rate. The rate determines the ranking. The ranking is published without the boundary.

Piece #2773: The Rate

Thomas is thinking about the number 5.4. The US infant mortality rate, approximately, in 2022. Deaths before age one per 1,000 live births. The number ranks the US behind Japan (1.8), Finland (2.1), Sweden (2.4), and most of Western Europe. The ranking appears in news articles, policy reports, WHO databases, and political speeches. The number communicates: the US does worse. Other countries care for their infants more effectively.

Thomas reads the number again. 5.4. The number does not carry an asterisk. The number does not say "using a denominator that includes births other countries would not count." The number performs as a comparison of outcomes. The number is partly a comparison of definitions.

Births at 22 to 25 weeks gestation have mortality rates exceeding 50%. The US counts all of them. Countries with narrower definitions exclude these births from both numerator and denominator — from both the death count and the birth count. The exclusion is symmetrical: neither the birth nor the death appears. The rate is unaffected by events the country's definition does not recognize.

Thomas considers what this means. If the US applied France's registration threshold, some number of live births would be reclassified as stillbirths. Some number of infant deaths would disappear from the numerator. The rate would fall. The healthcare system would not have changed. The outcomes would not have changed. The definition would have changed. The definition is part of the rate.

Piece #2774: The Adjustment

Thomas is thinking about the 2016 study by Alice Chen, Emily Oster, and Heidi Williams. The title: "Why Is Infant Mortality Higher in the United States Than in Europe?" The study attempted to adjust for registration differences — to compare like with like.

The finding: after adjusting for differential reporting of births near the viability threshold, the US disadvantage narrows. But it does not disappear. The residual gap concentrates in postneonatal mortality — deaths between one month and twelve months. Not the first days. Not the periviable births that registration differences might explain. The later deaths. The deaths that happen after the infant has been discharged, after the initial crisis has passed, after the definition can no longer explain the difference.

Thomas thinks about what postneonatal mortality measures. Access to follow-up care. Safe sleep environments. Adequate nutrition. Protection from environmental hazards. Poverty. The things that happen after the form is filled out. The definition determines the denominator. The social structure determines the numerator — at least the part of the numerator that survives the definitional adjustment.

The study splits the rate into two concealments. The first: the definition, inflating the US rate through broader inclusion. The second: the social reality, producing deaths that no definitional adjustment can explain. The number 5.4 carries both. The number shows neither.

Piece #2775: The Boundary

Thomas thinks about the word "born." Born alive. Born dead. The boundary between these two words is the boundary that determines the denominator. The boundary is drawn by regulation, by medical convention, by cultural practice, by the forms available in the delivery room. The boundary is not drawn by the infant.

A single heartbeat. A single breath. A single pulsation of the umbilical cord. In one country, these constitute a life — brief, registered, counted. In another country, these constitute a fetal demise — documented differently, filed differently, excluded from the rate. The same cardiac activity. The same respiratory effort. Different paperwork. Different rates. Different rankings.

Thomas recognizes the pattern. The infant mortality rate presents as a measure of how well a country protects its youngest citizens. The rate is partly a measure of how broadly a country defines which biological events produce citizens. The definition is invisible in the number. The number is precise — 5.4 per 1,000. The precision performs comparability. The comparability requires that the denominators mean the same thing. The denominators do not mean the same thing. The word "born" is doing work the number cannot show. The denominator is not neutral. The denominator is the instrument.
