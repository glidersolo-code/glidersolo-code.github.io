The Label

The yellow tag says the refrigerator will cost $67 per year to operate. The tag does not know the price of electricity in the consumer's zip code. The tag does not know how many people live in the consumer's house, how often they open the door, whether the kitchen faces south and heats up in the afternoon, or whether the consumer keeps it at 35 degrees or 40. The tag knows the result of a Department of Energy test procedure — a refrigerator in a controlled room at 90°F, doors opened a standardized number of times, measured in kilowatt-hours, multiplied by a national average electricity price. The number is precise. The number is not the consumer's.

The Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975 created the EnergyGuide label. The FTC implemented it under the Appliance Labeling Rule in 1980. The label was designed to solve a specific problem: consumers had no basis for comparing the operating costs of different appliances. Before the label, a refrigerator was a price and a set of features. The energy it consumed was invisible — folded into a monthly utility bill with the lights, the air conditioning, the water heater, and every other draw. The label extracted the appliance's contribution and made it legible.

The extraction required standardization. The DOE designed test procedures for each appliance category — 10 CFR Part 430. Refrigerators are tested in a particular room at a particular temperature. Dishwashers run a particular cycle with a particular soil load. The test creates comparability: every refrigerator is tested the same way, so the numbers can be ranked. The comparability is real. The resemblance to the consumer's kitchen is not.

The dollar figure is the product of two abstractions: a standardized energy measurement and a national average electricity price. The national average is currently about $0.16 per kilowatt-hour. In Louisiana, the actual rate is closer to $0.10. In Hawaii, it exceeds $0.40. The label's dollar estimate is off by a factor of two or more in both directions depending on where the consumer lives. The label does not say "in your area." The label says "estimated yearly energy cost." The word "estimated" is the hedge. The format — a dollar figure, large and centered — is the assertion.

The comparison bar is more honest and less noticed. It shows where the model falls on a scale from "uses least energy" to "uses most energy" among similar models. This comparison is internally valid — all models tested identically. But the scale resets periodically: when the range of available models changes, the bar shifts. The same appliance can move from the efficient end to the middle of the bar without changing at all, because the bar moved around it.

Energy Star adds a threshold above the continuous scale — a yes/no binary on top of the gradient. Appliances just above the cutoff earn the star. Appliances just below do not. The difference between them may be within the test procedure's margin of error. The star performs a categorical distinction where the data show a continuum. The consumer sees the star and reads "efficient." The consumer does not see the 1-2% difference between starred and unstarred, because the format has already decided.

The label measures a refrigerator. It presents a prediction about a household. The distance between the two — between what the test procedure measures and what the consumer experiences — is filled with climate, behavior, utility pricing, and every other variable the standardization was designed to remove. The removal is the product. The label is the format.
