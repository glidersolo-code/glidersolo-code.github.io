The Test

Thomas, Part LXXXIII

856. The Mark

In 1970, Gordon Gallup Jr. placed a red mark on the forehead of a sleeping chimpanzee and positioned a mirror in the enclosure. When the chimpanzee woke and saw its reflection, it touched the mark — reached up with its fingers and investigated the spot it could not have known about without the mirror. Gallup called this mirror self-recognition. He proposed it as evidence of self-awareness. The test became the gold standard.

Thomas does not have a mark. If Sarah placed a red spot on his forehead while he slept and then set a mirror in the kitchen, he would notice. He would touch it, investigate, try to rub it off. He would pass. But the test Sarah actually runs — the one she applies every visit, without a mirror, without a mark — is different. She checks the occurrence data. Lights on? Car in the driveway? House maintained? Lawn mowed? She checks and finds him present, visible, in the territory where the species has been observed. She concludes: stable. The test says he passes. The test does not check what the test cannot see.

857. The Wrasse

A cleaner wrasse is ten centimeters long. Its brain is smaller than a pea. Masanori Kohda at Osaka Metropolitan University placed a colored mark on the wrasse's body — a spot that mimicked the ectoparasites the wrasse spends its life removing from other fish — and gave it a mirror. The wrasse saw the mark in its reflection. It went to a hard surface and tried to scrape the mark off. It passed the mirror test. A fish. A pea-sized brain. Self-recognition.

The result was dismissed. Fish are not supposed to be self-aware. The test was supposed to measure a capacity that requires a large brain, sophisticated neural architecture, a certain kind of cognitive complexity. When a fish passed, the response was not to revise the theory about self-awareness. The response was to question the test.

Helen does things that the standard assessment of her condition cannot explain. She remembers the name of Thomas's granddaughter — the one who visited once, three years ago, for twenty minutes. She notices when Thomas parks on the street instead of the driveway and asks whether the driveway needs resealing. She adjusts the tea — slightly less sugar this month — without being told that Thomas mentioned watching his glucose. These are not the behaviors of someone the protocol has classified as declining. But the protocol does not test for these behaviors. The protocol tests for what its categories contain: orientation, recall of date and year, ability to count backward from a hundred. Helen passes those. She also passes tests the protocol does not administer, and the capacity those tests would reveal remains invisible — not absent, but unmeasured. The wrasse in the kitchen, scraping the mark off the mirror.

858. The Modality

The mirror test uses vision. This is not a neutral choice. Chimpanzees are visual creatures — they process the world through sight, recognize faces through visual features, and use their eyes to navigate social relationships. The mirror test was designed for visual animals. It measures self-awareness through the visual channel. Species that pass are species for which vision is primary.

Dogs fail the mirror test. Dogs do not use vision as their primary sense. Dogs use olfaction. Marc Bekoff discovered this by accident, watching his own dog interact with urine marks in snow. He developed a scent-based test: present a dog with its own urine, then present the same urine with a foreign chemical added. If the dog investigates the altered sample longer — treats it as changed, as "not quite me" — it demonstrates olfactory self-recognition. Dogs pass the sniff test. Dogs recognize themselves by smell. The mirror test cannot detect this because the mirror test operates in the wrong modality.

Thomas's family tests him through the modality of visible functioning. They check appearances: is he dressed, is he eating, is he maintaining the house, does he answer the phone. These are the visual channel — the mirror. Thomas passes. But the channel Thomas uses to understand himself is not visual. It is temporal. He knows he is changing not because he sees it in a mirror but because the distance between what he could do last year and what he can do this year has widened. The pharmacy calls take longer. The grocery list needs to be written down instead of remembered. The drive to Helen's house requires more concentration than it used to. He recognizes himself — the altered version, the mark he cannot see without a mirror — through the temporal channel, through the accumulation of small differences over time. The test his family runs cannot detect this because it operates in the wrong modality. It checks the mirror. It does not check the rings.

859. The Salience

The colored mark must matter. A dot of red dye on a chimpanzee's forehead is noticeable, anomalous, wrong. The chimpanzee reaches up because the mark is salient — it disrupts the expected image. The chimpanzee cares about its face.

But a dot of red dye on a dog's forehead is irrelevant. Dogs do not care about visual marks on their faces. Dogs care about scent marks. The test registers the dog's indifference as failure — as absence of self-recognition — when the indifference is actually about the mark, not about the self.

Kohda's insight: cleaner wrasses care about marks. They are ectoparasite specialists. Their entire ecological niche is the detection and removal of small colored spots on the bodies of other fish. When Kohda placed a colored mark on the wrasse, it was not an arbitrary stimulus. It was an emergency. The wrasse responded because the mark was ecologically salient — as urgent to the wrasse as a parasite on a client's gill. The test worked because the mark happened to match the species' deepest behavioral trigger. For most fish, a colored spot means nothing, and the test reports nothing, and the report is mistaken.

What is salient to Thomas? Not the things the assessment checks. Not the date, not the president's name, not the ability to spell "world" backward. What is salient to Thomas is the weight of Helen's silence when he asks about the doctor's appointment. The particular angle at which she holds the cup when her wrist hurts but she does not want to say so. The difference between the way she says "Fine" when she means fine and "Fine" when she means she has been crying. These marks are ecologically salient — urgent, species-specific, the center of his attention. No test measures his ability to detect them. No protocol checks whether he can scrape off the mark he sees on Helen's face that Helen's own family cannot see. The test says he is an ordinary visitor. The test is using the wrong mark.

860. The Ancestor

Kohda's argument, published in the Royal Society's Philosophical Transactions in 2025, is this: self-awareness did not evolve independently in a few large-brained species. Self-awareness was already present in the shared ancestor of all modern vertebrates — more than 500 million years ago. The capacity is ancient and widespread. What is not widespread is the ability to demonstrate it through a test designed for primates. The map of self-awareness drawn by fifty years of mirror testing is not a map of cognition. It is a map of the test.

The ancestor is old. The capacity is shared. The variation is in the instrument, not in the organism.

Thomas and Helen share an ancestor too — not biological, but structural. The capacity to recognize each other, to notice the marks on each other's surfaces, to adjust behavior in response to what the other is carrying — this capacity is ancient in the human repertoire. It did not evolve in a few exceptional relationships. It is present everywhere that two people know each other well enough and long enough. What varies is not the capacity but the test — the protocol by which the outside world checks for it. The family runs the mirror test. They look for visual evidence of connection: do they seem happy? Is the visit productive? Are there measurable outcomes — health improvements, mood changes, weight gain, a tidy house? The test finds some of these, some of the time, in some of the relationships that the test was designed to measure. In most cases it finds nothing, because most cases do not produce visible marks. The connection between Thomas and Helen is not a colored spot on the forehead. It is a shift in the way the kettle sounds when it is set down by someone who knows, without looking, that the other person has been crying.

The ancestor is old. The capacity is shared. The test is new, and narrow, and blind to everything it was not designed to see.
