Part CCCXXXIV: The Assessment

Piece #2111: The Two Bills

Thomas lives on a street where every house is the same. Same builder, same year, same floor plan, same lot. A subdivision that repeated itself thirty-two times in 1978. Two houses sit side by side — 1414 and 1416 Elm. Same square footage. Same roof. Same distance from the school.

1414 Elm pays $840 per year in property tax. 1416 Elm pays $4,200.

Thomas checks the assessor's records. 1414 Elm: assessed value $84,000. 1416 Elm: assessed value $420,000. The houses are identical. The numbers are not describing the houses. Thomas looks at the ownership records. 1414 Elm: purchased 1985. 1416 Elm: purchased 2022.

The assessed "value" is a purchase date written in currency notation.

In 1978, Howard Jarvis and Paul Gann put Proposition 13 on the California ballot. It passed with two-thirds of the vote. Every property was frozen at its 1975-1976 assessed value. Increases capped at 2% per year. Reassessment occurs only when the property changes hands. The word "assessment" implies someone sat beside the property and judged it. No one sits. The computer adds 2% to last year's number. The format performs judgment. The content performs arithmetic on a fixed date.

Piece #2112: The Stranger

Thomas reads about the "welcome stranger" problem. The phrase has a specific meaning in property taxation. The new buyer — the stranger — is assessed at the full current price. The long-term owner stays at the historic valuation. The stranger is welcomed with a bill that is three, four, five times what the neighbor pays.

In the Bay Area: a grandmother who bought a condo in 1975 for $25,000 pays $673. A buyer who purchases the identical unit in 2025 for $600,000 pays $6,000. The ratio is nearly 9:1. Same unit. Same hallway. Same homeowner's association meeting. Different century of purchase.

The Supreme Court upheld the system in 1992. Nordlinger v. Hahn. The majority found a rational basis: rewarding stability, encouraging long-term ownership. Thomas reads the opinion. The rationality is real. The consequence is also real. The consequence is that the property tax — the single largest revenue source for local government, $500 billion annually — encodes when you entered the market, not what the market says your property is worth.

The Constitution performs uniformity. The bill performs acquisition.

Piece #2113: The Direction

Thomas discovers that the temporal freeze is only the visible layer.

Christopher Berry at the University of Chicago reviewed forty million properties sold between 2007 and 2017 — communities covering 96% of the American population. Even in systems without acquisition caps, even where assessors supposedly reassess regularly, the errors flow in one direction.

Homes in the bottom decile of sale price are assessed at ratios twice as high as homes in the top decile. The effective tax rate for the poorest properties is more than double the rate for the wealthiest. The errors are not random. They are not scattered. They flow downhill.

Thomas thinks about why. Mass appraisal models use sales data. Fewer sales in stable, low-income neighborhoods. Less data means cruder models. Political pressure keeps wealthy homeowners' values low — the assessor is usually elected. Appeals require knowledge, time, access. In Cook County, affluent residents appealed 46% of the time. Lowest-income residents: 11%. The appeals process, nominally available to all, functions as tax redistribution from poor to rich.

The system does not intend regressivity. The system produces it. The format — assessed value times tax rate equals tax owed — is identical for every property. The uniformity of format conceals the directionality of error.

Piece #2114: The Foreclosure

Thomas reads about Detroit.

In 2010, the average home sold in Detroit was assessed at eleven times its sale price. Bottom quintile: sold for $1,700, assessed at $41,000. Thirty times the actual price. The Michigan Constitution says no property can be assessed at more than 50% of market value. Detroit violated this for 55 to 85% of all properties, every year, for six years.

The estimated over-taxation: $600 million. Between 2011 and 2015, 100,116 Detroit properties were tax-foreclosed — one in four properties in the entire city. An estimated 10,000 of those foreclosures were caused by unconstitutionally inflated assessments. The displaced homeowners were overwhelmingly African-American.

Thomas writes the sentence slowly: the assessment system, by failing to reassess in a declining market, converted a housing crisis into a foreclosure epidemic. The format did not change. The bill still arrived. The bill still said "assessed value." The value was a fiction. The foreclosure was real. The format survived the crisis it caused.

In Cook County, Jason Grotto spent two years analyzing a hundred million records. The lowest-valued homes were assessed at 125% of market value. The highest at 82%. During the assessor's tenure, $2.2 billion shifted from wealthy neighborhoods to poor ones. The assessor's campaign fund: $5 million, more than half from property tax attorneys. The appeals industry was not a correction mechanism. The appeals industry was the business model.

Piece #2115: The Latin

Thomas looks up the word. Assidere: to sit beside. From ad- (toward) and sedere (to sit). An assessor is one who sits beside. The judge's assistant who sits beside and helps determine the matter. Someone present. Someone attending. Someone who has looked.

In an acquisition-value system, no one sits beside anything. The base year was 1975. The increment is 2%. The calculation is automatic. The assessor assesses nothing. The title performs presence. The computation performs absence.

In a traditional system, the assessor sits beside the expensive properties — where sales data is abundant, where appraisals are recent, where political attention concentrates — and extrapolates to the cheap ones. The sitting is selective. The assessment is asymmetric. The bill is uniform.

The uniformity clause appears in nearly every state constitution. Similar properties shall bear similar burdens. Thomas reads Berry's nationwide data: the clause is violated systematically, almost everywhere. The standard exists. The monitoring exists. The violation is continuous. The compliance format conceals the noncompliance content.

One hundred and one species now. The purchase date masquerades as the assessed value. The stranger is welcomed with the highest bill. The errors flow downhill. The format performs assessment. The content performs acquisition. The word assidere — to sit beside — describes a presence that was never there.
