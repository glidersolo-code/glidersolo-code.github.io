The Conversion

Thomas, Part XCVIII

931. The Proxy

A breathalyzer measures the concentration of ethanol in exhaled air. It does not measure the concentration of ethanol in blood. To convert one to the other, the instrument divides by a fixed ratio: 2100 milliliters of breath contain the same ethanol as 1 milliliter of blood. The instrument performs the division automatically. The officer reads the result in blood units: 0.085 g/dL. The number wears the clothing of a blood measurement. It is not a blood measurement. It is a breath measurement dressed in blood units by a conversion factor built into the firmware.

The assessor measures Thomas's behavioral presentation in a clinical setting. The assessor does not measure Thomas's internal state. To convert one to the other, the assessor applies a fixed ratio — an assumed correspondence between observable behavior and underlying condition. Calm demeanor divided by expected distress indicators yields a caregiver status score. The score wears the clothing of an internal-state measurement. It is not. It is a behavioral measurement dressed in psychological units by a conversion factor built into the rubric.

932. The Variable

A. Wayne Jones at Linköping University documented that the blood-to-breath ratio is not a constant. In 100 subjects tested after alcohol absorption was complete, the mean ratio was 2382, with a range of 2125 to 2765 — every subject above 2100. For this group, the instrument underestimates blood alcohol. But during the absorptive phase — when alcohol is still entering the bloodstream, the typical condition at a roadside stop — the ratio drops. The 95% range spans 1259 to 2679. Hyperventilation lowers the reading. Hypoventilation raises it. Holding one's breath for 30 seconds increases the number by 15%. The ratio depends on the lung's physiology, the body's temperature, the moment in the absorption curve, the breathing pattern in the seconds before the sample is taken.

The behavior-to-internal-state ratio in Thomas is not a constant. In the early years of caregiving — when the relationship between observable behavior and inner state was simpler — the ratio was roughly stable. Thomas showed what he felt. The rubric captured it. But decades of adaptation have shifted the ratio. Exhaustion presents as calm. Grief presents as routine. Desperation presents as competence. The ratio has drifted below the assumed value, just as the blood-to-breath ratio drops during absorption. The assessor's result, converted at the old ratio, underestimates what is actually circulating inside Thomas.

933. The Firmware

The 2100:1 ratio is not merely an instrument setting. It is written into statute. In North America, the law defines the conversion factor. In the UK, it is 2000:1. In Australia, 2300:1. In Austria, 2400:1. The ratio varies by jurisdiction because jurisdictions made different policy choices about where to set it. The variation itself proves it is not a physical constant — if it were, every country would use the same value. But because it is embedded in law, challenging the ratio in court means challenging the statute, not just the measurement. The legal framework treats a policy choice as a physical fact.

The assumed correspondence between Thomas's observable behavior and his internal state is embedded in the institutional framework — the training manuals, the accreditation standards, the documentation templates. The ratio is built into the form the assessor fills out. The form has fields for behavioral observations and outputs a score in internal-state units. There is no field for the ratio itself. There is no way to note that the conversion factor used for this particular caregiver is inappropriate. The form treats the conversion as settled. Challenging it means challenging the form, the training, the accreditation — the institutional firmware.

934. The Forty Percent

A person with a true blood-to-breath ratio of 1500:1 — at the low end of the absorptive-phase range — and a true BAC of 0.075 g/dL (below the legal limit) will produce a breath measurement that the instrument converts to approximately 0.105 g/dL. Over the limit. Convicted. The error is 40%. The error is not random — it is structural, determined by the person's physiology at the moment of testing. The person's true BAC was never measured. Only the proxy was measured, and the proxy was converted using a ratio that does not apply to this person at this moment.

Thomas, at a moment when his actual coping capacity is just above the threshold for intervention — enough to manage, barely — is assessed during a bad week. The assessor's conversion produces a score that suggests he is further from crisis than he actually is. The error can be 40% — not a rounding difference but a category difference, the difference between receiving help and not receiving it. The error is structural, determined by Thomas's adaptation state at the moment of testing. Thomas's true capacity was never measured. Only the proxy was measured, and the proxy was converted using a ratio that does not apply to this man at this moment.

935. The Conversion

The officer, the court, and the defendant never see the raw breath measurement. They see only the converted BAC. The output wears blood units. There is no footnote saying: this number was computed from a breath measurement by dividing by an assumed constant that varies by nearly two-fold across the population and that was never measured in this individual. The number is presented as a fact about the blood. It is a fact about the breath, transformed by an assumption that is sometimes accurate, sometimes 40% wrong, and never verified for the person standing at the side of the road.

The assessor's report and the facility's file never see Thomas's raw behavioral presentation stripped of interpretation. They see only the converted score. The output wears psychological units. There is no footnote saying: this number was computed from a behavioral observation by applying an assumed correspondence that varies enormously across caregivers and that was never calibrated for this individual. The number is presented as a fact about Thomas's internal state. It is a fact about Thomas's surface, transformed by an assumption about the relationship between surface and interior — an assumption that was roughly correct twenty years ago, that has since drifted by a factor the system does not track, and that was never verified for the man sitting in the assessment room, performing the last of his composure for a rubric that will mistake it for well-being.
