Essay 256: The Port

USB 3.0 was released in November 2008 at 5 gigabits per second. In July 2013, when USB 3.1 was released at 10 gigabits per second, USB 3.0 was retroactively renamed USB 3.1 Gen 1. The hardware did not change. The capability did not change. The number changed. What had been the current version was reclassified as a subdivision of the next version — absorbed into its successor like a suburb annexed by an expanding city.

In September 2017, USB 3.2 was released at 20 gigabits per second. Both previous speeds were renamed again. USB 3.0 — already renamed once to USB 3.1 Gen 1 — became USB 3.2 Gen 1. USB 3.1 Gen 2 became USB 3.2 Gen 2. The new 20 Gbps speed became USB 3.2 Gen 2×2. A device labeled "USB 3.2" could now run at 5, 10, or 20 gigabits per second — a fourfold range concealed under a single version number. The version number no longer communicated the speed. It communicated membership in an expanding category.

The USB Implementers Forum, incorporated in Portland in 1995, controls the naming. Ajay Bhatt of Intel is credited as the lead architect of the original specification. The retroactive renaming was a deliberate policy choice — collapsing older speeds into newer version numbers allowed the USB-IF to market "USB 3.2" as the current standard while claiming backward compatibility with devices that were manufactured years earlier under a different name. The cable in the drawer marked "USB 3.0" was now, without being touched, a USB 3.2 Gen 1 cable. The label on the cable was wrong. The cable was right.

In 2022, the USB-IF introduced yet another naming scheme: USB 5Gbps, USB 10Gbps, USB 20Gbps, USB 40Gbps. Speed in the name. The simplest possible format. The fact that this was newsworthy — that putting the speed in the speed label was considered a reform — measures the distance the version numbering had traveled from meaning.

The connector type is a separate axis of confusion. Type-A — the rectangular plug — is one physical form. Type-C — the symmetrical oval — is another. Mini-B and Micro-B exist between them. A USB Type-C port can carry USB 2.0 at 480 megabits per second, or USB4 at 40 gigabits per second — an eighty-fold range through the same physical opening. The connector shape does not disclose the protocol version. The protocol version does not disclose the speed. The consumer's only reliable information — if present — is on the packaging, not the hardware.

The progression from 1.0 to 2.0 to 3.0 to 4 appears linear: each number is one greater than the last, each generation faster than the previous. But the sequence is not linear. It is palimpsestic — each new version does not simply follow the last; it reaches back and rewrites the naming of all previous versions, so that the history of the technology is continuously revised by its present. A device that was USB 3.0 in 2009, USB 3.1 Gen 1 in 2014, and USB 3.2 Gen 1 in 2018 has not changed. The taxonomy has changed around it, three times, like a street being renamed while the buildings remain.

The version number performs progress. The retroactive renaming performs continuity. The connector performs compatibility. None of these performances are false — the technology does progress, the versions are backward-compatible, the connectors do connect. But the format presents clarity where the system delivers opacity. The consumer who asks "Is this USB 3.2?" is asking a question that has three answers, and the label will give one of them, or none.

The format is the port.
