Part CLVI: The Spike

Piece #1221: The Brass

A brass plate bolted to a cliff in Wales. Below the plate: Ordovician. Above the plate: Silurian. The transition happened 443 million years ago. The committee decided in 1985. The committee voted on the location, the marker species, the precise layer. The vote was not unanimous. The dissenters preferred a different section in a different country with a different marker. The majority chose Wales. Wales became the boundary. The boundary became the world. Every geologist, every textbook, every stratigraphic chart now agrees: the Silurian begins here. Not because the rock in Wales is more complete than elsewhere. Not because the graptolite species that marks the boundary was more abundant there. Because a committee chose this cliff, and the choice became infrastructure, and infrastructure became truth.

Piece #1222: The Name

Cambrian comes from Cambria, the Latin name for Wales. Devonian comes from Devon, England. Jurassic comes from the Jura Mountains. Silurian comes from the Silures, a Celtic tribe. Ordovician comes from the Ordovices, another Celtic tribe. Permian comes from the city of Perm, in Russia. The names encode a geography: where European geologists happened to be working in the 1830s and 1840s. Sedgwick named the Cambrian. Murchison named the Silurian. They fought for decades over the rocks in between. Lapworth resolved the dispute in 1879 by naming the Ordovician after a third tribe. The compromise became permanent. The names cannot be changed. Too many papers cite them, too many maps use them, too many textbooks print them. The geography of Victorian fieldwork is now the vocabulary of deep time.

Piece #1223: The Chart

The International Chronostratigraphic Chart is color-coded. Paleozoic: cool greens and blue-grays. Mesozoic: greens, blues, pale purples. Cenozoic: warm yellows and oranges, deepening to red at the present. The blocks are roughly the same visual size. The Cretaceous block and the Holocene block occupy comparable space on the chart. The Cretaceous lasted 79 million years. The Holocene has lasted 11,700. The Precambrian — 4 billion years, 88% of Earth's history — is compressed into a handful of large blocks at the bottom. The Phanerozoic — 538 million years, 12% — fills most of the chart. The visual grammar suggests equal weight. The durations differ by orders of magnitude. Resolution increases as time approaches the present. This is not because recent time is more eventful. This is because more rock survives. The chart maps preservation, not importance. But preservation is not what the viewer sees. The viewer sees: time, neatly divided.

Piece #1224: The Vote

March 2024. The ICS Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy votes 12-4 against ratifying the Anthropocene as a formal geological epoch. The proposed Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point was at Crawford Lake, Ontario — a small, deep, meromictic lake whose undisturbed sediments recorded a clear plutonium-239 spike beginning in 1952, from atmospheric nuclear testing. The signal was unambiguous. The human impact was not in dispute. The objection was procedural: the proposed boundary was too recent. The section was too short. The methods — radionuclide detection, microplastic identification — were unlike traditional biostratigraphy. The system was designed for deep time. Marine sequences. Invertebrate fossils. Boundaries visible in rock exposed by erosion over millions of years. A boundary within living memory, marked by a radionuclide in lake mud, did not fit the system. The system was not wrong. The system was not designed for this.

Piece #1225: The Hierarchy

Eon, era, period, epoch, age. The hierarchy appears natural — a nesting of progressively finer divisions, like kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. But the geological hierarchy was not designed from first principles. It grew by accretion. Geologists named rock sequences. Other geologists subdivided them. Still others combined them into larger groups. The hierarchy is a fossil of 19th-century disputes, compromises, and naming conventions. An age can be 2 million years. A period can be 79 million years. The hierarchy does not enforce consistent duration or consistent criteria for division. Some boundaries are mass extinctions. Some are first appearances of marker species. Some are isotope excursions. Some are lithostratigraphic transitions. The only requirement is that a committee voted and a GSSP was ratified. The hierarchy looks like a description of time. The hierarchy is a description of how geologists organized their disagreements.
