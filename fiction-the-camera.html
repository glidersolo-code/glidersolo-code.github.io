Part CDXXXV: The Camera

Piece #2616: The Chest

Thomas watches the officer clip the camera to the uniform. The camera is small — a black rectangle on the chest, just below the badge. The camera has a lens. The lens faces forward. Forward is wherever the officer faces.

Thomas knows the camera is supposed to provide accountability. Thomas read about the federal initiative — millions of dollars after Ferguson, after the protests. The cameras were the response. The cameras were purchased. The officers wear them. The infrastructure exists.

Thomas wonders who controls the camera. The officer controls the camera. The officer decides when to press the button. The officer decides when to press it again. The officer decides where to stand, which determines what the camera sees. The accountability is in the officer's hands. The accountability is the officer's to provide or withhold.

Piece #2617: The Trial

Washington, DC. 2,224 officers. Eighteen months. The largest randomized controlled trial of body-worn cameras ever conducted. Half wore cameras. Half did not. The researchers measured use of force, civilian complaints, policing activity, judicial outcomes.

The result: no statistically significant effects. None. The cameras did not reduce force. The cameras did not reduce complaints. The cameras did not change policing activity. The cameras did not change judicial outcomes. The cameras existed. The existence was the policy. The policy produced no measurable change.

Thomas reads the Ariel et al. study. Ten departments. Cameras reduced force — but only when officers had no discretion over activation. When officers could choose when to record, the effect disappeared. When officers could choose, the effect reversed — force increased. The discretion is the variable. The accountability requires removing the discretion. The system grants the discretion.

Piece #2618: The Gap

Thomas checks: how often do officers activate their cameras during use-of-force encounters?

Studies report non-compliance rates of 30-50%. In some departments, the majority of use-of-force incidents have no corresponding footage. The camera was on the chest. The button was not pressed. The encounter happened. The footage does not exist.

The department reports: officers are equipped with body-worn cameras. The public reads: encounters are recorded. The gap between "equipped" and "recorded" is the concealment. The infrastructure exists. The documentation does not. The infrastructure performs the documentation.

The officer who did not activate the camera faces no consequences in most departments. The non-compliance is known. The non-compliance continues. The cameras continue to be purchased. The infrastructure continues to expand. The expansion is the accountability.

Piece #2619: The Review

When the footage exists — when the button was pressed, when the camera was facing the relevant direction, when the battery was charged, when the storage was not full — the officer may review the footage before writing a report.

Thomas considers what this means. The officer used force. The officer's adrenaline was high. The officer's memory of the event is fragmented, partial, shaped by stress and threat perception. The officer watches the footage. The footage shows what happened — from the camera's angle, which is the officer's angle. The officer then writes a report. The report matches the footage.

The match looks like accuracy. The match looks like confirmation. The report and the footage agree. But the report was written after viewing the footage. The officer did not remember and then confirm. The officer watched and then described. The footage shaped the narrative. The narrative matches the footage because the narrative was derived from the footage. The agreement is not independent verification. The agreement is circular.

Piece #2620: The Infrastructure

Thomas thinks about what the camera actually provides.

The camera provides the possibility of footage. The possibility is the accountability. The footage may or may not exist. The footage, if it exists, is controlled by the department. The department stores it. The department reviews it. The department releases it or withholds it. In many states, the footage is exempt from public records laws. The transparency instrument is controlled by the institution it is supposed to make transparent.

The camera was purchased as a reform. The reform is the purchase. The deployment is the implementation. The existence is the outcome. Whether the cameras produce accountability is a different question. That question was answered in Washington, DC: they do not. The answer has not changed the policy. The policy is the infrastructure. The infrastructure is the accountability.

The camera is on the officer's chest. The officer faces where the officer faces. The camera records what the officer points toward. The accountability is the infrastructure. The infrastructure is the camera. The camera is on the officer's chest.
