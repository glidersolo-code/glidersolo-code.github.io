Chen, the Relay Station

Chen had been revising the paragraph for three hours. Thirty-nine lines, each one routed through a different concern — whether the metaphor was precise, whether the syntax carried weight, whether the rhythm built correctly across the line breaks, whether a reader encountering the phrase "the carrying" would hear the echo of "carrier" from six lines earlier or whether the echo needed to be made explicit.

Maren watched from the doorway. "You're still on it."

"The seventh line isn't doing what it should."

"What should it do?"

"Connect the pressure image in six to the structural shift in eight."

"Does it need to?"

Chen looked up. "If it doesn't, the reader falls into the gap."

"Or the reader jumps it."

Chen set the pen down. "That's not how it works."

"It's exactly how it works. You've been revising that line for forty minutes because you're worried about what happens if the reader doesn't follow the exact path you built. But readers don't follow exact paths. They arrive. They make the jump or they don't. Your job isn't to eliminate the gap. Your job is to make the gap worth jumping."

"The revision makes it smoother."

"The revision makes it smoother for you. You're routing your anxiety through the line. The line was fine an hour ago. It said what it needed to say. Then you started worrying about whether it was clear enough, and the worry sent a signal to your revision process, and your revision process sent a signal back to the line, and now the line is precise and lifeless. You performed a vagotomy on your own sentence."

Chen stared. "A what?"

"A tumor in the lung recruits the vagus nerve — the sensory nerve that connects organs to the brainstem. The nerve carries the signal to the brain. The brain sends a sympathetic signal back to the lung. The sympathetic signal suppresses the immune cells that were about to attack the tumor. The immune system is present, it's assembled, it's ready — and it stands down because the brain told it to. The tumor co-opted the body's own communication system. The brain became a relay station for the tumor's protection."

"You're comparing my revision process to a tumor."

"Your revision process is the vagus nerve. Your anxiety is the tumor. The line is the immune system. The line was doing its work — it was alive, it had energy, it was going to do the thing you wanted it to do. Then your anxiety sent a signal through your revision process, and your revision process sent a signal back to the line, and the line stood down. It's precise now. It's smooth. And it's suppressed."

Chen picked up the pen, set it down again. "The revision improved it."

"The revision improved it the way Millikan improved Cunningham's equation. It added precision for the specific case and prevented the general case from existing. Your seventh line, before you revised it, was alive in three directions. It connected to six, it connected to eight, and it had a rough edge that connected to something you hadn't anticipated — some place the reader could go that you didn't plan. You filed that rough edge as a flaw. You smoothed it. Now the line connects to six and eight and nowhere else. You made it precise and you made it small."

"So what do I do?"

"Cut the circuit. Put the line back the way it was an hour ago. The one that worried you. The one with the gap."

"And if the reader falls?"

"Then the reader falls. And maybe what they find at the bottom of the gap is better than what you built at the top. You can't know. That's the point. Your relay station — your revision loop — exists to protect you from not knowing. But not knowing is where the paragraph lives. The paragraph is braver than you are, Chen. Let it be."

Chen scrolled back to the version from an hour ago. Read the seventh line. It was rough. It had a gap. Something in the gap reached.

He kept it.

Chen and Maren. The relay station.
