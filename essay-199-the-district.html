The District

In 1964, the Supreme Court mandated that electoral districts contain roughly equal populations. "Legislators represent people, not trees or acres," Chief Justice Warren wrote in Reynolds v. Sims. The principle — one person, one vote — required that every district carry the same weight. The requirement was about fairness. What it did not specify was shape.

A district can be a square or a salamander. Both can contain 761,169 people — the average for a congressional district after the 2020 census. Both satisfy the constitutional requirement. But the square and the salamander will elect different representatives, because the shape determines which people are inside. The population is equal. The composition is not. The composition is the point.

The technique was named in 1812. Governor Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts signed a redistricting bill that gave his party 29 of 40 state senate seats on 49% of the vote. The Boston Gazette printed a cartoon: a winged salamander drawn from the outline of an Essex County district. The practice was older than the name. Two centuries later, the name is quaint and the practice is computational. Algorithms test millions of possible maps in minutes, optimizing for partisan advantage with a precision Gerry could not have imagined.

"Cracking" disperses opposition voters so they form a minority in every district. "Packing" concentrates them so they win a few seats overwhelmingly and are irrelevant elsewhere. Both techniques produce wasted votes — votes that do not contribute to electing a representative. After the 2020 redistricting cycle, the party controlling the process in 17 states secured 72% of available seats on 52% of the vote. The arithmetic is public. The method is documented. The map is available. What the map conceals is not itself but its authorship: who drew the lines, and why.

In 2019, the Supreme Court declared partisan gerrymandering nonjusticiable. Rucho v. Common Cause, 5-4: federal courts cannot review the practice because no "clear, manageable, and politically neutral" standard exists to separate legitimate redistricting from manipulation. The decision did not deny that gerrymandering occurs. It said that courts could not reliably distinguish it from ordinary politics.

This is the mechanism at its most complete. The requirement to redistrict is genuine — the Constitution demands it, population shifts necessitate it, equal protection requires it. But the requirement contains a choice: not whether to draw lines but where. The mandate is legitimate. The discretion within it is unreviewable. The legitimacy of the task transfers to all decisions made inside it. "We had to draw lines somewhere" absorbs "we drew them here." The obligation provides the alibi.

The voters choose the representative. The mapmaker chose the voters.
