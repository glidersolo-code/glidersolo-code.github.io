The Interview

The interview lasts one to four hours. Over sixty percent are conducted by telephone. The applicant sits in a detention facility. The asylum officer sits somewhere else. The interpreter is on another line. Only about one percent of applicants have a lawyer.

The question the interview is designed to answer: does this person have a credible fear of persecution? The question the interview actually answers: can this person produce a detailed, consistent, chronological narrative, in a language that may not be their own, through a telephonic interpreter of uncertain quality, while detained, without counsel, within twenty-four hours of arrival, about the worst thing that ever happened to them?

The REAL ID Act codified demeanor as a credibility factor. The officer may assess eye contact, emotional display, responsiveness, candor. In much of East Asia, parts of Africa, and indigenous communities throughout the Americas, avoiding eye contact with authority figures is a sign of respect. Many cultures value emotional restraint in formal settings. A flat affect during testimony — the officer reads fabrication. The culture reads composure. The trauma reads numbness. Three different explanations for the same observation. The format selects the first.

The deepest structure is the relationship between trauma and the format that assesses it. Every participant in a study of Kosovan and Bosnian refugees changed some responses between interviews. Those with higher PTSD scores were more inconsistent the longer they waited. Sixty-nine percent of asylum seekers in medico-legal evaluations received PTSD diagnoses. Individuals with PTSD had three-fold higher odds of memory issues.

The interview demands exactly what PTSD destroys: linear chronology, consistent detail, emotional congruence with content. Fragmented memory is a core trauma symptom. The interview treats fragmented memory as evidence against the claim that trauma occurred. The instrument constitutes the credibility it claims to assess. The person who presents as traumatized is judged not credible because they present as traumatized.

The geographic variation reveals the instrument. Immigration judge credible fear reviews ranged from one percent positive in Lumpkin, Georgia, to sixty percent in Arlington, Virginia. Within San Francisco, denial rates ranged from 1.3% to 91.6% across judges. A ninety-point spread. The same legal standard. The same statutory language. The location of the interview is the strongest predictor of the interview's outcome.

Pass rates shifted from eighty-three percent positive under one administration to fourteen percent at the review stage under another. No statutory change. No amendment. No new law. The same words — "significant possibility" — producing different outputs depending on who reads them, where, and under what institutional pressure.

The one-year filing deadline eliminates claims on procedural grounds. More than 15,000 applications involving over 21,000 refugees were rejected on deadline grounds that would otherwise have been granted on merit. The deadline punishes exactly the delays that trauma, lack of counsel, and lack of information predictably produce. The clock does not observe lateness. The clock constitutes the bar.

The interview does not observe credibility. The interview constitutes it — through a format that treats the symptoms of persecution as evidence against the claim of persecution, through a geographic lottery that makes the courthouse more important than the testimony, through a clock that converts the consequences of trauma into procedural failure. The instrument is inside the phenomenon. The person's credibility is partly what happened to them and partly what the format permits them to say about it. Nobody can tell which part is which.
