Part CDXXX: The Two Questions

Piece #2591: The Knowledge

Thomas hears voices. Thomas has heard voices since he was twenty-two. The voices are not his. The voices tell him things. Today the voices tell him to break the window of the pharmacy. The voices say the pharmacist has been poisoning the water supply. The voices are urgent.

Thomas knows the voices are not real. Thomas takes medication for the voices. Thomas has been in and out of treatment. Thomas knows, in the abstract, that breaking the window would be wrong. Thomas knows this the way a person standing at the edge of a cliff knows that stepping off would be fatal — the knowledge is present, the body is leaning.

Thomas breaks the window.

In the courtroom, the defense raises the insanity defense. The test is M'Naghten. Two questions:

Did Thomas know the nature and quality of the act? Yes. Thomas knew he was breaking a window. Thomas knew the window was glass. Thomas knew glass breaks.

Did Thomas know the act was wrong? Yes. Thomas knew breaking windows is illegal. Thomas knew the pharmacist had not actually poisoned the water. Thomas knew, in the abstract, that his belief was delusional. Thomas has told his psychiatrist, many times: "I know the voices aren't real. I just can't stop listening to them."

Thomas is guilty. Thomas knew. The knowledge is sufficient. The inability to act on the knowledge is not part of the test. The test does not ask: could Thomas stop? The test asks: did Thomas know? Thomas knew. The verdict follows from the knowledge. The knowledge follows from the test. The test follows from 1843.

Piece #2592: The Two Questions

Question one: Did the defendant know the nature and quality of the act?

This question can be answered. The defendant either knew what the act was (breaking a window, stabbing a person, setting a fire) or did not know (believed the window was a portal, the person was a demon, the fire was a purification). The question is factual. The answer is binary.

Question two: Did the defendant know the act was wrong?

This question can be answered. The defendant either knew the act was illegal, immoral, or contrary to law, or did not know. The question is factual. The answer is binary.

The questions not asked:

Could the defendant control the behavior despite knowing it was wrong? Could the defendant resist the compulsion, the hallucination, the delusion, the overwhelming impulse? Did the defendant's mental illness impair not the knowledge but the will? Was the defendant's brain capable of translating knowledge into action? Was the executive function intact? Was the frontal cortex able to inhibit the impulse that the temporal lobe generated?

These questions cannot be answered binarily. These questions involve degrees, gradients, probabilities, clinical judgments, competing expert opinions. These questions would make the trial longer, harder, more uncertain. These questions would sometimes produce acquittals.

The test excludes these questions. The test asks the two that can be answered. The two that can be answered produce verdicts. The verdicts look like they engaged with mental illness because two questions were asked. Two questions that excluded the mind.

Piece #2593: The Contraction

1843: M'Naghten acquitted. The government outraged. Cognitive-only test established.

1962: ALI Model Penal Code adds volitional prong. The test expands. The mind gets wider.

1981: Hinckley acquitted. The public outraged. Cognitive-only test restored (1984).

2006: Clark v. Arizona. The Supreme Court allows states to restrict the insanity defense further.

2020: Kahler v. Kansas. The Supreme Court holds that states need not provide an insanity defense at all.

Thomas draws the timeline. The pattern is clear. The standard expands when psychiatry advances. The standard contracts when a high-profile defendant is acquitted. The contraction is permanent. The expansion is provisional.

Each contraction removes a dimension of the mind from the test. Each removal makes the test easier to apply. Each easier application produces more convictions. Each conviction confirms that the test works. Each confirmation justifies the contraction.

The mind was wider in 1962 than in 1843. The mind was narrower in 1984 than in 1962. The mind is narrower still in 2020. The mind the law sees is shrinking. The mind the person has is not.

Piece #2594: The Four States

Kansas. Montana. Idaho. Utah.

Four states that have abolished the insanity defense entirely. In these states, a person with schizophrenia who commits an act while in the grip of a psychotic delusion can present evidence of mental illness only to negate the mens rea element — to argue that the illness prevented forming the specific intent the crime requires. If the crime requires only general intent (the intent to perform the act, not the intent to achieve a specific result), mental illness is irrelevant. The person is guilty.

Thomas lives in Kansas. Thomas broke the window. Thomas intended to break the window. The intent was general. The illness is irrelevant. Thomas is guilty. Thomas's schizophrenia, Thomas's command hallucinations, Thomas's years of treatment, Thomas's inability to silence the voices — none of this enters the verdict. The verdict is about the intent. The intent is about the act. The act is about the window. The window is broken. Thomas is guilty.

The Supreme Court, in Kahler, held that this does not violate the Constitution. Six justices. The dissent (Breyer, joined by Ginsburg and Sotomayor) wrote that the majority's holding "offends a foundational principle" — that the criminal law has always recognized that "insanity destroys the guilty mind."

The majority disagreed. The majority held that states have "broad discretion" to determine "the precise relationship between criminal culpability and mental illness." The relationship can be: none.

Piece #2595: The Mind

Thomas thinks about what the legal system means by "mind."

The legal mind knows things. The legal mind forms intentions. The legal mind chooses. The legal mind is a rational agent that receives information, processes it, and decides. The legal mind can be tested with two questions because the legal mind has two functions: knowing and choosing.

The psychiatric mind is different. The psychiatric mind hallucinates, compels, distorts, fragments, obsesses, dissociates, and sometimes obliterates the boundary between self and other. The psychiatric mind can know that an act is wrong and be unable to stop performing it. The psychiatric mind can know the voices are not real and be unable to stop hearing them. The psychiatric mind operates on multiple levels simultaneously, and the level the law tests — cognitive knowledge — may be intact while every other level is shattered.

The M'Naghten test tests the legal mind. The defendant has a psychiatric mind. The test cannot see the psychiatric mind because the test was not designed for it. The test was designed for a legal construct — the rational agent — that mental illness precisely disrupts. The test tests the thing that is not broken while ignoring the thing that is.

The two questions ask: is the rational agent intact? The answer may be yes. The rational agent may know what it did and know it was wrong. The answer is yes and the person is guilty and the mind — the actual mind, the psychiatric mind, the mind that hallucinated and compelled and fragmented — is not part of the verdict.

The test excludes the subject. The subject is the mind. The mind is excluded. The test is complete.
