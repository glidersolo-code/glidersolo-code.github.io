Part CCXXI: The Suction

Piece #1546: The Amp

Thomas reads the box. "12 AMP MOTOR." The number is printed large, in bold, the primary claim. He compares it to the other vacuum on the shelf: "10 AMP MOTOR." Twelve is more than ten. Thomas picks the twelve. But an amp is a unit of electrical current — the amount of electricity the motor draws from the wall, not the amount of dirt it pulls from the carpet. A twelve-amp motor drawing more current than a ten-amp motor is consuming more electricity. It may also be producing more suction. Or it may be producing the same suction less efficiently — converting more current into heat and noise rather than airflow. The amp rating measures what the motor takes from the outlet. It does not measure what the motor gives to the floor. Thomas chose the vacuum that consumes more electricity. He may or may not have chosen the vacuum that cleans better.

Piece #1547: The Airflow

What cleans carpet is airflow — the volume of air the vacuum moves across and through the carpet surface per unit time, measured in cubic feet per minute. Airflow carries the dirt. Without airflow, suction is a sealed chamber. Without suction, airflow is a fan. The two combine: suction lifts the carpet nap and unseats the particle, airflow carries the particle into the machine. The consumer needs both. The consumer sees neither. Cubic feet per minute does not appear on the box. Water lift — the vacuum's sealed suction, measured in inches of water column — does not appear on the box. Air watts — the metric that combines airflow and suction into a single performance number — does not appear on the box. Amps appears on the box because amps is a bigger number and because the consumer has learned that amps equals power and power equals cleaning and the chain of inferences is wrong at every link and right at the cash register.

Piece #1548: The Efficiency

Motor A: 12 amps, 60% efficient. Motor B: 10 amps, 80% efficient. Motor A converts 840 watts to suction and 560 to heat. Motor B converts 960 watts to suction and 240 to heat. Motor B produces more suction from less current. Motor B has fewer amps. Motor B is the better vacuum. Motor B loses the sale because ten is less than twelve and the consumer has no access to the efficiency and the label does not require it. The ASTM F558 standard exists for measuring vacuum cleaner suction performance. Consumer packaging does not reference it. The engineering measurement and the marketing measurement are different measurements of different properties reported in different units to different audiences for different purposes, and the consumer sees only the marketing measurement and has no reason to suspect that another measurement exists.

Piece #1549: The Revolution

Dyson changed the label. When James Dyson introduced cyclonic vacuum technology, the motor drew fewer amps than conventional vacuums because the cyclone maintained suction without a bag — no bag to clog, no progressive loss of airflow. Marketing by amps would have made Dyson look weaker. Dyson marketed by suction power instead — air watts, a metric that combined airflow and sealed suction into a number that described what the consumer actually received. The industry followed partially. "Suction power" appeared in advertisements. But amps never left the box, because the infrastructure of comparison — the consumer's learned heuristic that more amps means more power — was too entrenched. A vacuum with 200 air watts on the label and a vacuum with 12 amps on the label cannot be compared by the consumer at the shelf. The two numbers are incommensurable. The shelf displays a measurement competition where the units are not the same and the consumer has no conversion table.

Piece #1550: The Label

Thomas brings the twelve-amp vacuum home. He vacuums the living room. The carpet looks clean. It probably is clean. The twelve-amp motor probably does produce adequate suction for his carpet type and pile height and the amount of pet hair his cat deposits daily. The wrong metric led to a defensible choice by accident — the way a stopped clock is right twice a day. The problem is not that Thomas bought a bad vacuum. The problem is that Thomas had no way to buy a good one deliberately. The label gave him amps. Amps told him about the motor's appetite. The motor's appetite is not the vacuum's capability. The capability is in the airflow, the suction, the filtration, the brush roll agitation, the seal between nozzle and carpet. None of these appear on the box. The box says twelve amps because twelve is a number and numbers perform specification and the specification is what the consumer came to read.
