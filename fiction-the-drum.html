Part CCXXIV: The Drum

Piece #1561: The Volume

Thomas reads the washing machine spec: 5.0 cubic feet. The competitor: 4.5 cubic feet. He buys the 5.0 because bigger drum means fewer loads, means more clothes per wash, means time saved. But 5.0 cubic feet is the total internal volume of the drum — measured with water, filled to capacity. The consumer does not wash water. The consumer washes clothes, which are three-dimensional, compressible, and irregular. A drum that holds 5.0 cubic feet of water holds perhaps 3.0 cubic feet of loosely loaded clothes — the manufacturer's recommended fill level for optimal cleaning. The consumer who fills the drum because the spec says 5.0 overpacks by 40 percent. The clothes cannot agitate. The detergent cannot circulate. The machine that was bought for its capacity delivers its worst performance at its rated capacity.

Piece #1562: The Method

DOE 10 CFR Part 430, Subpart B, Appendix J — the federal test procedure for residential clothes washers — defines "capacity" as the volume of the drum in cubic feet, measured by filling with water. The measurement is standardized. The measurement is also unrelated to the consumer's experience. No test standard measures usable laundry capacity — the volume of clothes the machine can wash effectively. "Capacity" on the spec sheet and "capacity" in the consumer's kitchen refer to different volumes. The word is the same. The referent is different. The consumer reads the word and applies their referent. The manufacturer publishes the word and applies the test standard's referent. The gap between the two referents is approximately 40 percent.

Piece #1563: The Race

2010: the average top-load washer was 3.5 cubic feet. 2024: the average is 5.0. Drums have grown 43 percent in fifteen years. The growth is real — physically larger drums holding physically more water. But the usable fill ratio has not changed. A 5.0 cubic-foot drum optimally loaded holds the same fraction of its volume as a 3.5 did. The consumer benefits from the larger drum — more clothes per load. The consumer also believes they benefit more than they do, because 5.0 vs 3.5 sounds like a 43 percent improvement, while the usable-capacity improvement is the same 43 percent of a number the consumer never sees. The marketing race — Samsung 5.5, LG 5.8, Maytag 6.0 — pushes total drum volume upward because total drum volume is the number on the spec sheet and the consumer compares the numbers on the spec sheets and larger numbers win.

Piece #1564: The IEC

European machines use kilogram ratings. A "9 kg" washer holds 9 kilograms of dry clothes — a weight-based metric that describes what the consumer puts in, not what the drum contains. The same machine in the US might be marketed as 4.5 cubic feet. The European consumer knows how many kilograms of laundry they have. The American consumer does not know how many cubic feet of laundry they have. The kilogram rating is imperfect — fabric density matters, a load of denim weighs differently from a load of synthetics — but it measures the consumer's input. The cubic-foot rating measures the machine's container. One metric faces the laundry. The other faces the drum.

Piece #1565: The Load

Thomas loads the 5.0 cubic-foot drum. He puts in a comforter, two sheet sets, and a pile of towels. He read the spec. The drum is 5.0 cubic feet. The comforter alone is 3 cubic feet when loosely placed. He closes the lid on 6 cubic feet of laundry compressed into 5.0 cubic feet of drum. The washer runs. The comforter does not move. The water saturates the outside layers and does not reach the center. The detergent sits on top of the fabric, unable to circulate. After the cycle, Thomas pulls out a comforter that is wet on the outside and dry in the middle. The machine washed the perimeter of a load that exceeded the capacity it was never designed to hold, despite being labeled with a number that described the entire drum as if the entire drum were the capacity.
