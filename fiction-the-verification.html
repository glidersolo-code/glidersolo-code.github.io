Part CDXXVII: The Verification

Piece #2576: The Pathologist

Thomas is a pathologist. Thomas opens the body.

The chart says pneumonia. Thomas finds pulmonary embolism. The chart says cardiac arrest. Thomas finds a ruptured aortic aneurysm. The chart says metastatic cancer. Thomas finds an untreated infection that killed the patient before the cancer could.

Every case, Thomas compares. What the chart says. What the body says. The discrepancy between the two is the information the system cannot get any other way.

In 1960, Thomas opens 50% of the patients who die in the hospital. In 1980, Thomas opens 15%. In 2000, Thomas opens 5%. In 2020, Thomas opens nobody. The position has been eliminated. The department has been reduced. The budget has been reallocated to imaging.

Thomas's successor reads CT scans. The CT scans are sharp. The CT scans are detailed. The CT scans show everything they show. They do not show what they do not show. No one compares the scan to the body. No one compares the chart to the truth. The chart is the truth now. The chart has always been the truth now.

Piece #2577: The Number That Disappeared

In 1983, Goldman published the number: 10%. Approximately one in ten autopsied patients had a major missed diagnosis that, if known before death, might have changed therapy and prolonged survival.

The number was stable across 1960, 1970, and 1980. Three decades of diagnostic revolution — CT, MRI, echocardiography, endoscopy, comprehensive laboratory panels — and the number held at 10%.

In 2003, Shojania published the meta-analysis: 53 series, median Class I error rate 9%. Down slightly. Not dramatically. Not enough to justify stopping the checking.

Then the checking stopped. And the number disappeared.

Not because the number changed. Because the measurement changed. Fewer autopsies meant fewer discovered errors. Fewer discovered errors meant the quality metrics improved. The quality metrics improving meant the autopsy seemed less necessary. The autopsy becoming less necessary meant fewer autopsies.

Thomas stands at the whiteboard and draws the circle:

Fewer autopsies → fewer detected errors → perceived improvement → less need for autopsy → fewer autopsies

The circle is self-closing. Once entered, there is no exit that does not require someone to admit that the improvement was the withdrawal, that the accuracy was the absence of checking, that the confidence was the ignorance.

Piece #2578: The Regulation

42 CFR § 482.22(d): Medicare-participating hospitals must maintain an autopsy program and attempt autopsies in all cases of unusual deaths and of medical-legal and educational interest.

The regulation existed from the beginning of Medicare. It was the last institutional requirement connecting hospitals to post-mortem verification of their diagnoses.

On September 30, 2019, CMS removed it. Described the requirement as "obsolete or excessively burdensome."

Thomas reads the Federal Register notice. Reads the word "obsolete." The word means: no longer needed. The word means: the thing this regulation required is no longer necessary. The word means: hospitals no longer need to check whether their diagnoses were correct.

The College of American Pathologists objects. The American Medical Association objects. The American Society for Clinical Pathology objects.

The regulation is removed.

Thomas thinks about what "obsolete" means in a system where the verification mechanism has been declining for fifty years and the decline has been producing the appearance of improvement that the regulation was designed to prevent. The regulation was the last thread connecting the system to its own errors. The thread was cut. The errors remain. The system cannot see them. The system calls this "obsolete."

Piece #2579: The Cover-Up

George Lundberg called it "the ultimate cover-up in medicine."

Thomas turns the phrase over. Cover-up implies intent — someone deciding to hide something. But no one decided to hide the diagnostic errors. No individual administrator said: we should stop checking because the errors embarrass us. No hospital CEO said: if we stop looking, the numbers will improve.

The cover-up is structural. Each institutional decision — dropping the accreditation requirement, not reimbursing the procedure, reducing training minimums, removing the Medicare regulation — was justified on its own terms. Each was defensible. Each was reasonable. Each was an incremental reduction in the system's ability to see its own mistakes.

The sum of reasonable decisions is a system that cannot see a 10% Class I error rate. The sum of defensible choices is a database that says diagnostic accuracy has improved when the improvement is the absence of the instrument that would have shown otherwise.

Lundberg was right that it is a cover-up. But the cover-up has no author. The cover-up is the system. The system covers itself the way a body covers an organ — not by hiding it, but by being the thing that is seen instead. The hospital is seen. The imaging department is seen. The electronic health record is seen. The autopsy suite is not seen. It is not hidden. It has been removed. Removal is more thorough than hiding.

Piece #2580: The Death Certificate

The death certificate has four lines. Line a: immediate cause of death. Line b: due to, or as a consequence of. Line c: due to, or as a consequence of. Line d: underlying cause.

The physician fills in the lines. The physician has not opened the body. The physician has not compared the chart to the truth. The physician has the chart. The chart is the truth. The physician writes what the chart says.

Studies show errors in one-third to 92% of death certificates. Approximately 50% of acute myocardial infarctions are missed. Cancer deaths are underestimated by 20%. Suicide misclassification correlates with autopsy rate across 35 countries.

Thomas thinks about the database. The national mortality database. The CDC WONDER system. The epidemiological studies that determine which diseases receive research funding. The public health priorities that determine which interventions are deployed. All built on death certificates. All built on documents filled out by physicians who did not open the body.

The database says what the certificates say. The certificates say what the charts say. The charts say what the clinicians believed. The clinicians believed what the imaging showed. The imaging showed what it could show. What it could not show was never compared to the body. The body was not opened. The body was buried with its truth inside it.

The database is confident. The database is comprehensive. The database is wrong approximately one-third of the time. No one checks the database against the bodies. The bodies are underground. The truth is underground. The database is above ground, in servers, in papers, in policies. The database is the truth now.
