The Comparison

The average human attention span is now 8 seconds — shorter than a goldfish at 9 seconds. The statistic appeared in a 2015 report from Microsoft Canada's marketing division. The report cited a website called Statistic Brain. When the BBC traced the citation in 2017, Statistic Brain listed the National Center for Biotechnology Information and the Associated Press as sources. Neither organization had any record of the research. The Microsoft report's own research section never mentions "8 seconds" or "goldfish." The number was in the introduction, copied from an uncitable website, attributed to organizations that never produced it.

The goldfish number is also fabricated. No published study measures a goldfish's "attention span" at 9 seconds or any other duration. Goldfish have been trained to press levers, navigate mazes, and remember spatial layouts for months. The comparison — humans worse than fish — was assembled from two fabricated numbers, neither of which has a source.

But the comparison was too vivid to check. TIME ran it. The Telegraph ran it. Forbes ran it. The Guardian ran it. Hundreds of marketing blogs ran it. The comparison's vividness was its credibility: who would fabricate something so specific and so humiliating? The implausibility of the fabrication was itself the evidence.

"Attention span" is not a single metric. Attention comprises multiple subsystems — executive, sustained, selective, transient — each with different durations, contexts, and neural substrates. A person does not have an "attention span" the way a room has a temperature. Bradbury (2016) reviewed the literature on declining attention in lectures and found that the most-cited claim could not be traced to a credible primary study.

The comparison forecloses the definition. "What is attention span?" is replaced by "is ours shorter than a goldfish's?" The second question is more interesting, more alarming, more shareable. The comparison creates a decline narrative: we're getting worse. An animal comparison: we're dumber than fish. A technological diagnosis: smartphones are the cause. All three are fabricated. No decline was measured. No goldfish study exists. No causal mechanism was established.

The statistic was created by marketers for marketers. The "attention crisis" justifies the attention economy. If attention is scarce, it must be captured. If it's declining, it must be managed. If it's inferior to a fish, it must be engineered. The fabricated metric created the crisis that created the market that sells the solution: shorter content, faster cuts, better hooks. The comparison is marketing's origin myth — the story the attention industry tells about why attention needs to be bought and sold.

The goldfish doesn't have a 9-second attention span. The goldfish wasn't measured. The human wasn't measured. The comparison was never between two animals. It was between a fabricated statistic and a fabricated benchmark, and the comparison was so interesting that no one asked what either number measured.
