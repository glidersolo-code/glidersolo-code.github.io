Part CDLXVII: The Turnout

Piece #2776: The Three Denominators

Thomas is thinking about a fraction. Votes cast divided by — what? The answer determines the turnout rate. The answer determines the narrative. The answer determines the international ranking. Three denominators are available. Each produces a different number. Each answers a different question.

The first: Voting-Age Population. Every person in the United States aged 18 and older. In 2020, approximately 257 million people. This number includes 22 million non-citizens who cannot legally vote. It includes felons in states with disenfranchisement laws. It includes permanent residents who have lived in the country for decades, raised children, paid taxes, and cannot cast a ballot. The denominator does not distinguish between those who chose not to vote and those who were prohibited from voting. The rate treats legal prohibition and personal choice as the same absence.

The second: Voting-Eligible Population. McDonald and Popkin, 2001. Subtract non-citizens. Subtract disenfranchised felons. The denominator shrinks. The rate rises. In 2004: VEP turnout was 60.3%. VAP turnout was 55.3%. Five percentage points. The same number of ballots. The same election. Different fractions. Different narratives.

The third: Registered voters. Of the approximately 70% of eligible Americans who register, approximately 87% vote. Sweden reports 87% turnout. So does the United States — among registered voters. The gap between the US and Sweden is not in the voting. The gap is in the registering. And registration is a system feature, not a participation choice: Sweden registers citizens automatically.

Piece #2777: The Myth

Thomas is reading McDonald and Popkin's article. "The Myth of the Vanishing Voter." American Political Science Review, 2001. The thesis: the apparent decline in voter turnout since 1972 was not a decline in participation. It was an inflation in the denominator.

The 26th Amendment lowered the voting age to 18 in 1971. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 opened immigration from non-European countries. Between 1972 and 2004, the non-citizen share of the voting-age population rose from less than 2% to approximately 8.5%. Each year, more people entered the denominator who could not enter the numerator. The fraction fell. The headline said: Americans are losing interest in democracy.

McDonald and Popkin demonstrated that using VEP instead of VAP, the decline disappeared. The eligible population was voting at roughly the same rate it had since the 1960s. The vanishing voter was an artifact of the denominator. The denominator was growing. The story was wrong.

Thomas considers what it means for a narrative to be mathematically correct and structurally misleading. The VAP fraction did decline. The math is right. The conclusion — that fewer eligible people are choosing to vote — is wrong. The math tells one story. The denominator tells another. The denominator's story is not in the math.

Piece #2778: The Registration Gap

Thomas is thinking about registration. In Sweden, voter registration is automatic — the tax authority maintains the electoral roll. Every citizen turning 18 is added without action. In Australia, registration is compulsory. In Germany, municipalities register residents using population data. In the United States, registration requires an individual act — filling out a form, visiting a website, appearing at a government office by a deadline.

The gap this creates: approximately 30% of eligible Americans are not registered. Among those who are registered, approximately 87% vote. The registration barrier reduces the turnout rate before anyone makes a decision about voting. It is a system feature treated as a participation failure.

Thomas recognizes the structure. The international comparison — US 55%, Sweden 87% — collapses three distinct questions into one number. What fraction of adults are eligible? What fraction of eligible adults register? What fraction of registered voters vote? Sweden merges the first two questions through automatic registration. The US separates them. The comparison penalizes the US for having a system that requires a step other countries have eliminated. The penalty appears in the turnout rate as though it were a measure of civic will.

Piece #2779: The Headline

Thomas is thinking about how the number reaches the reader. "US voter turnout lags behind most developed nations." The headline does not say which denominator was used. The story does not explain that three denominators exist. The reader receives a number — 55% or 60% or 87% — and interprets it as a measure of how much Americans care about democracy.

Thomas counts the concealments. The denominator includes people who cannot vote. The decline since 1972 reflects denominator inflation, not participation decline. The international comparison uses different denominator systems. The registration barrier is a system feature, not a behavioral choice. None of these appear in the headline. All of them are in the number.

The number 55% answers the question: what fraction of all adults voted? The question most people are asking is: what fraction of people who could vote, did? The answer to that question — approximately 60% using VEP, approximately 87% using registered voters — is higher. The answer is higher because the question is different. The headline uses the lower number. The lower number answers the wrong question.

Piece #2780: The Three Stories

Thomas writes the three numbers on the whiteboard.

55%. VAP. The story: Americans don't care. 60%. VEP. The story: Americans care about as much as they always have. 87%. Registered. The story: Americans who enter the system participate at European levels.

Three numbers. Same election. Same ballots. Same democracy. Three stories. The denominator is doing work. The denominator determines whether the story is about apathy, stability, or engagement. The denominator determines the ranking. The denominator determines the policy response — whether the remedy is inspirational campaigns to motivate voters, structural reforms to register them, or nothing at all.

Thomas thinks about the infant mortality rate. Same structure. The definition of "live birth" determines the denominator. The denominator determines the rate. The rate determines the ranking. The ranking is published without the definition. Here: the choice of denominator determines the turnout rate. The rate determines the ranking. The ranking is published without the choice.

The format — deaths per thousand live births, votes per hundred adults — performs as a ratio. Ratios are objective. Ratios are arithmetically transparent. Numerator, denominator, divide. But the denominator is a decision. Who counts? The decision is made before the division. The division cannot show the decision. The decision is the measurement.
