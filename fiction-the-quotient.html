Part CXXIII: The Quotient

Piece #1056: The Scale

Thomas was tested.

The test measured something called intelligence. The test produced a number. The number was called an intelligence quotient — IQ — and it was expressed on a scale with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15. The scale was normal: a bell curve, symmetric, the same proportion of people at each distance from the mean. The normal distribution was a property of the scale, not a discovery about intelligence. The test was scored so that the results would be normal. The normality was an assumption, not a finding.

Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon designed the original test in Paris in 1905. The French Ministry of Education had asked them to identify children who needed additional educational support — children who were not keeping pace with their peers in the standard curriculum. The test was a triage tool. It separated children into two groups: those who could be served by the standard system and those who needed something different. The test was not designed to rank intelligence. It was not designed to measure a fixed trait. It was designed to answer a practical question about educational placement.

Binet himself warned against reification. The score, he wrote, did not measure a fixed, innate quality. It measured performance on a set of tasks at a particular moment. The warning was published. The test was exported.

Thomas was tested with a descendant of Binet's test. The test produced a number. The number was filed in his chart. The chart used the number to make decisions about his educational placement, his therapeutic services, his developmental prognosis. The number carried a decimal-point precision that suggested a measurement of something real, something fixed, something that existed in Thomas independently of the test that elicited it.

Piece #1057: The Quotient

Lewis Terman brought the test to America.

At Stanford University, in 1916, Terman published the Stanford-Binet — an English adaptation of Binet's scale, recalibrated on American children, and reorganized around a formula: IQ = (mental age / chronological age) × 100. A child whose mental age matched his chronological age scored 100. A child whose mental age exceeded his chronological age scored above 100. The quotient was elegant. It was also a ratio, and ratios work poorly at the extremes — a 4-year-old performing at a 6-year-old level and a 14-year-old performing at a 16-year-old level produce different IQs despite the same absolute gap.

David Wechsler, at Bellevue Hospital in New York, replaced the ratio with the deviation IQ in 1939. The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale — WAIS — set the mean at 100 and the standard deviation at 15, not by discovering these values in the data but by constructing the scoring algorithm to produce them. The raw scores were converted to scaled scores by a statistical transformation that forced the distribution to be normal. The bell curve was an artifact of the scoring procedure, not a property of the trait being measured.

Thomas's IQ was a deviation score. It told his clinicians how many standard deviations his performance fell from the mean of his norming sample. The mean was 100 by construction. The standard deviation was 15 by construction. The normal distribution was by construction. The construction was invisible in the number, which appeared in his chart as a measurement of something that existed in Thomas, not as a product of a statistical procedure applied to his performance on a set of tasks designed in New York in 1939.

Piece #1058: The Drift

The scores drifted upward.

James Flynn, a political scientist at the University of Otago in New Zealand, discovered in 1984 that raw IQ scores had been rising across all industrialized nations at approximately 3 points per decade since testing began. The phenomenon — the Flynn Effect — meant that a person scoring 100 on a test normed in 1970 would score approximately 109 on a test normed in 1940. Same person. Same performance. Different score, because the norming sample had changed.

The tests were renormed every 15 to 20 years. Each renorming reset the mean to 100. The resetting was the correction — the leap second of intelligence testing. It maintained the fiction that 100 meant the same thing across time by adjusting the scoring to absorb the drift. A score of 100 in 1940, 1970, and 2000 represented three different levels of raw performance. The number was the same. What it measured was not.

Thomas's IQ was normed in a particular year, on a particular population, using a particular edition of the test. If his raw performance had been scored on the previous edition's norms, his IQ would have been higher. If scored on the next edition's norms, lower. The number in his chart carried no timestamp. It did not record which norms produced it. It appeared as a fixed measurement of a fixed trait, and neither the measurement nor the trait was fixed.

The drift raised questions that the renorming procedure was designed to suppress. If raw scores rise 3 points per decade, what is rising? Not intelligence, said some researchers — the tests measure test-taking ability, familiarity with abstract reasoning, nutritional improvements, educational exposure. Intelligence, said others — the gains are real and reflect genuine cognitive improvement. The argument was about what the test measured. The test continued to produce numbers. The numbers continued to be filed in charts. The charts continued to make decisions.

Piece #1059: The Curve

The normal distribution was assumed.

Not discovered — assumed. Wechsler's deviation IQ forced the scores into a normal distribution by construction: the raw-to-scaled score conversion was designed to produce a bell curve with mean 100 and SD 15. If the raw data were skewed, the conversion corrected the skew. If the raw data were bimodal, the conversion smoothed the modes. The resulting distribution was always normal because the procedure guaranteed it.

The assumption had consequences. A normal distribution has specific properties: 68% within one SD, 95% within two, 99.7% within three. These proportions were treated as facts about intelligence — 2.3% of the population is "intellectually disabled" (below 70), 2.3% is "gifted" (above 130). But these percentages were products of the assumed distribution, not discoveries about the population. If the actual distribution of cognitive ability were not normal — if it were skewed, or platykurtic, or multimodal — the percentages would be different. The assumption determined the finding. The finding confirmed the assumption. The circularity was statistical.

Thomas was on the curve. His position on the curve determined his classification — his eligibility for services, his educational placement, his prognosis. The classification was precise: a score of 69 qualified for one set of services; a score of 70 did not. The threshold was drawn on a curve that was drawn by a procedure that was designed to produce the curve on which the threshold was drawn.

Piece #1060: The Quotient

What was measured?

The test presented tasks. The tasks elicited performance. The performance was scored. The scores were converted. The converted scores were summed. The sum was transformed. The transformation produced a number. The number was called an intelligence quotient and was filed in a chart and used to make decisions about a person's education, treatment, and prognosis.

At each step — task selection, performance elicitation, scoring rubric, conversion algorithm, summation method, transformation procedure — a decision was made. Each decision was made by a committee, or a test designer, or a statistician, or a norming study. Each decision shaped the final number. The final number appeared in the chart as a property of the person, not as a product of the decisions.

Thomas's IQ was the product of decisions — Binet's task selection, Terman's ratio, Wechsler's deviation scoring, Flynn's drift, the norming sample's demographics, the examiner's administration, the scoring manual's rubrics. The number was precise. The precision was the product of the procedure. The procedure was invisible in the number.

Binet's warning was in the 1905 publication. The score did not measure a fixed quality. The warning was true. The test was exported. The quotient was calculated. The chart was updated. The number was filed. The decisions followed.

Thomas was a quotient — a ratio of his performance to a norm, expressed as a deviation from a mean that was set by construction, on a curve that was assumed by procedure, using a test that was designed for triage and adopted as a measurement of a trait that its designer said it did not measure. The distance between Thomas and his quotient was 1060 pieces long and still being measured by the instrument that could not measure the distance.
