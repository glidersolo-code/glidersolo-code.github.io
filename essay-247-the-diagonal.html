Essay 247: The Diagonal

The television says fifty-five inches. The consumer reads size. The number is a length — not the width of the screen, not the height, but the diagonal, corner to corner, the longest possible straight line across the rectangle. No other consumer product is sold by its diagonal. A table is forty-eight by thirty inches. A window is thirty-six by twenty-four. A rug is eight by ten feet. The television is fifty-five inches, and the fifty-five describes a line no one looks along.

The convention dates to cathode-ray tubes. The CRT was a glass envelope — a funnel-shaped vacuum tube with a phosphor-coated face. The tube was round or nearly so, and the diagonal of the visible rectangle inscribed within it corresponded roughly to the tube's diameter. Manufacturers specified the tube by its diameter because that was the manufacturing dimension — the size of the glass funnel they cast. When the tube was mounted in a cabinet, the bezel covered the curved edges. The advertised size was the tube diagonal. The viewable size was smaller. A nineteen-inch monitor might show seventeen inches of image. The number on the box described the glass, not the picture.

The Federal Trade Commission intervened in the mid-1990s, requiring computer monitor manufacturers to disclose viewable image size alongside the advertised diagonal. The distinction between tube size and viewable size was sometimes two inches — a difference the consumer could not see until the box was opened. The regulation addressed the discrepancy without changing the convention. Monitors were still sold by diagonal. The correction was a footnote.

Flat panels eliminated the bezel problem. An LCD's diagonal is its viewable diagonal — no hidden glass, no overscan cropping. But the convention survived the transition, carrying forward from a technology where it described a manufacturing dimension to a technology where it describes a geometric abstraction. And it carries forward across a change that makes the number less informative than before: the aspect ratio shift from four-by-three to sixteen-by-nine.

The arithmetic is direct. For a rectangle with diagonal d and aspect ratio w:h, the area equals d-squared times the product of w and h divided by the sum of their squares. A fifty-five-inch screen at sixteen-by-nine has an area of approximately 1,293 square inches. A fifty-five-inch screen at four-by-three has an area of approximately 1,452 square inches. The widescreen television has twelve percent less viewing area than the older shape at the same diagonal. A consumer who replaced a thirty-two-inch standard television with a thirty-two-inch widescreen got a number that stayed the same and a picture that shrank.

The diagonal conceals which dimension changed. Widescreen is wider and shorter. The width grew; the height contracted. For television — where humans and landscapes are typically wider than they are tall — this is arguably better. For documents, spreadsheets, code — where content flows vertically — the lost height matters. A twenty-seven-inch sixteen-by-nine monitor stands 13.2 inches tall. A twenty-seven-inch four-by-three monitor stood 16.2 inches tall. Three inches of vertical space disappeared behind the same number.

Ultrawide monitors extend the principle. A thirty-four-inch ultrawide at twenty-one-by-nine has less area than a thirty-four-inch standard widescreen at sixteen-by-nine. The diagonal grows or stays constant; the shape stretches; the total viewing area shifts in ways the single number cannot express. The consumer compares diagonals across aspect ratios as though comparing sizes. The comparison is between lines, not surfaces. Two rulers laid corner to corner, measuring nothing the eye actually uses.

No one has proposed selling televisions by area. The units exist — square inches, square centimeters. The calculation is trivial. But area is a two-dimensional quantity, and two-dimensional quantities resist the single-number format that marketing requires. Fifty-five inches fits in a headline. Twelve hundred and ninety-three square inches does not. The diagonal persists because it is one number, and one number is what a spec sheet, a price tag, and a product comparison demand. The format selects the measurement. The measurement that survives is the one that fits the format.

A length measured along a line no one looks along, inherited from a technology that no longer exists, applied across aspect ratios it cannot distinguish, persisting because it is one number in a world that prints one number. The format is the diagonal.
