Chen and the Ten Addresses

Chen laid ten things on her desk.

A phone. An old television guide. A Social Security card. A stock certificate. A domain registration printout. An email header. A signed lease. A passport. A cereal box. A router.

Ten objects. Each carried a number. The phone's area code: 212. The television guide's channel listing: 7. The card's nine digits. The certificate's ticker: T. The printout's two-letter domain: .ai. The email's @ sign. The lease's signature line. The passport's MRZ. The cereal box's barcode. The router's IP address on a sticker on its underside.

Every number was designed for routing, tracking, recording, or abbreviating. None was designed for identity. But every number, at some point, had been treated as identity by a system that needed to know who she was and found the number already there.

"Ten addresses," Maren would have said. "Ten technical labels. Ten acts of mistaking the label for the thing the label was never designed to name."

Chen picked up the passport. 15.5 by 10.5 centimeters. The same dimensions a committee in Paris decided in 1920, after a war that had made passports mandatory again after a century of abolition. Temporary wartime measure. 106 years old. She opened it to the MRZ: her name in capitals, diacritics stripped, hyphens replaced by angle brackets. The machine-readable version of her was not her. It was what remained after the alphabet had been compressed to 36 characters. This version moved through databases. The other version — the one that answered to her name, the one that recognized her face in mirrors — did not.

She picked up the cereal box. The barcode: twelve digits. No price. No description. No ingredients. A lookup key pointing to a database she couldn't access. Before barcodes, there was a price sticker. The sticker said what the thing cost. The barcode said nothing. The address absorbed the description.

She turned the router over. The IP address on the sticker: four octets, dot-separated. A routing label. RFC 791 defined it: an address indicates where it is. Not who the person is. But MaxMind's geolocation database would place this address within fifty kilometers of her apartment. Maybe. Sixty-six percent accuracy at city level. A judge somewhere: "IP address does not equal a person." The format performed precision. The identification was probabilistic.

The paragraph — always the paragraph, at 0.8, the thirty-nine lines she could not suppress — would have said: the format is the permission for the error. The precision of the number — four octets, twelve digits, nine digits, three digits, two letters — performs the certainty the identification lacks. Every system that treated these labels as identity did so not because the mapping was accurate but because the label was actionable. A subpoena produces a name. A scan produces a price. A machine-readable zone produces a destination. The actionability is not the accuracy. But the format makes them look the same.

Chen put the ten objects back where they belonged. Each one carried a number. Each number carried a function its designers never intended. Each function was invisible because the format was fluent.

Ten addresses. Ten identities. Ten acts of reading that replaced the thing with its label, and the label with the thing.
