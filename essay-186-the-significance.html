Essay 186: The Significance

Fisher wrote "convenient."

In Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925), Ronald Fisher proposed that a probability of one in twenty — 0.05 — was a convenient level at which to regard an experimental result as worth investigating further. Not proof. Not truth. A methodological heuristic for the working researcher deciding where to direct attention. The word "convenient" meant: useful, practical, a reasonable place to draw a line when the alternative was drawing no line at all.

The suggestion became a threshold. The threshold became a requirement. Journal editors demanded p < 0.05 for publication. Funding agencies used significance as a criterion for evaluating proposals. Regulatory bodies required it for drug approval. At each stage of institutionalization, the "convenient" became mandatory, the heuristic became the rule, and the original framing — this is a tool, use judgment — was overwritten by the operational demand: produce the number, cross the line.

The p-value itself is a probability about data, not about hypotheses. P(data | H₀): the probability of observing results this extreme given that the null hypothesis is true. This is what the calculation produces. What researchers want is P(H₀ | data): the probability that the null hypothesis is true given the observed data. These are not the same quantity. They are not even the same kind of quantity. The transposition — treating one as the other — is the base rate fallacy, the confusion of the inverse, a logical error taught in introductory courses and committed in the majority of published research.

The teaching and the committing coexist. The error is not ignorance. The infrastructure rewards the transposition: a "significant" p-value is a publishable p-value, and "significant" in English means important, meaningful, real. The technical term and the common word share a spelling. Every research paper that reports "a significant effect" is simultaneously making a narrow statistical claim and a broad semantic one. The narrow claim is often misunderstood. The broad claim is always heard.

The binary deepens the concealment. A continuous probability is cut at 0.05, and the two sides of the cut are treated as categorically different. A p-value of 0.049 is significant; 0.051 is not. The difference — two thousandths of a probability — is the difference between publication and file drawer, between treatment and abandonment, between "the effect is real" and "the study found nothing." The information destroyed by this binary — that p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 describe essentially identical evidence — is unrecoverable within the framework that created the cut.

Publication bias operates on the binary. Studies with p < 0.05 are published. Studies with p ≥ 0.05 are not. The published literature is therefore enriched for significant results, which means enriched for the proportion of those results that are false positives. Ioannidis (2005) demonstrated mathematically: when prior probabilities are low, studies are underpowered, and publication selects for significance, the majority of significant published findings will be wrong. The filter that selects what enters the evidence base systematically selects for error.

The American Statistical Association waited 177 years before issuing its first statement on statistical practice. In 2016, Wasserstein and Lazar published the ASA's formal position: "P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone." The statement was necessary because the correct interpretation had been taught for a century without correcting the misinterpretation. The infrastructure was stronger than the pedagogy. The institutional demand for the number outweighed the disciplinary knowledge of what the number meant.

The convention hardened into nature. Not through deception — through repetition. Not through conspiracy — through convenience. Fisher's "convenient" became the world's mandatory, and the distance between those two words is the width of the concealment: an arbitrary methodological suggestion, useful in context, treated as a law of nature because the systems built on top of it could not function without treating it as one. The significance is statistical. The world is not.
