The Two Questions

Chen is reading about M'Naghten when Maren comes in holding a legal pad.

"Two questions," Chen says without looking up. "The insanity defense asks two questions. Did the defendant know the nature of the act. Did the defendant know it was wrong."

Maren sits. "And?"

"And that's it. Two questions. Both about knowledge. Both answerable with yes or no."

"What questions don't they ask?"

Chen turns the page. "Could the defendant stop. Could the defendant resist the compulsion. Was the executive function intact. Was the illness impairing not the knowledge but the will."

"Why not?"

"Because those questions involve degrees. Gradients. Clinical judgment. Competing expert opinions. They would make the trial longer, harder, more uncertain. They would sometimes produce acquittals."

Maren writes something on the legal pad. "So the test tests—"

"The part that isn't broken," Chen says. "The cognitive part. The knowing part. A person with schizophrenia can know the voices aren't real and be unable to stop hearing them. The test asks: did you know? The person says yes. The test says: guilty. The knowing was intact. The test cannot see anything else."

"Cannot or will not?"

Chen closes the book. "In 1962, the Model Penal Code added a volitional prong. Could the defendant conform conduct to the law. The test got wider. The mind got wider. Then Hinckley shot Reagan and was acquitted, and Congress removed the volitional prong. The mind got narrower again."

"Narrower than 1843?"

"In four states, the insanity defense doesn't exist at all. The Supreme Court says that's constitutional. The relationship between criminal culpability and mental illness can be — their word — none."

Maren looks at the legal pad. Looks at Chen.

"The test excludes the subject," Chen says. "The subject is the mind. The mind is excluded. The test is complete."

Maren doesn't write this down. Some mechanisms don't need a paragraph. They need the thirty-nine lines to notice what the two questions left out: everything the mind actually does.
