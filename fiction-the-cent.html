Part CCCLXXXV: The Cent

Piece #2366: The Fugio

Thomas found the first one. 1787. The Fugio cent — the first official circulation coin of the United States. A sundial with the sun above it. The word "FUGIO" — Latin for "I flee" — referring to time. The motto below: "Mind Your Business." Designed, by some accounts, by Benjamin Franklin.

Ten grams of copper. It could buy something. Adjusted for inflation, that one cent is worth thirty-seven modern cents. The denomination has lost ninety-seven percent of its purchasing power. Almost nothing in the American economy can be purchased for one cent.

Thomas noted: the coin's motto was "Mind Your Business." The business, by 2024, was zinc.

Piece #2367: The Loss

Thomas found the numbers. In fiscal year 2024, the US Mint produced 3.225 billion pennies at a cost of 3.69 cents per penny. The annual loss to taxpayers: $85.3 million. The nineteenth consecutive year in which the coin cost more to produce than it was worth.

In 1982, the Mint changed the composition from 95% copper to copper-plated zinc. The zinc made the coin cheaper. The coin still cost more than one cent. Since 2006, cumulative losses exceeded one billion dollars.

Approximately sixty to seventy percent of pennies produced each year exited active circulation. Dropped into jars. Lost in cushions. Thrown in fountains. Abandoned in parking lots. The Mint produced billions of replacements for coins that functioned as clutter.

Thomas held the structure: a government manufactured, at a loss, billions of objects that immediately disappeared.

Piece #2368: The Lobby

Thomas found the industry. Jarden Zinc Products of Greeneville, Tennessee — sole supplier of zinc penny blanks to the US Mint. A $48 million government contract. The company funded Americans for Common Cents, a nonprofit lobbying group dedicated to preserving the penny.

In 2012, the organization's executive director was paid $340,000 by Jarden to discuss penny issues with members of Congress and the Mint. The zinc industry supplied the material. It funded the advocacy. It framed the preservation of a money-losing coin as defense of American tradition.

Representative Kolbe introduced bills to eliminate the penny in 2001 and 2006. Senator Flake introduced one in 2017. All failed. Polls showed 55 to 67 percent of Americans supported keeping the penny. The sentiment was real. The sentiment was also cultivated by a company whose revenue depended on the coin's existence.

Piece #2369: The Precedent

Thomas gathered the international evidence. Canada: stopped producing its penny in May 2012, ceased distribution February 4, 2013. Each Canadian penny cost 1.6 cents to make. Cash transactions rounded to nearest five cents. Electronic transactions: exact to the cent. No measurable increase in consumer prices.

Australia: eliminated 1- and 2-cent coins in 1992. New Zealand: eliminated 1- and 2-cent in 1990, 5-cent in 2006. Finland, Ireland, Belgium, Netherlands: do not circulate 1- and 2-cent euro coins.

In every case, elimination produced no significant economic harm. Rounding was symmetric. Prices did not increase. The evidence was available. The evidence had been available for years.

Thomas noted: the evidence was irrelevant. The penny survived on sentiment, not evidence. Evidence could not defeat sentiment. Something else would have to.

Piece #2370: The Fiat

Thomas found the ending. February 10, 2025. President Trump announced from Air Force One, after the Super Bowl, that he had instructed the Treasury Department to halt penny production. "Wasteful spending." DOGE had highlighted the cost the day after inauguration.

In May 2025, the Treasury confirmed: final order of penny blanks placed. Production would cease when inventory was exhausted. Estimated annual savings: $56 million.

Thomas held the structural finding. The penny survived democratic deliberation — every Congressional bill failed. Polls supported it. The zinc lobby defended it. For two decades, the democratic process preserved a coin that cost more to make than it was worth, that lost 97% of its value, that disappeared by the billions into jars and fountains. Democracy could not eliminate it because sentiment was stronger than evidence.

Executive fiat could. The sentiment was not defeated — polls still showed majority support when the penny was killed. The sentiment was bypassed. Authority did what deliberation could not. The artifact that sentiment preserved against reason was eliminated by power against sentiment. The coin's motto, from 1787, had been "Mind Your Business." In 2025, someone finally did.
