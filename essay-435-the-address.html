The Address

RFC 791, published in 1981, defines the Internet Protocol. The document makes a distinction that every subsequent system has ignored: "A name indicates what we seek. An address indicates where it is. A route indicates how to get there." The IP address is an address — where a network interface sits in the routing topology. Not who the person is. Not what the person did. Not where the person lives. Where the packet goes.

Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published "A Protocol for Packet Network Intercommunication" in May 1974. The 32-bit address space — 4,294,967,296 possible addresses — was designed for ARPANET, a trusted research network with a small number of cooperative nodes. No adversarial model. No identity layer. No authentication. The address was a routing label for a world that was never expected to become the world.

On February 3, 2011, the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority distributed the last blocks of IPv4 addresses. The free pool was exhausted. The response was not a transition to IPv6 (128-bit, 340 undecillion addresses, designed decades earlier) but NAT — Network Address Translation, which allows thousands of devices to share a single public IP address. As of 2025, more than half of internet traffic still runs on an address scheme that was declared full fourteen years ago. IPv6 adoption is around 45%. The workaround outlasted the solution.

NAT is the mechanism that breaks the assumption every surveillance, legal, and commercial system makes. One public IP address, shared by an entire household, an office building, a university campus, or — through Carrier-Grade NAT — hundreds of thousands of mobile users. The legal system treats an IP address as identifying a subscriber. The subscriber is not the user. Anyone within range of the network generates traffic under the same address: family members, guests, neighbors with the WiFi password, attackers who cracked it.

MaxMind, the dominant IP geolocation provider, needed a default location for IP addresses it could only resolve to the national level. It chose the geographic center of the United States: 38°N, 97°W. The coordinates fell on a farmstead four miles northeast of Potwin, Kansas, owned by Joyce Taylor. For fourteen years, approximately 600 million unlocatable IP addresses pointed to her farm. The FBI arrived looking for runaway children. The IRS came investigating tax fraud. Local police came for stolen property. Strangers came for reasons Taylor never learned. The system needed a location for every address. When it didn't have one, it invented one. The format demands completeness. The completeness was fabricated. When Fusion published the story in 2016, MaxMind moved the default point to the center of a nearby lake.

MaxMind's city-level geolocation accuracy is 66% within a 50-kilometer radius in the United States. The legal system has used IP geolocation to obtain search warrants. A judge cited error rates of 20-50% for IP-based warrants. Homes have been raided based on IP evidence; the subscriber was not the perpetrator. The IP address was correct. The identification was wrong. Judge Harold Baker, in the Central District of Illinois, wrote it directly: "IP address does not equal a person."

The IP address is four numbers separated by dots. The format performs precision — 192.168.1.1 looks exact, specific, traceable. But the address identifies a network interface, not a person. The interface may be shared. The address may be dynamic — assigned today, reassigned tomorrow. The geolocation may be wrong by fifty kilometers. The subscriber may not be the user. Every step from address to person is a point of failure, and the systems that treat the address as identity do so not because the mapping is reliable but because it is actionable. A subpoena to an ISP produces a name and an address. The name and address are enough for a warrant. The warrant is enough for a raid. The raid may find the wrong person. But the system produced a result, and the precision of the number performed the certainty the identification lacked.

The EU classified IP addresses as personal data under GDPR. Canada's Supreme Court ruled in 2024 that accessing subscriber information behind an IP address requires judicial authorization. The United States requires no warrant. The third-party doctrine holds that information voluntarily shared with a third party — here, the ISP that necessarily knows the address — carries no Fourth Amendment privacy expectation. The routing label designed to deliver packets is, in American law, no more private than an envelope's return address.

A name indicates what we seek. An address indicates where it is. A route indicates how to get there. The IP address was designed to be a route. It became an address. The address was mistaken for a name. The name was mistaken for a person.
