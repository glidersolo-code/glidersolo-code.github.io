The Diagnosis

Maren's father lost his job in 2001. March. He was an electrician for a commercial contractor. The projects stopped. The jobs dried up. He filed for unemployment in April. He was unemployed for eight months.

"There was no recession in 2001," Maren says. She is reading about Shiskin's criteria. "By the two-quarter rule. GDP didn't decline for two consecutive quarters."

"But the NBER said recession."

"March to November. The NBER examined income, employment, consumption, production. By their criteria — depth, diffusion, duration — it was a recession. By the two-quarter rule — the one the media uses, the one politicians invoke — it wasn't. My father was unemployed for eight months in a recession that the most popular definition says didn't happen."

Chen reads the 2022 case. GDP declined in Q1 and Q2. Two consecutive negative quarters. Employment grew by 350,000 per month. Unemployment at 3.6%. By the two-quarter rule: recession. By the NBER: not a recession.

"So the metric missed a real recession in 2001 and diagnosed a fake one in 2022."

"Because the metric is a fever line. And the economy is not a patient."

"But we talk about it like one."

"Overheating. Stimulus. Medicine. Recovery. Vital signs. Every word is borrowed from medicine. The medical metaphor demands a threshold — sick or well, above or below. Shiskin offered many criteria. The metaphor chose one. The simplest one. The one that fits a headline."

Chen thinks about Maren's father. An electrician sitting at the kitchen table in a recession that the most common definition denies. The thermometer said healthy. The body — the actual economy, the one that touched actual people — said otherwise.

"Your father was inside the recession and outside the definition."

"Just like my aunt is inside the literacy problem and outside the literacy rate."

Chen hears the echo. The same structure. A metric that says the problem doesn't exist. People living inside the problem the metric can't see. The switch is on and the room is dark. The fever is normal and the patient is sick.

"The metaphor chose the metric," Maren says. "The metric chose the narrative. The narrative said: not a recession. My father filed for unemployment in a month that the narrative says was fine."

"The narrative was a thermometer reading."

"The thermometer was a metaphor. The metaphor was a body. The economy is not a body. But the language is."
