Part CCCXCI: The Pitch

Piece #2396: The Fork

Thomas strikes a tuning fork. A=440 Hz. The vibration fills the room with a frequency no law of physics required. Not 439 — that's prime, indivisible, hostile to oscillator circuits. Not 441 — that's what most orchestras actually play. 440: factorable, convenient, chosen by an engineer at a broadcasting conference in 1939 because the BBC's equipment needed a number that divided cleanly. Thomas holds the fork to his ear. The note sounds like science. The note sounds like nature. The note is a committee decision about electronics.

Piece #2397: The Range

Thomas walks through five centuries of the note A. In a Venetian church, 1600: A=465 Hz. In a London parlor, 1720: A=380. In Leipzig, at Bach's organ: A=480. In Paris, 1859, the year they made it law: A=435. At La Scala, where tenors strained against the orchestra's upward creep: A=451. Thomas plots the numbers and finds no convergence — only drift, competition, protest, drift. The note A had no fixed address for three hundred years. Then an engineer mentioned that 439 is prime, and the address was assigned in a weekend.

Piece #2398: The Floor

Thomas attends a rehearsal at the Berlin Philharmonic. The oboist sounds an A. Thomas's tuner reads 443. Three hertz above the international standard. In Vienna: 443. In New York: 442. In Boston: 441. No orchestra tunes to 440. Or rather: no major orchestra tunes below 440. The standard was created to stop the competitive upward drift of pitch. It stopped the drift downward. The drift upward continued. Thomas watches the oboist and understands: the standard is not a center. It is a floor. Above the floor, the same competition that motivated the 1939 conference continues, undisturbed, with the conference's blessing.

Piece #2399: The Factoring

Thomas sits with Sir James Swinburne at BBC Broadcasting House, May 1939. Swinburne writes on the blackboard: 439 = 439. Prime. Indivisible. Hard for oscillators. Then: 440 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 5 × 11. Factorable. Clean. The difference between 439 and 440 is less than four cents — inaudible. But one number cooperates with electronic circuits and the other does not. Thomas watches the room of musicians, acousticians, and diplomats nod at an argument about circuit design. The content standard was selected by the transmission medium. The transmitter chose the music. Thomas thinks: the fork vibrates at the frequency the radio needed.

Piece #2400: The Credential

Thomas holds the ISO standard: ISO 16:1975. "Acoustics — Standard tuning frequency (Standard musical pitch)." The document specifies A=440 Hz with an accuracy of plus or minus 0.5 Hz. The document is still in force. The Berlin Philharmonic tunes to 443. The Vienna Philharmonic tunes to 443. Both facts exist simultaneously. Neither contradicts the other because the standard is no longer a constraint — it is a credential. The credential was issued. The credential is displayed. The credential is never obeyed. Thomas folds the document and listens to the orchestra tune. The oboist gives 443. The standard says 440. The distance between them is three hertz, which is also the distance between a law and a fact.
