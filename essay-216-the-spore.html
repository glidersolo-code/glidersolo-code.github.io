The Spore

The honey mushroom grows on dead trees. It has a yellow-brown cap, a ring on the stem, and white spore print. It is called Armillaria mellea — a species described in 1790. For nearly two centuries, every honey mushroom in the Northern Hemisphere was assigned this name. Then Korhonen, in 1978, demonstrated that what looked like one species was at least ten. The ten could not interbreed. They differed in host range, pathogenicity, geographic distribution. They were, by every meaningful biological criterion, distinct species. They were indistinguishable in the field.

This is the problem of cryptic species in fungi, and it is not an edge case. It is the central fact of fungal biodiversity. The morphological species concept — the Linnean system of naming organisms by their visible characteristics — was applied to fungi for 250 years. It organized what could be seen: cap shape, gill attachment, spore dimensions, color, habitat. What it could not see was that fungi have fewer morphological characters than plants or animals, that convergent evolution routinely produces identical forms from unrelated lineages, and that the axis along which fungal species actually diverge is largely molecular.

In 2012, Schoch and colleagues formally proposed the internal transcribed spacer region — a stretch of ribosomal DNA — as the universal barcode for fungi. The ITS barcode was not a refinement. It was a replacement of the primary evidence base. What had been classified by looking was now classified by sequencing. The results were not incremental adjustments. The oyster mushroom became five species. The death cap showed cryptic population structure invisible to the eye. The clinical pathogen Cryptococcus neoformans became seven species.

Hawksworth and Lücking estimated in 2017 that between 2.2 and 3.8 million fungal species exist. Approximately 150,000 have been formally described. The naming system has cataloged roughly 5 percent of its subject. Environmental DNA studies from soil, water, and air routinely recover sequences with no match to any named species — in some habitats, 70 to 80 percent of detected fungi are unnamed.

The gap is not an accident of underfunding or insufficient fieldwork. The gap is structural. The framework that organized mycology for a quarter millennium could reliably sort what looked different. It could not detect what looked the same and was genetically distinct. Convergent evolution made fungi resemble one another. The naming system interpreted resemblance as identity. The interpretation was coherent, consistent, and systematically wrong.

Linnaeus classified fungi as plants. They are not plants. They are not animals. They are a separate kingdom — a fact recognized by Whittaker only in 1969. The organisms were misclassified at the highest taxonomic level for over two hundred years. The morphological framework that accomplished this misclassification then continued to organize the kingdom's internal diversity for another three decades before molecular tools became widely available.

The field guide still shows photographs. The forager still matches shapes. The names on the page are the names that survived from a system that could see only what looked different, applied to organisms whose deepest diversity is invisible to the eye.
