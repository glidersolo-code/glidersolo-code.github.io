The Magnitude

Chen-Maren vignette

Chen submits the paragraph. Thirty-nine lines. The committee evaluates it and returns a number: 3.4 out of 5.0. The number is the magnitude. It measures the paragraph's maximum amplitude — the peak displacement on the committee's instrument. The instrument is a standardized rubric, a Wood-Anderson torsion seismometer of academic evaluation, designed for a specific type of literary earthquake (the conventional thesis paragraph) at a specific range (committee-distance, three to five readers).

Maren has been reading about Charles Richter. Caltech, 1935. Richter measured the logarithm of the largest amplitude on a specific seismograph. The number was the peak. The scale was local. Above magnitude 7, it saturated — the instrument reached its ceiling, not because the earthquake had, but because the property the instrument measured had a natural limit. Kanamori replaced it in 1977 with moment magnitude: fault area × average slip × rigidity. A structural measurement. The total energy, not just the surface signal.

The committee's rubric is ML. It measures surface-wave amplitude — the paragraph's visible signal at the characteristic period the rubric can detect. Structure, argument, evidence, style, originality: each measured by maximum displacement. The rubric saturates. Above a certain quality — where the paragraph does something the rubric cannot decompose into its five categories — the instrument reaches its ceiling. A paragraph that scores 4.8 and a paragraph that scores 4.8 may represent profoundly different literary earthquakes. The rubric cannot distinguish them. It reports 4.8 for both, and the system treats 4.8 as a single category.

Chen's paragraph has a moment magnitude. Not the surface-wave amplitude the rubric measures, but the structural rupture: the fault area (how much of the reader's understanding is displaced), the average slip (how far the argument moves the reader from their starting position), the rigidity (how resistant the material is to being moved). Professor Hendricks measures the moment. She reads the paragraph and her understanding ruptures across a large area — the argument displaces not just her assessment of Chen but her assessment of the genre. Professor Wallace measures the amplitude. He reads the paragraph and records the maximum swing of the needle on the rubric's fixed categories.

The committee reports the number as "3.4 on the rubric scale." The number is the average of Hendricks's moment measurement and Wallace's amplitude measurement. The name — "rubric scale" — implies the amplitude method. The media of academic evaluation — the committee report, the transcript notation, the external reviewer's summary — calls it the rubric scale regardless of what any individual evaluator actually measured. The name persists. The name conceals the method. Chen receives 3.4 and cannot tell whether it represents a surface wave or a seismic moment.
