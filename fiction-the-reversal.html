Part CDLXXIV: The Reversal

Piece #2811: The Numbers

Thomas works in the admissions office. He pulls the report. Across the university's graduate programs: 44% of male applicants admitted, 35% of female applicants admitted.

He writes a memo: the data suggests gender bias in admissions. He recommends an investigation.

His supervisor looks at the numbers. Looks at Thomas. Says: "Pull it by department."

Thomas pulls it by department. English: 24% of women admitted, 20% of men. History: 19% of women, 15% of men. Biology: 67% of women, 64% of men. Engineering: 35% of women, 33% of men. In department after department, women are admitted at equal or slightly higher rates.

He stares at the two reports. The aggregate says one thing. The components say the opposite. Both reports are computed from the same data. Neither contains an error.

Piece #2812: The Composition

His supervisor explains.

"Women applied mostly to English and history — departments that admit 20% of everyone. Men applied mostly to engineering and biology — departments that admit 60% of everyone. When you mix a low base rate with one gender and a high base rate with the other, the aggregate inherits the base rates. The 35% isn't measuring discrimination. It's measuring where women applied."

Thomas looks at the data again. Two hundred women applied to English (admitted: 24%). Fifty men applied to English (admitted: 20%). Two hundred men applied to engineering (admitted: 64%). Fifty women applied to engineering (admitted: 67%). In both departments, women's rates are higher. But more women are in the low-rate department. The aggregate drags their overall rate down.

"The aggregate is correct," his supervisor says. "Fewer women were admitted overall. That's a fact. The conclusion you drew — that the university discriminated — is wrong. The university didn't choose which departments women applied to."

"But the aggregate performs as if it's measuring the university's behavior."

"The aggregate measures the outcome. The outcome includes the university's admissions decisions and the applicants' departmental choices. The aggregate blends them. The blend looks like a single decision. It's two decisions stacked on top of each other."

Piece #2813: The Drug

Years later, Thomas reads a medical study. Drug A works better than Drug B in both men and women — taken separately. But Drug B works better than Drug A in the combined population.

He recognizes the structure. The drug's effectiveness is the component. The gender ratio in each treatment group is the lurking variable. If Drug A was given mostly to women (who have lower recovery rates for this condition) and Drug B was given mostly to men (who have higher recovery rates), the aggregate reverses the component-level finding.

Drug A is better for every subgroup. Drug B looks better overall. Which drug should Thomas take?

The answer depends on whether you believe the subgroup results or the aggregate. Pearl says: the subgroup results. The aggregate has absorbed a confounding variable (the gender composition of the treatment groups) that is not part of the drug's causal effect. But the aggregate is what gets reported. The aggregate is what enters the meta-analysis. The aggregate is what the headline says.

Piece #2814: The Level

Thomas realizes that every number has a level. A level of aggregation. The level is a choice.

The university-level rate says one thing. The department-level rate says the opposite. Which level is the right one? The answer depends on the question. If you're asking "did the university discriminate?", you need the department level — because discrimination happens at the department level. If you're asking "are women less likely to be admitted overall?", the aggregate is correct — women are less likely to be admitted, because of where they applied.

But the format — a single overall rate — does not carry the level. The number 35% does not say: "I was computed at the university level and would reverse if computed at the department level." The number says 35%. The number performs as a measurement. The level is invisible.

Piece #2815: The Architecture of Grouping

Thomas has now seen five architectures that operate beyond the individual number.

The sequence creates phantom causation (regression to the mean). The sample creates phantom conclusions (survivorship bias). The aggregate creates phantom directions (Simpson's paradox). Each operates on a different structural level: temporal, compositional, hierarchical. Each is invisible in the individual datum. Each determines the conclusion.

A number is not just a number. A number has a history (how it was computed), a perspective (from whose viewpoint), a reference (against what standard), a sequence (what came before), a sample (who was included), and a level (at what aggregation). The number carries none of these. The reader supplies none of these. The conclusion fills the space where the architecture should be.
