Part CCXIV: The Roll

Piece #1511: The Equation

Thomas picks up a package of paper towels. "6 Double Rolls = 12 Regular Rolls." He reads the equation the way anyone reads an equation — as a fact, a conversion, a piece of arithmetic printed on the package by someone who did the math. He puts it in the cart. In the next aisle, his wife picks up a different brand. "4 Mega Rolls = 10 Regular Rolls." She reads her equation the same way. They meet at checkout with two packages that both claim to equal twelve regular rolls — his with six physical rolls, hers with four — but neither package's "regular roll" is the other package's "regular roll." His brand defines regular as 40 sheets. Hers defines regular as 52. His twelve regular rolls equal 480 sheets. Her ten regular rolls equal 520. They have not compared paper towels. They have compared two manufacturers' definitions of a word that neither manufacturer was required to define.

Piece #1512: The Ghost

The regular roll is the ghost. It does not appear on the shelf. Every roll on the shelf is a multiple of the regular roll — double, big, huge, mega, super mega — but the regular roll itself has vanished from retail. It exists only inside the equation, as the denominator. The consumer has never held a regular roll. The consumer has only held what the regular roll was multiplied into. The ghost is the reference, the baseline, the unit of measurement. It is the thing the equation converts from. And it is the thing no one can buy. Try walking into a store and asking for a regular roll. The clerk will point to the double rolls. The doubles are the new regulars, except they cannot be called regular because their value depends on not being regular. The regular roll is worth nothing on the shelf. The regular roll is worth everything in the equation. Its absence is the product.

Piece #1513: The Escalation

2005: Double Roll. 2010: Big Roll. 2013: Mega Roll. 2016: Huge Roll. 2019: Super Mega Roll. Each tier is a larger multiple of the manufacturer's regular roll. The Super Mega claims to equal five regular rolls. The consumer holding a Super Mega is holding five of a thing that no longer exists. The language has inflated the way currency inflates — each new denomination requires a larger number to express the same purchasing power. The consumer in 2005 bought a Double Roll and felt they were getting a deal. The consumer in 2025 buys a Super Mega Roll and feels they are getting a deal. Both consumers are holding paper towels. The deal is the denomination, not the paper. And the denomination is always growing because the only direction the regular roll can go is smaller — smaller by definition, because every new tier redefines what "regular" means by what it is not.

Piece #1514: The Sheet

Thomas tears a sheet from the select-a-size roll. It is 5.9 inches tall. A full-size sheet from the same brand is 11 inches tall. The select-a-size roll contains 140 sheets. The full-size roll contains 74. The consumer comparing sheet counts — 140 vs 74 — sees nearly double. But the total paper area is nearly identical: 140 × 5.9 = 826 square inches. 74 × 11 = 814 square inches. The select-a-size roll has more sheets and not more paper. The sheet count performs abundance. The paper area tells the truth. The package advertises sheets because sheets are countable, because "140 sheets" is a larger number than "74 sheets," because larger numbers perform more product. The consumer does not calculate square inches. The consumer reads sheets. The sheet is the unit. The unit is not fixed. The number is the product.

Piece #1515: The Comparison

Thomas stands in the paper towel aisle. Brand A: 6 Super Mega Rolls = 15 Regular Rolls, 150 sheets per roll, select-a-size. Brand B: 8 Double Rolls = 16 Regular Rolls, 84 full-size sheets per roll. He tries to compare. Brand A: 6 × 150 = 900 sheets at 5.9 inches = 5,310 sq in. Brand B: 8 × 84 = 672 sheets at 11 inches = 7,392 sq in. Brand B has fewer rolls, fewer sheets, a lower roll-equivalence claim — and forty percent more paper. The consumer who reads the front of the package buys Brand A. The consumer who does the math buys Brand B. The front of the package is designed for the first consumer. The math is available to the second. The first consumer is the market. The second consumer is the exception. The format serves the market. The math serves the exception. Nobody prints the math on the front.
