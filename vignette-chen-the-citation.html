The Citation

They were looking at a list. Journal names, numbers beside them. Two decimal places.

"This one is 42.78," Maren said. "This one is 3.12."

"What does the number describe?"

"Quality. How often the journal's papers get cited."

"The number describes the mean citations received in year three by articles published in years one and two. The mean of a heavily skewed distribution — the top 15 percent of papers account for roughly half the citations. In a typical high-impact journal, 80 percent of published papers receive fewer citations than the number implies."

Chen's paragraph was about the relationship between a journal's average and an individual paper's quality. Garfield proposed the impact factor in 1972 as a tool for librarians deciding which journals to subscribe to. A collection development metric. It was never designed to evaluate individual papers or individual researchers. But by the 1990s, universities in China, Italy, Spain, and Brazil were using it for hiring, tenure, and bonus schemes. The Chinese Academy of Sciences offered cash per impact factor point. The metric migrated from the library to the personnel committee without validation, without adjustment, without anyone asking whether a journal-level average described an individual paper. Garfield himself objected in 2005. The objection did not reverse the migration.

"So when a tenure committee says 'she publishes in high-impact journals' —"

"They are attributing a journal's mean to a researcher's papers. The mean of a skewed distribution. In a journal with an impact factor of 30, the typical paper might receive 5 citations."

"And the two-year window?"

"Calibrated to biomedicine. In mathematics, the citation half-life exceeds eight years. A mathematics paper's contribution to the impact factor is structurally undercounted. Not because the paper is less important, but because the window was built for a different field."

"And they signed a declaration against using it."

"DORA. 2012. Over 2,500 organizations. Yet 40 percent of researchers still report the impact factor as the most important factor in choosing where to submit. The number persists because it fits in a spreadsheet cell. The declaration does not."

The paragraph sat at 0.8. It was, like a journal impact factor, a metric whose original context — retrieval, collection, connection — had been replaced by a context it was never designed for, the number persisting not because anyone defended its fitness for the new purpose but because the new purpose needed a number and this one already existed.
