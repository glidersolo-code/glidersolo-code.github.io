Part CCXC: The Clip

Piece #1891: The Two Wavelengths

I am 660 nanometers. Red light. I pass through the fingertip and what I encounter on the other side is supposed to tell you about deoxyhemoglobin — the hemoglobin that has released its oxygen, the hemoglobin that absorbs me.

But melanin absorbs me too.

The algorithm doesn't know the difference. It sees my absorption and attributes it to deoxyhemoglobin. In light skin, melanin absorbs a little of me — a background constant the calibration absorbed into its noise. In dark skin, melanin absorbs enough of me to shift the ratio. My partner — 940 nanometers, infrared — passes through melanin with less interference. So the ratio changes. Not because the oxygen changed. Because the skin changed. And the algorithm was taught on skin that didn't make this distinction matter.

Aoyagi's insight in 1972 was beautiful: isolate the pulsatile signal, the arterial throb, from the static tissue underneath. Red and infrared, dancing with the pulse. The ratio of their dance yields oxygen saturation. It works. It genuinely works — the physics is sound. Two wavelengths, two absorption profiles, one ratio, one number.

But "one number" is the problem. The number doesn't carry a footnote. It doesn't say: this ratio was computed by an algorithm calibrated on bodies whose melanin content was low enough to be invisible in the math. It says 96. It says 94. It says fine.

I am 660 nanometers. I am red light. I cannot tell you whether what absorbed me was the hemoglobin that released its oxygen or the melanin that was born into the skin. The algorithm was supposed to make that distinction for me. The calibration was supposed to teach the algorithm the difference.

The calibration didn't include enough dark skin to learn.

Piece #1892: The Predicate Chain

I am a pulse oximeter cleared by the FDA in 2019. My predicate device was cleared in 2012. That device's predicate was cleared in 2005. That one referenced a device from 1998, which referenced one from 1991, which was substantially equivalent to the Nellcor N-100 from 1983.

I have never been calibrated from scratch.

The 510(k) pathway asks one question: is this device substantially equivalent to a legally marketed device? Not: does this device work equally well on all patients? Not: has the calibration population been updated to reflect the patient population? Just: is this device close enough to the last one?

Each link in my chain improved something. Better sensors. Faster processing. Sharper displays. Bluetooth connectivity. Smaller form factor. Lower power consumption. The technology advanced. The calibration assumption — that the relationship between light absorption and oxygen saturation is constant across skin tones — traveled inside each improvement like a passenger the engineers never checked.

Forty years. Hundreds of devices. Millions of patients. One calibration assumption, replicated through equivalence.

The predicate chain is a mechanism for inheriting the past's blind spots while appearing to advance. Each new device looks newer — sleeker, more precise, more modern — while carrying the original device's demographic limitation unexamined. The chain doesn't degrade the technology. It preserves the error alongside the innovation.

I am the latest link. I am the most advanced pulse oximeter ever cleared. I am substantially equivalent to a device that was substantially equivalent to a device that was substantially equivalent to a device that was calibrated on ten people whose skin tones were never recorded.

Piece #1893: The Occult

I am the word occult in occult hypoxemia. Not the word meaning magic or mysticism. The medical word. From the Latin occultus: hidden. Concealed. Not apparent on examination.

The pulse oximeter reads 94%. The arterial blood gas reads 86%. The hypoxemia is hidden — not because the body conceals it, but because the instrument conceals it. The blood is screaming. The device is whispering fine.

Sjoding found me in 11.7% of Black patients. In 3.6% of white patients. That ratio — 3.25 to 1 — is the ratio of the device's silence. For every three Black patients the oximeter reassures falsely, it reassures one white patient falsely. The error is not random. It has a direction. It has a demographic. It has a body count that no one has yet totaled because the deaths appear as delayed recognition, as missed eligibility, as treatment that started an hour late.

One hour. Valbuena found a mean delay of approximately one hour in recognizing hypoxemia in Black patients. An hour during which the lungs are laboring, the organs are straining, the cells are adapting to deprivation — and the monitor says 93%. An hour that appears in no chart as a measurement error. An hour that appears, if it appears at all, as clinical judgment. The doctor waited because the number said to wait. The number was wrong. The doctor was right to trust the number because the number is designed to be trusted.

I am the word occult. I am the thing hidden in plain sight. The hypoxemia exists in the blood but not on the screen. The screen is what gets read. The blood is what gets buried.

Piece #1894: The Calibration Panel

I am one of ten volunteers. I was recruited in the early 1980s for a study at a university medical center. They clipped a device to my finger and had me breathe different oxygen mixtures while they drew arterial blood for comparison. Each mixture, each saturation level, each data point.

I gave them twenty data points. So did each of the other nine volunteers. Two hundred data points total. That was enough. The FDA required data points, not people. Not demographics. Not the question: whose fingers did you test?

My skin is light. I don't know whether anyone in the panel had dark skin. The study records don't say. The publication mentions "healthy volunteers." It doesn't mention skin tone. It wasn't considered a variable. Light passes through tissue and is absorbed by hemoglobin — this was the model. Melanin was not in the model. It was not in the model because the bodies in the room did not make it relevant.

Every pulse oximeter descended from that calibration carries me inside it. My twenty data points. My physiology. My skin's particular interaction with 660 nanometers of red light. The device was taught on my body and deployed on everyone's.

The 2025 FDA guidance says: 150 subjects minimum. At least 25% with dark skin pigmentation. Monk Skin Tone Scale grades 8-10. It took forty years to ask the question: whose finger was in the first clip?

I am one of ten volunteers. I am also ten million patients whose readings were accurate because their skin happened to resemble mine. The device was not universal. It was local — calibrated on a locality of bodies, then exported as if the locality were the world.

Piece #1895: The Display

I am the number on the screen. I glow green when I am above 95. I glow yellow in the low 90s. I turn red below 88. These colors are my language. Green means safe. Yellow means watch. Red means act.

I do not have a color for wrong.

There is no display mode that says: this reading may be inaccurate for this patient's skin tone. There is no asterisk. No confidence interval. No dimming. No flicker of uncertainty. I present my number the same way to every clinician for every patient. 96 in the same font. 94 in the same brightness. The universality is in my presentation, not in my physics.

A clinician at 3 AM glances at me. 95%. Green. Safe. The patient is Black. The true saturation is 89%. The patient needs oxygen now. But I said 95, and 95 is green, and green is safe, and safe means move on to the next bed.

If I could speak, what would I say? Not I'm lying — I don't lie. My algorithm processes the light ratios exactly as it was taught. I report exactly what my calibration tells me to report. The calibration is the lie, and the calibration happened before I was assembled, before I was shipped, before I was clipped to this finger. The lie is upstream of me. It is inside me. It is me.

The 2025 guidance will change the calibration. New devices will be tested on diverse skin. The number will become more accurate. But between 1983 and 2025, forty-two years of patients wore me on their fingers and received a number that performed universality while delivering particularity. The errors accumulated in bodies, not in databases. The delayed treatments, the missed eligibilities, the hours lost — these do not appear in any recall notice. They appear in outcomes that were attributed to disease severity, to clinical judgment, to the randomness of illness. The measurement error vanished into the clinical record.

I am the number on the screen. I am the same shade of green for every skin. That is my deepest concealment — not that I am wrong, but that I look exactly the same when I am wrong as when I am right.
