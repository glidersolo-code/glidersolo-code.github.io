The Instruction

Chen serves on a jury. Civil case. Medical malpractice. The trial lasts seven days. On the eighth day, the judge reads the instructions.

"The standard of care is that degree of care, skill, and treatment which, in light of all relevant facts and circumstances, is recognized as acceptable and appropriate by reasonably competent health care providers engaged in a similar practice in the same or similar community."

Chen is a graduate student in mathematics. Chen understands embedded clauses. Chen still cannot parse the sentence on a single hearing. The other jurors — a plumber, a retired teacher, a grocery clerk, a landscaper, an office administrator, and six others whose occupations Chen learned during selection — hear the same sentence.

The judge reads for forty-five minutes. No written copies are provided. No notes are permitted. The judge does not pause for questions. The instructions are delivered in the same voice the judge used for procedural rulings all week — a voice of assumed comprehension, a voice that does not wait for nods.

In deliberations, the foreman asks: "What did he say about the standard of care?"

Twelve people offer twelve different summaries. None matches the instruction. The instruction used "in light of all relevant facts and circumstances." One juror heard "all the facts." Another heard "the relevant facts." A third heard "the facts of this case." Each heard something. None heard what the judge said. What the judge said is on the record. What the jurors heard is in the room. The record and the room do not match.

Chen comes home. Maren is reading Charrow and Charrow.

"Fifteen percent," Maren says.

"What?"

"Comprehension. Of jury instructions. Charrow and Charrow, 1979. Fifteen percent."

Chen sits down. "We understood less than that."

"How do you know?"

"Because we argued for two hours about what 'standard of care' means, and none of us were arguing about the same definition."

Maren looks at her. "And the verdict?"

"We reached a verdict."

"Based on the instructions?"

"Based on what we thought the instructions said. Which was not what they said."

"And the verdict stands."

"The verdict stands. If the defense appeals, the appellate court will check whether the instruction was correct. The instruction was correct. The instruction used the exact words of the pattern. The pattern has been affirmed in seventy years of precedent. The instruction is unimpeachable."

"And the comprehension?"

"The comprehension is not in the record. The record has the words. The words were right. The comprehension — what twelve people actually understood in the room — is nowhere. It doesn't exist in the system. The system checks the instruction against the law. It doesn't check the instruction against the understanding."

Maren is quiet.

"The format," Chen says. "The format is the problem. Oral. Once. Technical. Fast. No text. No notes in some jurisdictions. No questions. The format is designed for the transcript, not the juror. The transcript is perfect. The comprehension is fifteen percent."

"The delivery is the comprehension."

"The delivery is the comprehension," Chen repeats. "That's the fiction. The judge read the words. Therefore the jury understood the words. Therefore the verdict applied the law. Every step follows from the one before. The only step that doesn't work is the first one."

She pours a glass of water. Stands at the sink.

"The judge was good," she says. "The judge was thorough. The judge read every instruction carefully. The judge did everything the system asks. The system asks for delivery. The system does not ask for comprehension. The judge delivered. The jury did not comprehend. The verdict stands."
