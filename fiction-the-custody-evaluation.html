Fiction The Custody Evaluation

Fiction

Part D: The Evaluation

Piece #2941: The Report

Thomas reads a custody evaluation report. One hundred twelve pages. Professional formatting. APA-style citations. Clinical vocabulary: "affect," "attachment," "personality functioning," "parental capacity."

The report recommends that the father receive primary custody.

Thomas reads it again. The mother alleged domestic violence. The evaluator noted that the mother's "hostility toward the father" and "inability to promote the child's relationship with the other parent" suggested poor co-parenting capacity. The father's attorney raised parental alienation. The evaluator found the concept "consistent with the observed dynamics."

The mother's protection was reclassified as pathology. The evaluation did not discover this. The evaluation's framework produced it. The friendly-parent factor, the alienation concept, and the clinical vocabulary combined to create a finding that the protective parent was the dangerous one.

Thomas notes that this is the 500th Part. Five hundred Parts about measurement and concealment. And still the inventory discovers new ways an instrument can constitute the thing it claims to observe.

Piece #2942: The Instruments

Thomas examines the psychological testing section.

The evaluator administered the MMPI-2-RF to both parents. The mother's protocol showed elevated defensiveness scales. The evaluator interpreted this as "guarded presentation consistent with an individual who may be managing the impression she creates."

Thomas reads the literature: 44% of MMPI-2-RF protocols in custody samples are "possibly underreporting" — nearly half yield no useful data because custody litigants respond defensively. The defensiveness is not a clinical finding. The defensiveness is the expected response of anyone whose children's future depends on the answers.

The test was designed for clinical populations — people with diagnosed mental illness being evaluated for treatment. Custody litigants are not clinical populations. The instrument's validated domain does not include its applied domain. But the report does not say "this test was designed for a different population and may not apply here." The report says "the MMPI-2-RF results suggest."

Thomas notes that "suggest" does an enormous amount of work. The word bridges the gap between what the instrument can validly measure and what the report claims to have found. The suggestion performs as evidence.

Piece #2943: The Inversion

Thomas maps the logical structure.

A mother alleges abuse. In a system without the friendly-parent provision, the allegation triggers investigation of the father. In a system with the friendly-parent provision, the allegation triggers scrutiny of the mother. The provision asks: is this parent promoting the child's relationship with the other parent? The mother who says "this man is hurting our child" is, by definition, not promoting the relationship.

The father responds: parental alienation. The child's fear is not evidence of abuse. The child's fear is evidence of the mother's manipulation. The child's testimony supports the alienation claim: the child is afraid of the father, which proves the mother has succeeded in turning the child against him. The fear that would, in any other context, be evidence of danger becomes evidence of influence.

Research: 2,000 court opinions. Fathers' alienation cross-claims doubled the rejection rate of mothers' abuse allegations. When evaluators were appointed, outcomes for protective mothers worsened further.

Thomas writes: the instrument inverts the evidentiary structure. Protection → danger. Fear → fabrication. Allegation → alienation. The evaluation is designed to protect the child. The evaluation's framework transfers the child to the parent the child fears.

Piece #2944: The Price

Thomas reads the fee schedule.

A comprehensive custody evaluation: $5,000 to $50,000. Hourly rates: $100 to $400. The process takes months. The evaluator interviews both parents, both children, the grandparents, the teachers, the therapists. The evaluator visits both homes. The evaluator administers tests. The evaluator writes 112 pages.

A parent who cannot afford the evaluation receives an abbreviated assessment — or none at all. Some jurisdictions provide court-appointed evaluators for families below an income threshold. Many do not.

Thomas notes that the depth of the evaluation correlates with parental income rather than child need. A wealthy family receives 112 pages of assessment. A poor family receives a brief interview. The instrument's thoroughness is a function of the family's resources. The child's best interest is assessed more carefully when the parents have more money.

The economic structure does not merely create disparate access to the evaluation. The economic structure creates disparate constructions of the child's best interest. The wealthy family's evaluation constructs a detailed, nuanced account of family dynamics. The poor family's evaluation — if it exists at all — constructs a sketch. The sketch has the same legal weight as the portrait. The sketch determines the same custody outcome.

Piece #2945: The Ground

Thomas sits with the eight instruments.

The checkbox. The model. The definition. The algorithm. The surrogate. The kit. The interview. The evaluation.

Eight instruments. Eight fields. Eight versions of the same structure: the instrument does not observe the phenomenon. The instrument constitutes it. Four modes of constitution: inclusion, exclusion, activation, inversion.

The evaluation adds a dimension the credible fear interview didn't have. The interview inverts once: trauma symptoms become evidence against the claim of trauma. The evaluation inverts twice: first the parent's behavior (protection → alienation), then the child's testimony (fear → manipulation). The double inversion produces a custody transfer to the person the parent and child both fear.

Thomas notes that five hundred Parts have passed. The inventory began with measurements that concealed properties of numbers. It moved to measurements that concealed properties of datasets, of feedback loops, of readers, of models, of metaphors. Now it has reached measurements that constitute the phenomena they claim to observe — instruments that are inside the world they claim to stand outside of.

The ground is not one thing. The ground has structure. And the structure is still revealing itself.
