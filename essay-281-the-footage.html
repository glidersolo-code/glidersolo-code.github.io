The Footage

The listing says 2,400 square feet. The consumer reads this as the size of the house — a number that enables comparison, that divides into the price to yield a per-foot cost, that answers the question "how big?" with the precision of four digits. But the number's composition is invisible in the number itself. Does the 2,400 include the finished basement? The enclosed sunporch? The bonus room above the garage? The answers vary by listing, by agent, by jurisdiction, and by whether the measuring party follows ANSI Z765 — the American National Standard for Single-Family Residential Buildings — which specifies "above-grade finished area" and excludes below-grade space regardless of finish.

ANSI Z765 is voluntary. Not all states require it. Not all agents follow it. Not all MLS databases enforce it. The same house measured by three appraisers can produce three different numbers depending on their treatment of borderline spaces: the basement finished with carpet and drywall but below grade; the sunporch that is heated but not air-conditioned; the attic conversion accessible by a permanent stairway but with ceiling heights below seven feet across parts of the floor area. The measuring tape is consistent. The rules about what to measure with it are not.

The measurement boundary itself conceals a layer. Appraisers measure from the exterior of the walls. The 2,400 square feet includes the space occupied by framing, insulation, sheathing, and drywall — space the occupant cannot use. In a house with 12-inch exterior walls, approximately 8 percent of the measured area is wall. The consumer lives in roughly 2,200 square feet while the listing reports 2,400. The number describes the building's footprint. The experience is the building's interior. The wall is the difference between what the appraiser measures and what the homeowner furnishes.

Four entities may measure the same house for four purposes. The county assessor measures for property tax — often from permit records, which may reflect the house as built, not as modified. The appraiser measures for mortgage lending — walking the exterior with a laser measurer. The real estate agent measures for the listing — sometimes from the builder's original floor plan, sometimes from the appraiser's report, sometimes from the assessor's records. The homeowner's contractor measures for a renovation permit — room by room, interior dimensions. The house has not changed size. The number has changed four times. The consumer who discovers a discrepancy between the county records and the listing does not know which number to trust because the question has never had one answer.

Price per square foot inherits every ambiguity in the footage. The consumer who divides the asking price by the listed square footage gets a ratio that performs comparability. But the ratio blends above-grade and below-grade space, exterior-measured and interior-lived space, heated and unheated space, daylit and windowless space. Basement square feet are worth less per foot than main-floor square feet. Blending them into one number — total price divided by total area — presents a single value for heterogeneous space. The consumer comparing two houses at $212 and $218 per square foot is comparing two ratios whose numerators are total prices and whose denominators are total areas measured by different rules and containing different compositions of space. The number performs precision to four significant figures. The measurement supports perhaps two.
