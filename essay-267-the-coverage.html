The Coverage

A gallon of interior latex paint covers approximately 400 square feet. The number is printed on the label, sometimes in bold, sometimes in the fine specifications on the back, always in the same unit: area per volume. The consumer divides room by gallon and gets a purchase quantity. The number is the plan.

The number is measured on a smooth, sealed, primed surface of uniform light color, applied at the manufacturer's recommended thickness — typically four mils wet, drying to one and a half or two — using a standardized roller under controlled conditions. ASTM D5150, the Standard Test Method for Hiding Power of Architectural Paints, measures opacity: the paint's ability to obscure a patterned substrate of alternating black and white squares. When the contrast ratio — the ratio of reflectance over the black substrate to reflectance over the white — reaches a threshold (typically 0.98), the paint has "hidden" the surface. Coverage is derived from this: the spread rate at which acceptable hiding occurs. But hiding and coverage are different measurements. Hiding describes what disappears. Coverage describes what appears. The label conflates them into a single number because the consumer wants one answer.

The consumer's wall is not the test panel. Textured stucco absorbs paint into its valleys and peaks, increasing the effective surface area by forty to sixty percent. Unprimed drywall absorbs the first coat into its porous paper facing. A dark wall beneath a light coat shows through — each additional coat to achieve hiding halves the effective coverage rate. A gallon rated at 400 square feet, applied to a textured, unprimed, dark-colored surface in a single coat, may cover 150 to 200 square feet. The consumer who calculated two gallons for the room needs four.

What makes paint coverage a particularly clean instance: the gap is not hidden. The label often says "approximately." Some labels say "350-400 sq ft." The hedge is visible. But the consumer reads the number, not the hedge. The format — a single figure on a product label — demands a single answer, and the single answer becomes the plan. The word "approximately" performs acknowledgment. The number performs certainty. The number wins because the number fits the calculation. You cannot divide a room by "it depends."

The deeper structure is the relationship between hiding power and coverage rate. Hiding power is measured in a closed system — controlled substrate, controlled thickness, controlled conditions. Coverage rate is applied in an open system — unknown walls, unknown primers, unknown previous colors, unknown textures, unknown ambient humidity and temperature affecting drying and flow. The label translates a closed-system measurement into an open-system prediction without marking the translation. The consumer reads the result as a property of the paint. It is a property of the test.

Every professional painter knows this. The rule of thumb is to buy ten to twenty percent more than the label calculation suggests, and more for dark-over-light or rough surfaces. The expertise is knowing how much the label lies. Not that it lies — the number is accurate for the conditions it measures — but that the conditions it measures are not the conditions the paint will meet. The format performs universality. The wall performs locality. The label number is the paint's résumé. The wall is the job.
