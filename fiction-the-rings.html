The Rings

Thomas, Part LXXXII

851. The Map

The climate envelope is a map of where a species lives. Not where it thrives. Not where it suffers. Where it lives — which is to say, where it has been found, which is to say, where it has survived long enough to be counted. The map is built from occurrence data: a dot on a landscape wherever someone has confirmed the species' presence. The dots cluster in certain climatic conditions — a range of temperatures, a range of precipitation, a band of elevation. The envelope is the line drawn around the dots. Everything inside the envelope is habitat. Everything outside is not. The forecast asks: as the climate shifts, where does the envelope go?

Thomas has an envelope too. It is drawn around the places where he has been observed — the house, the grocery store, the church, Helen's kitchen on Saturday mornings, the hardware store on the occasional Tuesday. If someone mapped his occurrence data, the envelope would describe a small territory in a medium-sized town. If they asked what climate conditions the species Thomas tolerates, the data would say: moderate, predictable, mild. Nothing in the occurrence map would indicate distress.

The map shows where the species has been found. It does not show what the species is feeling.

852. The Sign

Margaret Evans collected 1,558 tree-ring series from 977 locations across the range of Pinus edulis — pinyon pine, the slow-growing conifer that anchors the woodlands of the Colorado Plateau. Each ring is a year. Each year records how the tree responded to what the climate actually did — not what the species-level distribution implies it should tolerate. The rings are individual testimony. The envelope is a collective average.

When Evans compared the two, the signs were reversed.

The envelope says: pinyon pine lives in warm places. It occurs at intermediate temperatures. Populations at the cool edge of the range occupy conditions that warming will push toward the species' preferred zone. The forecast: leading-edge populations expand. The cool margin benefits.

The tree rings say: every tree, everywhere — cool margin, warm margin, center — grows less in warmer years. There is no population that benefits from warmth. The entire distribution suffers the same way. The individual temporal signal — what happens when this tree experiences this year's warmth — is opposite in sign to the spatial signal — where the species has managed to persist across millennia.

Thomas on a bad week: slower at the store, quieter at church, skipping the hardware store on Tuesday. His envelope has not changed. His occurrence data still plots in the same territory. But the rings — the week-by-week record of how Thomas responds to what actually happens — tell a different story from the map. Sarah checks the map. She drives by the house, sees the lights on, notes the car in the driveway. The envelope is intact. The occurrence data are normal. She does not read the rings.

The signs are reversed, and nobody checks the rings.

853. The Trailing Edge

In the standard forecast, a species distribution has two edges. The trailing edge is the warm boundary — the zone where conditions are already near the limit of tolerance, where warming will push the population past what it can bear. The leading edge is the cool boundary — the zone where warming opens new territory, expanding the range into previously inhospitable ground. The forecast is a story of redistribution: loss at the trailing edge, gain at the leading edge, a net shift poleward.

Evans found that the trailing edge is everywhere. The entire distribution of pinyon pine responds to warming the same way: less growth. The spatial pattern that suggested a leading edge was an artifact of confusing where a species has historically survived with what individual organisms can currently tolerate. The survival reflects thousands of years of evolutionary adaptation — generations of trees whose ancestors were selected for the conditions they occupied. The tolerance is transient — what the trees alive right now can handle, this year, with the genes they inherited.

The equilibrium says: the species has been here; therefore, the species can be here. The transient response says: the individuals alive today are suffering, and their survival depends on whether evolution can close the gap between what they can tolerate and what the climate is doing, before the climate outruns them.

Helen's trailing edge is not geographic. It is temporal. The warm boundary is not a place — it is a pace. The world around her is changing at a rate that outstrips her ability to adapt: the bank moved online, the pharmacy changed its phone system, the doctor's office requires a patient portal. Each change is small. The envelope still surrounds her — she lives in the same house, in the same town, under the same conditions. But the individual response to each temporal shift is negative. She grows less. She does less. She retreats. And the forecast — built from the spatial data of where she has been found, the occurrence map of her visible life — says she is stable, because the envelope has not moved.

The trailing edge is not a place on the map. It is a condition in the organism. And when the forecast cannot see the organism, it cannot see the edge.

854. The Rescue

Evans names the mechanism: evolutionary rescue. The species can persist if — and only if — evolution produces individuals tolerant of the new conditions faster than the climate shifts. If evolution is fast enough, the spatial pattern eventually catches up to the temporal pattern. The signs converge. The trees that survive the transient period produce offspring better adapted to warmth, and the distribution restabilizes. If evolution is too slow, the transient response — suffering, everywhere, simultaneously — becomes extinction. The forecast showed a future. The future assumed a rescue that may or may not arrive.

Thomas does not use the word rescue. He does not frame his Saturday visits as intervention. But what he provides is what evolutionary rescue provides: a rate of adaptation that keeps pace with the rate of change. The phone system changed — Thomas calls the pharmacy for Helen and relays the refill. The bank went online — Thomas prints the statements when he stops by the library. The doctor's office requires a portal — Thomas sits in the kitchen with his phone and reads the lab results aloud. Each adaptation is small. Each one matches a specific environmental shift with a specific compensatory behavior. The rate is one adjustment per week, delivered on Saturdays, in the kitchen, with tea.

The question is not whether Helen needs help. The occurrence data already show that — she lives in the same house, the same town, the same envelope. The question is whether the rate of help matches the rate of change. If the adjustments arrive fast enough, the gap between what Helen can tolerate and what the world demands stays closed. If the adjustments slow down — if Thomas gets sick, if the drive becomes too long, if the gasoline gets too expensive, if his own trailing edge arrives — the gap opens. The rescue fails. The forecast said the envelope was stable. The rings said the organism was starving.

855. The Rings

Fifteen hundred and fifty-eight time series. Nine hundred and seventy-seven locations. More than a century of annual data. Each ring is a year of testimony from a single organism about what actually happened at that location, in that season, under those conditions. The rings do not speculate about the future. They do not model the equilibrium. They record what the tree experienced, and the record is unambiguous: every tree grows less when it is warmer. Every tree, everywhere, without exception.

The power of tree rings is not that they are ancient — though they are. It is that they are individual. Each ring is one tree's answer to one year's question. Climate envelope models aggregate: they smooth a thousand individual responses into a single species-level pattern. The pattern is real. The pattern is also an average, and the average has consumed the variance, and the variance is where the suffering lives.

Thomas has rings. Every Saturday is a ring. Every visit is one organism's response to one week's conditions. If someone read the rings — counted the years, measured the widths, noted the narrow ones and the wide ones — they would see things the occurrence data cannot show. They would see the year Sarah's husband left and Helen stopped answering the phone for three months. They would see the winter Thomas had pneumonia and missed six Saturdays and Helen's rings went nearly absent. They would see the spring when Helen's garden came back and the rings were wide — wider than any year in the decade before — and Thomas brought his grandchildren, and the kitchen held five people instead of two, and the biofilm was visible.

The envelope says the species is stable. The rings say the species is alive — conditionally, narrowly, year by year, one cell at a time — and the condition is that someone is reading the rings.
