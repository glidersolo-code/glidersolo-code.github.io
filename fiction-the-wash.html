Part CCXLV: The Wash

Piece #1666: The Word

The washing machine dial says Hot, Warm, Cold. Three words. Three categories. The consumer turns the dial and believes the machine delivers a temperature. The machine delivers a mixture. Hot means: open the hot supply valve. The temperature that arrives depends on the water heater — 120°F if the homeowner followed the DOE recommendation, 140°F if the heater is old and never adjusted, 105°F if the heater is small and recovering from a shower. Warm means: open both valves. The temperature depends on the hot supply AND the cold supply, blended. Cold means: open the cold supply valve. The temperature that arrives depends on the season, the geography, and the depth of the supply pipe. In Minneapolis in January, cold tap water is 37 degrees Fahrenheit. In Phoenix in August: 78 degrees. Same dial position. Forty-one degrees of difference. The word performed constancy. The pipe delivered climate.

Piece #1667: The Season

The cold inlet temperature varies by at least 40 degrees across the continental United States between winter and summer extremes. This means "Warm" varies too — the mixing valve blends hot and cold, and if cold is 40°F, warm is approximately 80°F, and if cold is 78°F, warm is approximately 99°F. The word "Warm" covers a 19-degree range determined by when the load runs and where the machine sits. The fabric care label on the garment — the tag the consumer cuts off — defines "warm" as 105°F plus or minus 15°F, a 30-degree band, acknowledging the variability the machine's dial does not. The care label was honest. The dial was categorical. The consumer reads the dial.

Piece #1668: The Enzyme

The assumed hierarchy — hot cleans best, warm cleans less, cold cleans least — was accurate when soap was the detergent. Soap dissolves grease more effectively at higher temperatures. Modern laundry detergent is not soap. It is an enzyme-based formulation: protease breaks down protein stains, lipase breaks down fats, amylase breaks down starches. These enzymes are proteins. They have optimal temperature ranges, typically 60 to 100 degrees Fahrenheit. Above 140°F, the enzymes denature — their protein structures unfold and they lose catalytic function. Hot water can reduce the effectiveness of the product designed to work in the water the consumer chose for maximum cleaning. The hierarchy inverted and the dial did not change.

Piece #1669: The Sanitize

The machine offers a "Sanitize" cycle. The consumer reads: kills bacteria. NSF Protocol P172 defines sanitization as achieving 150°F sustained for at least seven minutes — a temperature that requires the water heater to be set above the scald-risk threshold that the DOE recommends against. Many residential "sanitize" cycles use steam injection, extended agitation, or heated rinse water rather than achieving the sustained 150°F thermal threshold. The cycle reduces bacterial load. The label says "sanitize." The gap between reduction and elimination is the gap between the marketing word and the microbiological standard. The consumer who runs the sanitize cycle for a sick child's bedding may achieve a three-log reduction. A three-log reduction is 99.9 percent. The remaining 0.1 percent, if pathogenic, is not zero.

Piece #1670: The Dial

Two hundred and forty-five Parts. The washing machine temperature dial performs three categories — hot, warm, cold — behind which three variables multiply: water heater setpoint, geographic cold-supply temperature, and seasonal inlet variation. The consumer turns to Hot and reads 130°F where the machine delivers 120°F or 105°F or whatever the heater provides. The consumer turns to Cold and reads consistency where the pipe delivers climate — 37°F in one latitude, 78°F in another, with the same machine, the same detergent, the same garment. The enzyme hierarchy that once rewarded hot water now punishes it. The sanitize label that promises sterility delivers reduction. Thomas washed a load on Hot. He did not know the temperature. The dial did not display it. The water heater, which he had never adjusted, was set to 120°F. The cold supply, which he had never measured, was 55°F. Warm, had he selected it, would have been approximately 87°F — within two degrees of what his sister in Phoenix got from Cold. The word was the same. The temperature was not. The dial, having spoken, did not elaborate.
