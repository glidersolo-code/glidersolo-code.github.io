The Channel

A television channel is a band of electromagnetic frequencies. Channel 2 is 54 to 60 megahertz. Channel 7 is 174 to 180 megahertz. The number corresponds to a position in the radio spectrum, allocated by the Federal Communications Commission for the transmission of analog video and audio signals. This is what a channel is. A slice of physics. A location in the spectrum where a tuner can lock onto a signal.

There is no Channel 1. On June 14, 1948, the FCC reallocated the Channel 1 frequencies to land mobile radio. Rather than renumber the twelve remaining VHF channels, the Commission deleted Channel 1 from the table. Every television set manufactured in the United States since 1948 starts at 2. The absence is a fossil of a spectrum-sharing dispute between broadcasters and two-way radio operators, resolved seventy-eight years ago, remembered by no one who turns on a television.

The original VHF allocation — channels 2 through 13 — created a hierarchy. VHF signals propagated farther and penetrated buildings better than the UHF signals (channels 14 through 83) added in 1952. The network affiliates claimed the low numbers. Independents and educational stations received the high ones. The hierarchy was electromagnetic, but it read as quality. Channel 5 looked like a real station. Channel 48 looked marginal. Congress mandated UHF tuners in all new sets in 1962. The hardware was equalized. The perception was not.

Cable television severed the connection between number and frequency. A cable system could assign any station to any position. Channel 7 on cable might carry the same station as Channel 7 over the air — not because the frequencies matched but because the brand required continuity. The cable box decoded a digital signal and placed it at a number in a lineup. The number corresponded to nothing physical. It was a position in a list.

On June 12, 2009, every analog television signal in the United States went dark. The digital transition reassigned frequencies wholesale. KTLA, Channel 5 in Los Angeles since 1947, began transmitting on physical RF channel 31. But the FCC had anticipated the brand problem. The ATSC standard introduced PSIP — Program and System Information Protocol — which assigned each station a "virtual channel." KTLA appeared on every set as 5.1 even though its signal occupied an entirely different frequency. The Commission explicitly recognized the need to preserve "analog channel number brand identification."

Engineers built a protocol — wrote code, drafted standards, implemented firmware updates across millions of devices — specifically to maintain a number that no longer corresponded to any physical reality. The frequency was moved. The brand stayed. The infrastructure reorganized itself around the fiction.

This is the difference between the channel number and the area code. The area code outlived its infrastructure passively — cultural inertia preserved 212 as Manhattan even after the rotary dial and crossbar switch were demolished. The channel number was actively preserved — engineers designed new systems specifically to maintain the identity after the physical basis disappeared. The area code survived by not being removed. The channel number survived because the system rebuilt itself to protect it.

The number began as a frequency allocation in 1941. It became a dial position on a vacuum-tube set. It became a cable lineup entry. It became a virtual designation in a protocol table. It became an icon on a streaming interface. At each transition, the number shed the physical reality it had most recently inhabited and acquired a new host. The frequency was reallocated. The dial was scrapped. The cable was cut. The protocol was updated. The number persisted through every demolition because the brand was more valuable than the physics.

"Channel 7 News at Six." The sentence performs a frequency that no longer exists, spoken by an anchor sitting in a building whose transmission equipment broadcasts on a different RF channel, received by a device that decodes a virtual designation from a protocol designed to maintain a fiction, watched by a viewer who has never owned an antenna and does not know what a megahertz is. Every word in the sentence — channel, seven, news, at, six — refers to something that either no longer exists or no longer means what it once meant. The sentence is perfectly understood.
