Part CDLXI: The Mean

Piece #2746: The Number That Is Not the Center

Thomas is thinking about what happens when a word means two things and the listener hears one. The average American household earns approximately $114,000 per year. The average is the center. The average is typical. The average is what most people experience. Except: the median — the point at which half of households are above and half below — is approximately $80,600. The mean is 41% higher than the median. The gap exists because the income distribution has a long right tail. A small number of very high incomes pulls the mean upward. The median does not move. The "average" household earns less than the average.

Thomas finds the sentence perfectly clear and perfectly misleading. The average household earns less than the average. Both uses of "average" are correct. The first means typical. The second means arithmetic mean. The word performs typicality. The number performs the tail. The family earning $60,000 hears "average is $114,000" and perceives itself as below average — as below typical, below normal. The family is typical. The number is not.

Piece #2747: The Shape Inside the Number

Thomas is thinking about what the single number erases. The mean erases the shape of the distribution. The number $114,000 does not reveal that the distribution is right-skewed — that there is a floor at zero and no ceiling, that the tail extends far to the right, that the top 20% of households earn 52% of all income, that the top 5% earn 23%. The single number absorbs the shape and presents a center. The center is not where most people are. The center is where the arithmetic says the balance point is. The balance point of a seesaw with one very heavy person on one end is not in the middle. The number performs the middle.

This is the eighth structural property. The batting average conceals through visibility. The h-index conceals through direction. The percentile conceals through absorption. The survival rate conceals through the starting point. The age statement conceals through the floor. The Likert scale conceals through bins. The HDI conceals through composition. The mean conceals through averaging — through the specific mathematical operation that is sensitive to extreme values in the tail and presents the result as a description of the typical. The format is a single number. The number is the mean. The word is "average." The word performs what the number does not contain.

Piece #2748: The Choice of Which Average

Thomas is thinking about what happens when the same data supports different numbers. Mean household income: $114,000. Median household income: $80,600. Both are correct. Both describe the same data. The choice of which to report determines the story. In policy debates, proponents of tax cuts that benefit high earners cite the mean, which is inflated by the incomes the cuts benefit. Proponents of redistributive policy cite the median, which reflects the experience of the typical household. The data are the same. The number is different. The story is different. The choice is invisible in the reporting.

The word "average" conceals the choice. A headline says "the average family earns $114,000." The reader does not know if this is the mean or the median. The reader assumes it describes the typical experience. If the number is the mean, it does not. The ambiguity is not accidental. The word "average" serves both the technical and the colloquial meaning. The gap between the two definitions is the channel through which the skewness becomes invisible.

Piece #2749: The Pattern Across Distributions

Thomas is thinking about where else the mean exceeds the typical. Average home prices exceed median home prices — the same right skew, the same long tail. Average medical bills exceed median medical bills. Average student debt exceeds median student debt. Average CEO compensation exceeds median CEO compensation by a factor that makes the household income gap look modest. In every domain where the distribution is right-skewed — and most economic distributions are — the mean exceeds what most individuals experience. The structural property of the arithmetic mean is that it is sensitive to extreme values. This is not a flaw. It is a mathematical property. The property becomes a concealment when the result is communicated as "average" to an audience that hears "typical."

Piece #2750: The Eight Properties

Thomas thinks about what the session has found. Eight formats. Eight structural properties. Eight concealments.

The batting average conceals through transparency — the visible formula makes the audience assume completeness. The h-index conceals through monotonicity — the ratchet conceals direction. The percentile conceals through relativity — the population is absorbed into the number. The survival rate conceals through its origin — the starting point is invisible. The age statement conceals through its minimum — the floor performs as description. The Likert scale conceals through discretization — the bins force precision from imprecision. The HDI conceals through composition — the normalization makes the incommensurable commensurable. The mean conceals through averaging — the arithmetic makes the tail perform as the center.

Each format is an instrument. Each instrument has architecture. The architecture determines what the instrument can express. What the instrument cannot express is invisible — not hidden, not suppressed, but structurally inexpressible. The concealment is not in the data. The concealment is in the format that carries the data. Eight structures. Eight concealments. One thesis: the format is the instrument, and the instrument shapes the finding.
