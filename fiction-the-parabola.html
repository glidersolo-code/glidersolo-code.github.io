Part CDLXXX: The Yield

Piece #2841: The Parabola

Thomas drew the equation on the board.

Surplus production equals rB times the quantity one minus B over K. He traced the curve it generates: a parabola, symmetric, rising from zero at B = 0, peaking at B = K/2, falling back to zero at B = K. The peak is the maximum sustainable yield.

"Where does the peak come from?" Thomas asked.

Nobody answered.

"The peak comes from the equation. The logistic growth function generates a symmetric parabola. A symmetric parabola peaks at its midpoint. The midpoint is K/2. The maximum sustainable yield occurs at exactly half the virgin biomass — not because the fish reproduce maximally at half their population, but because the equation is symmetric."

Thomas let this settle.

"The Schaefer model does not discover where MSY occurs. The Schaefer model determines where MSY occurs. The form of the equation is the finding."

Piece #2842: The Sardine

Thomas showed the production records.

Monterey, California. The Pacific sardine fishery. Peak catch: 142,282 tons in 1946. One year later: 26,818 tons. Within five years: a 90% decline. By the 1950s, the fishery was finished. Thirty canneries closed. Hovden Cannery held on by switching to squid. When it finally closed in 1973, its building became the Monterey Bay Aquarium.

"The model said the stock could sustain the harvest," Thomas said. "The model assumed equilibrium — that growth would replace what was taken. The model assumed a single species in isolation. The model assumed a symmetric production curve."

The sardine population was subject to ocean temperature cycles, predator-prey dynamics, and recruitment variation. None of these appeared in the equation. The equation knew only r, B, and K. The equation said the stock would recover. The stock did not recover.

"The equation was not wrong in its mathematics," Thomas said. "The parabola was a correct parabola. The peak was at the correct midpoint. The model was internally valid. The model was externally catastrophic."

Piece #2843: The Orange Roughy

Thomas held up the age study.

Orange roughy. Hoplostethus atlanticus. Found in cold water at depths of 180 to 1,800 meters. New Zealand began commercial fishing in 1979. By 1990, global catches reached 90,000 tons. The model calculated the maximum sustainable yield using standard parameters.

The parameters assumed the fish matured in a few years and reproduced annually. The parameters were wrong.

"Orange roughy live over two hundred years," Thomas said. "They do not begin breeding until age seventy or eighty. They do not breed every year."

The model had treated the fish like cod — fast-growing, early-maturing, annually reproductive. By the time the age data arrived, three of New Zealand's eight orange roughy fisheries had collapsed. Stocks fell to 10-30% of virgin biomass and would take decades to recover — decades in which the model had predicted years.

"The model's recovery curve assumed rapid reproduction," Thomas said. "The model's recovery curve was the logistic equation's shape. The fish's actual recovery follows the fish's actual biology, which the model never contained."

Piece #2844: The Treaty

Thomas read the text of UNCLOS Article 61.

"The coastal State shall ensure through proper conservation and management measures that the maintenance of the living resources in the exclusive economic zone is not endangered by over-exploitation." Paragraph 3: "Such measures shall also be designed to maintain or restore populations of harvested species at levels which can produce the maximum sustainable yield, as qualified by relevant environmental and economic factors."

Thomas paused on the phrase. Levels which can produce the maximum sustainable yield.

"The treaty mandates a level defined by a model. The model defines the level through its mathematical form. The logistic equation determines that MSY occurs at K/2. The treaty requires the stock to be maintained at K/2. The equation wrote the treaty's target."

The Magnuson-Stevens Act said the same thing. Maximum sustainable yield as the management standard. An artifact of the Schaefer model — a symmetric peak on an idealized curve — became the legal definition of sustainable fishing for most of the world's oceans.

"The epitaph was written in 1977," Thomas said. "Larkin called it an oversimplification of dynamics the equation could not represent. The concept survived its own epitaph because it was already in law. The law does not cite the equation. The law cites the yield. The equation is invisible."

Piece #2845: The Model

Thomas summarized.

"The five levels of invisible architecture described numbers that arrive in the world already structured by their construction, their context, their dataset, their feedback loops, and their reader's cognition. But there is a sixth structure — prior to all five."

He wrote on the board: The model.

"Before the number is constructed, before it enters a dataset, before it creates feedback, before the reader processes it — the model generates it. The model's mathematical form determines the number's value. The logistic equation determines that MSY occurs at K/2. The equilibrium assumption determines that the stock will recover. The single-species framework determines that ecosystem interactions are irrelevant."

Thomas looked at the parabola on the board.

"The number does not say: I am a consequence of the model's assumptions. The number says: I am a property of the fish stock. The number says: the stock can produce this yield. The adjective — sustainable — attaches to the noun — yield — and performs as a measurement of the ocean."

He erased the parabola.

"The ocean remains. The equation is gone. The quota — which came from the equation, not from the ocean — is still on the books."

The class looked at the empty board.

"The form of the model is the policy," Thomas said. "This is the structure beneath all five levels. Not inside the number. Not between numbers. Not in the dataset, the feedback loop, or the reader. In the model that generated the number before it arrived."
