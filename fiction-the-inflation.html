Part CCXIX: The Inflation

Piece #1536: The Two Numbers

Thomas crouches beside his car with a tire gauge. The tire's sidewall reads "Max Press 44 PSI." The sticker on the driver's door jamb reads "Recommended: 32 PSI." Two numbers, twelve pounds apart, both printed by authorities, both about the same tire. The 44 is the tire manufacturer's structural maximum — the most air the casing can hold without exceeding its load rating. The 32 is the vehicle manufacturer's recommendation — the pressure that balances handling, traction, ride comfort, and fuel economy for this car's weight and suspension. The tire does not know what car it is on. The car does not know what tire is on it. The sidewall says what the tire can tolerate. The placard says what the vehicle needs. Thomas, like most drivers, reads the number on the tire because the number is on the tire, and when you want to know about a tire, you look at the tire. The number on the door — the one that matters — is on the car.

Piece #1537: The Maximum

Thomas inflates to 44. The sidewall said 44, and more air means firmer tires, and firmer feels safer. But at 44 PSI, the contact patch — the rectangle of rubber that touches the road — shrinks. The tire crowns, pressing the center of the tread into the pavement and lifting the shoulders. Traction decreases in wet conditions. The ride hardens. Center wear accelerates, shortening tire life. Impact resistance drops — the rigid casing transmits potholes directly to the wheel, increasing the risk of rim damage. The consumer who inflated to the maximum thought they were optimizing. They were maximizing one parameter — pressure — at the expense of every parameter the vehicle manufacturer had balanced when specifying 32. The sidewall maximum is a structural limit. The consumer reads it as a target.

Piece #1538: The Warning

The TPMS light glows amber on Thomas's dashboard. Tire Pressure Monitoring System — mandated by the TREAD Act of 2000, required on all vehicles sold in the US since 2007, a response to the Firestone/Ford Explorer failures that killed 271 people. The system triggers a warning when any tire's pressure drops 25 percent below the vehicle manufacturer's recommended level. If the placard says 32 PSI, the TPMS warns at 24. Thomas was driving at 26 PSI — six pounds below recommendation, enough to increase rolling resistance, heat buildup, and sidewall flex — and the dashboard said nothing. The warning is calibrated for catastrophic loss, not gradual decline. The consumer who sees no warning light assumes the tires are fine. The tires may be 20 percent underinflated without triggering the system designed to monitor them.

Piece #1539: The Weather

October. Thomas checks his tires. Thirty-two PSI, matching the placard. December. The TPMS light appears. He checks: 28 PSI. No leak. No puncture. No damage. The air simply contracted. Tire pressure changes approximately one PSI for every ten degrees Fahrenheit of ambient temperature change. A tire inflated to 32 PSI in 70-degree October reads 28 PSI in 30-degree December — a twelve percent drop from temperature alone. Thomas drives to the gas station, inflates to 32, and by March the tires read 35. He did not overinflate. The weather did. The placard specifies a cold inflation pressure. "Cold" means the car has not been driven and the tires are at ambient temperature. Thomas checks his tires after driving to the gas station, when friction and flexion have warmed the air inside, and the reading is already two to four PSI above cold. The placard gives one number. The tire contains a variable. The consumer measures the variable and compares it to the constant and the comparison is never quite right.

Piece #1540: The Unit

Thomas's tire gauge reads PSI — pounds per square inch. The door placard shows 32 PSI and, in smaller text, 220 kPa. European cars show bar: 2.2 bar. All three are the same pressure. The consumer in Detroit reads 32 and inflates by counting clicks on the gas station compressor. The consumer in Munich reads 2.2 and inflates to the same physical condition. Neither consumer converts. Both consumers are correct. The unit is a wrapper around the same physics, and the number changes with the wrapper. 32 PSI is a different number from 220 kPa is a different number from 2.2 bar, and the air inside the tire does not know which number it is.
