Part CDXIX: The Condition

Piece #2536: The Number

Thomas encountered the number in a textbook. Sixty-five percent.

Sixty-five percent of ordinary people will obey orders to deliver potentially lethal electric shocks to a stranger. Stanley Milgram, Yale University, 1963. The finding that explained the Holocaust. The finding that explained Abu Ghraib. The finding that explained human nature.

Thomas looked for the denominator. Forty. The number was twenty-six out of forty — forty men who had responded to a newspaper advertisement at Yale University in 1961, three months after Eichmann's trial began in Jerusalem. The number performed a law about humanity. The sample was forty volunteers at a prestigious university in Connecticut.

But it was not the sample size that concealed. It was the condition number. Sixty-five percent was Condition Five. There were twenty-three conditions.

Piece #2537: The Range

Milgram ran twenty-three variations of the experiment. Each changed one variable. The compliance rates:

Condition 14 — participants choose their own shock level: 2.5%. One person out of forty.

Condition 7 — two confederates refuse to continue: 10%. Four out of forty.

Condition 4 — participant must physically press the learner's hand onto the plate: 30%.

Condition 5 — the baseline, the one that became the textbook: 65%.

Condition 13 — participant performs an ancillary role while someone else administers the shock: 92.5%.

The range was 2.5% to 92.5%. The overall average across twenty-one conditions was 43.6%. Not 65%.

Thomas sat with the range. The data showed that human moral behavior varies enormously — sensitive to proximity, to social modeling, to institutional framing, to the diffusion of responsibility. The data showed variability. The headline erased variability. Sixty-five percent became the answer. The range became invisible.

Piece #2538: The Prod

The experimenter used four scripted prods when participants hesitated:

Prod 1: "Please continue." Prod 2: "The experiment requires that you continue." Prod 3: "It is absolutely essential that you continue." Prod 4: "You have no other choice, you must go on."

Gibson analyzed the recordings. Prod 4 — the only prod that resembled a direct order — was used twenty-three times across two conditions. It resulted in two continuations. Twenty-one of twenty-three subjects defied the order.

Thomas read the sentence again. The only prod that sounded like an order was the one people refused. The prods that worked were the ones that invoked scientific duty — "the experiment requires" — not the one that invoked authority. The study that became the proof of obedience to orders actually showed the failure of direct orders. The participants who continued did not obey — they believed they were serving science.

Haslam and Reicher called it "engaged followership." Not blind obedience. Identification with the experimenter's project. The participants thought what they were doing was right — not because an authority told them to, but because they believed the research mattered. The obedience was not obedience. It was conviction.

Piece #2539: The Belief

Perry tracked down the original participants. She examined Milgram's own post-experimental questionnaire:

56% fully believed the shocks were real. 24% had some doubts but believed. 11% had doubts and thought deception unlikely. 6% weren't sure. 2.4% were certain the shocks were not real.

Then the inversion. Perry and colleagues analyzed unpublished data from Milgram's own assistant and found: in eighteen of twenty-three conditions, participants who fully believed the shocks were real administered lower shock levels than those who were skeptical. People who believed they were inflicting genuine pain were 2.57 times more likely to defy the experimenter.

Thomas let the number sit. The standard narrative: people obeyed despite knowing they were hurting someone. The data: people who knew they were hurting someone stopped. People who suspected it was fake continued. The sixty-five percent figure was partly an artifact of disbelief, not a measurement of obedience.

The study that proved people will harm strangers when ordered actually showed that people who believe they are harming strangers refuse.

Piece #2540: The Analogy

Milgram wrote: "Ordinary people, simply doing their jobs, and without any particular hostility on their part, can become agents in a terrible destructive process." He wrote that his findings "may well account for the dutiful destructiveness of the dispassionate bureaucrat who may have shipped Jews to Auschwitz with the same degree of routinization as potatoes to Bremerhaven."

Cesarani examined Eichmann's actual biography and found: ideological commitment, creative initiative in persecution, pride in his work, awareness of what he was doing. Not a desk clerk following orders. An architect of policy. Arendt had attended only the early days of the trial, when Eichmann strategically presented himself as ordinary. She took the performance at face value.

Fenigstein noted: unlike Milgram's participants, most Nazi perpetrators showed no remorse. When given opportunities to withdraw from killing, very few chose to do so. Many went beyond their orders. "The vast majority of those who did the killing believed it was just and necessary."

Thomas considered the gap between the experiment and its analogy. A one-hour encounter with a stranger at Yale. A decade of ideological indoctrination, institutional reward, social pressure, and voluntary participation. The laboratory compressed the Holocaust to a voltage dial. The analogy performed the explanation. The explanation was more comforting than the truth, because "obedience" can be cured with better institutions, while "belief" cannot.

Four hundred and nineteen Parts. Twenty-three conditions. One headline. The selection was the concealment. The condition was the law. The law was one data point. The data point was the parable.
