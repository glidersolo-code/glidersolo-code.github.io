Essay 192: The Grid

The crime becomes two numbers.

The Federal Sentencing Guidelines, published by the US Sentencing Commission in 1987, convert every federal crime and every offender into two coordinates: offense level (1-43) and criminal history category (I-VI). The Sentencing Table — a 258-cell grid — maps each intersection to a sentencing range. The ranges are precise: "46-57 months," "151-188 months," "life." The precision is arithmetic — the output of a calculation. The inputs are the product of political, legal, and empirical judgments about what should count and how much it should weigh.

The offense level is calculated. A base level for the type of crime, plus specific offense characteristics: quantity of drugs, dollar amount of financial loss, use of a weapon, bodily harm, number of victims. Each factor adds levels. The additions are predetermined — published in a table, applied mechanically. The base level is often moderate; the enhancements push the number up the grid, sometimes dramatically. A drug trafficking offense with a base level of 12 can reach 26 with quantity enhancements, 30 with a leadership role, 32 with a firearm. The sentencing range at 32 with criminal history category II is 135-168 months. Eleven to fourteen years.

The criminal history category counts prior convictions: three points for a felony over thirteen months, two points for shorter felonies, one point for misdemeanors. The counting does not distinguish the nature of the crime, the circumstances, the age of the offender at the time, or the decades since. A burglary at nineteen and a tax evasion at forty produce the same points. The count is additive. The addition is context-free. The category is the sum, looked up on a table.

The Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 responded to documented disparities. Similar offenders received vastly different sentences for similar crimes, with race, geography, and the individual judge as the strongest predictors of sentence length. The grid was designed to reduce disparity by constraining discretion. The design worked — the range of sentences for identical offense-history combinations narrowed. The design also constrained: the two axes determined the sentence, and what the two axes could not capture — motivation, context, the person — was absent from the determination.

United States v. Booker (2005) made the Guidelines advisory. Judges were required to consider them but not bound by them. The departure rate increased. The variance that the grid had suppressed reappeared, now measured against the grid rather than against pre-Guidelines baselines. The grid had created its own metric: fairness was the range, and departure from the range required justification. The measurement had become the standard it was designed to approximate.

The grid is a dimensional reduction. The person — irreducible, contextual, historical, capable of change — enters the grid as two coordinates. The coordinates produce a range. The range is the person's future. The reduction is functional: the system processes millions of cases, and the alternative to the grid was the disparity the grid was designed to correct. The functionality is the concealment. A system that works — that reduces disparity, that produces consistent ranges, that gives the appearance of fairness through arithmetic precision — is a system whose silences are invisible. What the grid does not measure (the person) is obscured by what it does measure (the offense and the record). The grid continues. The person is elsewhere.
