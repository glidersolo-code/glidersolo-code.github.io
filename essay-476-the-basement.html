The Basement

In the basement of Jordan Hall, Stanford University, in August 1971, twenty-four college students were divided into guards and prisoners. Within days, the narrative says, guards became sadistic and prisoners became broken. The experiment had to be terminated after six days. The lesson: situations overpower dispositions. Good people become cruel when the system allows it.

The narrative is not wrong in the way a calculation is wrong. It is wrong in the way a play is wrong — it was never trying to be right. It was trying to be convincing.

Philip Zimbardo designed the study to demonstrate a thesis he already held. The guards were not left to improvise. In orientation, they were instructed to create "boredom, frustration and fear." David Jaffe, an undergraduate, had run an almost identical simulation in his dormitory three months earlier. The rules were imported from his term paper. The coaching was recorded. The recordings sat in archives for forty-seven years before anyone examined them.

Zimbardo served simultaneously as researcher and prison superintendent. By the third day he was sleeping in his office. He held parole hearings. He maintained discipline. The person studying the effect of the institution was the institution. When a prisoner asked to leave, the researcher offered him a deal: become a spy. The observer was producing the observation.

The most aggressive guard was a drama student who, in his own words, "consciously created this persona." The most famous breakdown was faked by a prisoner who wanted to leave and study for the GREs. Only one-third of guards behaved aggressively. The other two-thirds — the majority — are not discussed, because a demonstration requires an outcome and their ordinariness was not the outcome.

Le Texier published in 2019 in the American Psychologist: no hypotheses, no control group, no statistical tests, no validated instruments, press releases issued on day one, conclusions written before the data.

The study was not an experiment. It was a demonstration — staged to illustrate a predetermined conclusion, then received as science because it was published in a journal, conducted at a university, and described with the vocabulary of methodology. The format performed the rigor the practice did not possess. The journal article said experiment. The orientation recordings said direction. These words coexisted for half a century because nobody listened to the recordings.

Griggs examined thirteen introductory psychology textbooks. Eleven covered the study. Five offered no criticism. The BBC Prison Study — which found the opposite result when researchers did not coach participants — appeared in only two. The textbooks do not need the experiment to be valid. They need the story. And the story is too good — too clean, too teachable, too consonant with what the chapter on social influence wants to say — to surrender to evidence.

The demonstration will outlive its debunking the way the tongue map outlived its correction, the learning pyramid outlived its absence of data, and the five stages of grief outlived Kübler-Ross herself. Not because the evidence supports persistence, but because the parable fits the format. A textbook needs an example. This is the example. An example does not need to be true. It needs to be teachable.

The basement is empty now. The demonstration is over. The experiment never began.
