The Flood

The name says: once per century. The mathematics says: 1% per year, every year, independently. The same number produces two framings. One sounds like a calendar. The other sounds like a coin flip that never remembers its previous result. The calendar framing reached the public. The coin-flip framing remained in the hydrology textbooks.

Emil Julius Gumbel fled Nazi Germany and arrived in the United States in 1941, where he published "The Return Period of Flood Flows" in the Annals of Mathematical Statistics. His extreme value distribution formalized what engineers had been estimating informally: the statistical frequency of rare events. The recurrence interval T = 1/P converts annual exceedance probability into a reciprocal. For the 1% annual flood, T = 100. The reciprocal is a number. Somewhere between the equation and the public, the number became a noun: the hundred-year flood. The noun became a schedule.

The probability of experiencing at least one 100-year flood during a 30-year mortgage is 1 − (0.99)³⁰ = 26.03%. One in four. Not the kind of number that sounds like a century of safety. But the name — the hundred-year flood — sounds exactly like a century of safety. It sounds like a waiting period. It sounds like the river remembers what it did last year and will politely refrain from doing it again for ninety-nine more.

The river does not remember. Each year's probability is independent. After a 100-year flood, the probability of another one next year is still 1%. The gambler's fallacy — the belief that a rare event having occurred makes it less likely to recur — is not a bug in the name. It is the name. The name converts a stationary probability into a temporal interval and thereby converts statistical reasoning into temporal reasoning. Temporal reasoning has a waiting period. Statistical reasoning does not.

The National Flood Insurance Act of 1968 chose the 1% threshold. The choice was administrative — a workable cutoff for insurance mandates, not a scientific determination of acceptable risk. FEMA drew lines on maps. Inside the line: mandatory flood insurance for federally-backed mortgages. Outside the line: voluntary. The line performs certainty. The underlying hydrology performs probability. A quarter of the maps are 15 or more years old. Congress directed FEMA in 2012 to incorporate future climate conditions. As of 2025: not implemented.

Hurricane Harvey dropped over a meter of rain on Harris County in three days. Of 154,170 homes that flooded, 68% were outside the 100-year floodplain. The map said they were safe. The name said they had a century of waiting between floods. The rain did not consult the map.
