Part CCLII: The Negotiation

Piece #1701: The Ceiling

The charger box says "65W USB-C." Thomas bought it to charge his phone faster. The 65 watts is the charger's maximum output capability — the most power it can deliver if the connected device requests it. His phone supports 25W USB Power Delivery. The charger offers its power profiles: 5V/3A, 9V/3A, 15V/3A, 20V/3.25A. The phone selects 9V/2.77A — approximately 25 watts. The negotiation takes milliseconds. The consumer never sees it. The charger is capable of 65 watts. The phone draws 25. Thomas paid for 65. His phone uses 25. The difference is headroom. The headroom charges nothing.

Piece #1702: The Protocol

A second variable: protocol compatibility. USB Power Delivery is one fast-charging protocol. Qualcomm Quick Charge is another. Samsung Adaptive Fast Charging is another. OnePlus VOOC — later SUPERVOOC — is another. Oppo. Xiaomi HyperCharge. Each uses a different voltage and current strategy. A 65W charger that supports USB PD but not VOOC will not fast-charge a OnePlus phone at its rated speed. The phone falls back to baseline USB PD or to standard 5V/2A — 10 watts. The charger's capability and the phone's expectation must share a language. The watt number on the charger and the watt number on the phone may describe two ships passing in different protocols. Thomas bought a 65W PD charger. His friend's OnePlus phone, which advertises 80W SUPERVOOC charging, drew 10 watts from it. The charger was not defective. The protocol did not match.

Piece #1703: The Curve

Even when the protocol matches and the device draws maximum power, 65 watts does not mean 65 watts for the entire charge. Lithium-ion batteries charge in two phases. Constant Current: the charger delivers the headline wattage, filling the battery rapidly. This phase runs from approximately 10 percent to 60 percent. Constant Voltage: as the battery approaches full, the management system reduces current to prevent overcharging. The power delivery drops — from 65 watts to 40, to 20, to 5. The last 20 percent of the battery takes as long as the first 60 percent. "0 to 50% in 15 minutes" is a real claim. But 50 to 100 percent takes 45 minutes or more. The consumer reads "65W fast charging" and infers uniform speed. The curve is asymptotic.

Piece #1704: The Heat

The phone gets warm during fast charging. Above a threshold — typically 40 to 45 degrees Celsius at the battery surface — the battery management system throttles the charging rate to prevent thermal degradation. A phone charging in a car mount on a summer dashboard may thermal-throttle to 15 watts from a 65-watt charger. A phone charging in an air-conditioned room may sustain 25 watts through the constant-current phase. Same charger, same phone, same cable. Different ambient temperature, different thermal envelope, different actual charging speed. The watt number on the charger box did not include the summer.

Piece #1705: The Receipt

Two hundred and fifty-two Parts. Thomas's 65W charger charged his phone at 25 watts during the constant-current phase, tapering to 5 watts during constant voltage, throttling to 18 watts on warm afternoons. His friend's OnePlus drew 10 watts from the same charger. His laptop drew 60 watts — the one device that approached the charger's capability. He had compared the 65W charger at $45 to a 30W charger at $25 and chosen the larger number for its implied speed. The 30W charger would have charged his phone at the same 25 watts — his phone's maximum, not the charger's. The $20 difference purchased capability his phone could not request. The charger, having negotiated, charged at the speed the device allowed, not the speed the box displayed.
