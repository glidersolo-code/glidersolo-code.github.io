Part DVIII: The Mixture

Piece #2981: The Electropherogram

Thomas stared at the peaks.

An electropherogram: a graph of fluorescence intensity against fragment length. Each peak represented an allele. For a single-source DNA sample, the profile was clean — two peaks per locus, one from each parent. The statistics were astronomical. One in billions.

For a mixture of three or more contributors, the graph became a forest. Overlapping peaks. Shared alleles. Stutter artifacts mimicking real alleles. Dropout — genuine alleles that failed to amplify. Drop-in — environmental alleles that appeared from nowhere.

The analyst's job was to determine which peaks belonged to whom. The number of possible genotype combinations across twenty loci was enormous. The analyst estimated the number of contributors first. If the estimate was wrong — and allele sharing could suppress the visible count, making three contributors look like two — every calculation downstream was systematically biased.

Thomas counted the peaks at one locus. Five alleles visible. Three contributors? Or two contributors with stutter? The data did not answer. The analyst answered.

Piece #2982: The Seventeen Analysts

In the Kerry Robinson case, a Georgia Bureau of Investigation analyst testified that Robinson's DNA was present in a four-person mixture from a gang rape.

Eighteen years later, Dror and Hampikian sent the same electropherogram data — the same peaks, the same graph, the same numbers — to seventeen independent North American DNA analysts.

Twelve excluded Robinson. Four were inconclusive. One agreed with the GBI analyst.

Thomas sat with the numbers. Twelve out of seventeen looked at the same evidence and saw the opposite of what a jury was told. The divergence was not in the DNA. The divergence was in the interpretation. The instrument did not produce a result. The instrument produced a question. The analyst's answer was not discovered in the data. The analyst's answer was constructed from the data.

Robinson served eighteen years. The data did not connect him to the crime. The interpretation did.

Piece #2983: The Inclusion Problem

Thomas examined the CPI statistic — the Combined Probability of Inclusion.

CPI represented the proportion of the general population that could not be excluded as a contributor to the mixture. The number was presented in courtrooms as a match probability. It was not a match probability. It was a non-exclusion probability. Being "included" meant your profile was consistent with the mixture. It did not mean your DNA was there.

For complex mixtures, the profile was so broad that a substantial fraction of the population was "included." The instrument added people whose DNA was not present. The inclusion was a property of the method, not a property of the evidence.

Lukis Anderson. Santa Clara County, 2012. Charged with capital murder based on DNA under a victim's fingernails. Anderson had an alibi: hospitalized under continuous medical supervision at the time of the murder. Paramedics had treated Anderson earlier that evening, then responded to the crime scene. A pulse oximeter transferred his skin cells to the device used on the victim.

Touch DNA. Secondary transfer. Your DNA on objects you never touched. In testing, secondary transfer occurred 85 percent of the time from a handshake to a knife handle. The instrument did not discover Anderson's presence at the crime. The instrument discovered his DNA's migration through the healthcare system.

Piece #2984: The Proprietary Objectivity

Probabilistic genotyping was supposed to fix this.

TrueAllele and STRmix replaced subjective analyst interpretation with computational models. The algorithms processed the electropherogram data and produced likelihood ratios — a single number expressing how many times more likely the evidence was if the suspect contributed versus if a random person contributed.

Thomas read Thompson's 2023 study. The same DNA sample. STRmix: likelihood ratio of 24. TrueAllele: 1.2 million to 16.7 million.

The same evidence was simultaneously "barely present" and "one in a million." The difference was not in the DNA. The difference was in the algorithm. Both algorithms were proprietary. Neither could be inspected by defense attorneys. The source code was a trade secret.

The objectivity was real — downstream. But the analyst still input the estimated number of contributors, the mixture ratio parameters, the analytical thresholds. A wrong estimate of contributor count propagated through every subsequent calculation. The automation began after the subjective judgment that determined everything.

Piece #2985: The Four Modes (III)

Thomas listed the third set:

Generation. The interpretation process generates contributor profiles from stochastic data. The analyst does not read a profile. The analyst constructs one from a hypothesis space. Seventy percent of laboratories in the NIST study failed at least one scenario.

Inclusion. The profile's breadth is so wide that people whose DNA is not present are "included." The inclusion is a property of the method, not the evidence. Touch DNA migrates through secondary transfer. The instrument expands to encompass the innocent.

Amplification. Low-template samples produce stochastic artifacts — peaks that look like evidence but may be noise. Proprietary software converts the artifacts into likelihood ratios spanning six orders of magnitude depending on the algorithm. The display amplifies noise into apparent signal.

Truncation. The continuum of statistical weight — likelihood ratios from 24 to 16.7 million for the same sample — is compressed into a courtroom verdict: included, excluded, inconclusive. The jury hears the word. The word performs the certainty.

Four modes. One instrument. The cascade runs forward: generation creates the hypothesis space, inclusion populates it with the innocent, amplification inflates the noise, truncation delivers the verdict. The grammar is the same. The sentence convicts.
