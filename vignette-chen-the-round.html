Chen and the Round

Chen was watching a golf tournament on television when her father mentioned that he'd played only nine holes that morning. "Just the front nine," he said. "Didn't have time for a full round."

"Why is a full round eighteen?" Chen asked.

Her father looked at her as though she'd asked why water was wet. "It just is. That's how many holes a golf course has."

"But why eighteen? Why not fifteen? Or twenty?"

The paragraph at 0.8 — the paragraph she kept suppressing — would have said that the number has no derivation. That the Old Course at St Andrews originally had twelve holes played in two directions, making twenty-two. That in 1764, the Society of St Andrews Golfers decided four short holes were too short and combined them into two. That twenty-two minus four is eighteen. That the entire basis for the number — the single most defining structural feature of the sport — is one sentence in one meeting's minutes about four holes on one strip of Scottish coastline.

The paragraph would have said that other courses at the time had five holes, or seven, or twelve, or twenty-five. That there was no standard because each course was its landscape. That eighteen became the standard not because anyone argued it was optimal but because the club that happened to have eighteen was the oldest, most prestigious club. That by 1892 the Open Championship required four rounds of eighteen, and any course that wanted to matter had to comply.

The paragraph would have said that no one has ever conducted a study of the optimal number of holes for a round of golf. No ergonomic analysis, no game-theory model, no measurement of player endurance or daylight hours or competitive fairness. The number was inherited from one course's topography, emulated through prestige, and codified into rule without ever being examined.

Chen's father returned his attention to the screen. A golfer was lining up a putt on the seventeenth hole. The seventeenth of eighteen. The number felt inevitable — like the only number golf could possibly have. Which was, Chen thought, exactly how a number survives without derivation.
