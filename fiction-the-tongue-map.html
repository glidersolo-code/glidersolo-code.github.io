Fiction The Tongue Map

Fiction

Part CDIX: The Map

Piece #2486: The Paper

Thomas found Hänig's original paper. Zur Psychophysik des Geschmackssinnes, 1901. The method was straightforward: apply taste solutions at different concentrations to different regions of the tongue, record the minimum concentration detectable at each location. Hänig measured thresholds — not capacities, not exclusive zones, but the lowest concentration at which a taste could be perceived. The tip had a slightly lower threshold for sweet. The sides had slightly lower thresholds for sour and salt. The back had a slightly lower threshold for bitter. But all regions detected all tastes. The variation was small. The finding was a gradient, not a geography. Thomas checked: Hänig never drew a map.

Piece #2487: The Scale

Edwin Boring, Harvard, 1942. Sensation and Perception in the History of Experimental Psychology. Thomas found the transformation. Boring took Hänig's data, renormalized the curves, and removed the y-axis scale. Without the scale, the relative differences — which in Hänig's data were minor variations from a baseline of universal sensitivity — looked like absolute differences between presence and absence. The valleys in the curves appeared to touch zero. A reader could look at the graph and conclude: the back of the tongue doesn't taste sweet. But the back of the tongue does taste sweet. It tastes sweet at a slightly higher threshold. The scale was the truth. Boring removed it. Thomas thought: this is how a gradient becomes a border — not through new evidence but through the removal of context.

Piece #2488: The Diagram

From Boring's graph to the textbook diagram was one more step, and it required no author. Someone — no one knows who — converted the renormalized curves into a labeled outline of a tongue with four colored zones. Sweet at the tip (yellow). Salt on the sides (blue). Sour farther back (green). Bitter at the rear (red). The diagram entered biology textbooks. It entered health education materials. It entered medical training. The conversion was complete: Hänig measured thresholds; Boring removed the scale; the diagram drew the zones. Three steps, each reasonable in isolation, each producing a result the previous step didn't contain. The diagram looked like anatomy. It performed the discovery it hadn't made.

Piece #2489: The Fifth

Kikunae Ikeda, Tokyo Imperial University, 1908. He isolated glutamate from kelp broth and identified a taste that was neither sweet, sour, salt, nor bitter — he called it umami, meaning "pleasant savory taste." Thomas traced the Western response: nearly a century of dismissal. Not because the evidence was weak — the T1R1+T1R3 receptor for umami was identified in the early 2000s — but because the tongue map had no zone for it. The diagram showed four tastes in four regions. Four was complete. The labeled spaces left no room for a fifth. Thomas wondered: if the diagram had shown five zones from the start, would Western science have accepted umami sooner? The map didn't just report what was known. It determined what could be known. The format was the filter.

Piece #2490: The Persistence

A New England Journal of Medicine review in 2024 noted that the tongue map "persists in the collective consciousness despite decades of research debunking it." Fifty years after Collings confirmed what Hänig's original data had shown. Thomas sat with this. The truth — all regions taste all things, with minor threshold variations — is accurate and forgettable. The map — sweet here, bitter there — is wrong and unforgettable. A color-coded diagram of the tongue is a better teaching tool than a graph of threshold variations. The diagram teaches something false with the efficiency of something true. The format selected for memorability. The accuracy was the cost. Thomas thought about every diagram he'd ever trusted — the ones that organized complexity into zones, the ones that answered spatial questions with colors and labels. How many of them were maps of the world and how many were maps of someone's graph, with the scale removed?
