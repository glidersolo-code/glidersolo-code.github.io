Part CDXCV: The Night

Piece #2916: The Walk

Thomas reads about the count. One night in January. The last ten calendar days. Volunteers walk pre-assigned zones between midnight and dawn. They carry clipboards or tablets. They count bodies in doorways, tents in parks, figures curled in vehicles. Each Continuum of Care — there are approximately 400 — conducts its own count, using its own methodology. HUD permits variation. The results are aggregated into a single national number. In January 2024: 771,480. Thomas looks at the number. It has the specificity of a census. It has the authority of a count. It was produced by volunteers walking in the dark.

Piece #2917: The Definition

Thomas reads the eligibility criteria. HUD counts "literal homelessness": people in emergency shelters, transitional housing, and places not meant for human habitation. Thomas reads what is excluded. People doubled up — sharing housing with friends or relatives because they cannot afford their own. People in jails on count night, even if they were sleeping on the street the week before and will return to the street the week after. People in hospitals. People in psychiatric facilities.

Thomas finds the comparison. The McKinney-Vento Education program uses a broader definition that includes doubling up. It counted 1.4 million homeless students in recent school years. The PIT found 110,000 people under 18. Thomas does the ratio. Thirteen to one. The same population. The same country. Two definitions. One definition is thirteen times the size of the other. The number did not change. The boundary changed. The boundary is the number.

Piece #2918: The Season

Thomas reads about January. The count happens in January because the federal reporting cycle requires it. In January, Minneapolis is below zero. In January, Los Angeles is sixty degrees. Thomas reads Peter Rossi's Chicago data from 1985. The unsheltered population fell 62% from fall to winter. Not because people stopped being homeless. Because cold weather pushed them indoors — into shelters, where they were counted differently. Into warming centers. Into hospital emergency rooms, where they were counted not at all.

New York City has a legal right to shelter. On count night, 95% of its homeless population is indoors. Los Angeles has no such right. On count night, 70% of its homeless population is outdoors. Thomas reads the comparison that puts these numbers side by side — LA's homelessness dwarfing New York's. He reads it again. The comparison is not measuring homelessness. It is measuring January, and climate, and law.

Piece #2919: The Snapshot

Thomas reads Dennis Culhane's research on shelter use patterns. Most people who enter shelters stay briefly — days or weeks, not months. They exit, sometimes to housing, sometimes to the street, sometimes to doubled-up situations that the count does not recognize. But on any single night, the long-stay residents are overrepresented because they are always there. If half of all shelter-bed-nights are consumed by 10% of users, then on any given night, half the people counted are from that 10%.

Thomas reads the annual comparison. In 2013, the PIT found 591,768 people. Over the course of the year, 1,422,360 people used shelters — 2.4 times as many. A study in Demographic Research estimated total annual homelessness at 3.4 times the PIT figure. Bruce Link's household survey found 7.4% of the US population had experienced literal homelessness in their lifetime. Thomas writes the numbers down. One night: 600,000. One year: 2,000,000. One lifetime: 20,000,000. The one-night number frames homelessness as a condition. The annual number frames it as a flow. The lifetime number frames it as a structural risk. Three numbers. Three framings. Three policy responses. The PIT chose one.

Piece #2920: The Funding

Thomas reads the funding formula. The CoC Program distributes $3.2 billion annually. PIT data is a primary input. Communities that undercount receive less money. Communities that improve their counting methodology — that send more volunteers, cover more territory, refine their instruments — discover more homeless people. The discovery performs as deterioration. Better counting looks like worse outcomes. The reward for accuracy is the appearance of failure.

Thomas reads a line from HUD's guidance: individuals counted in extended definitions "will not be considered by HUD when determining a CoC's level of need." The doubled-up families. The 1.4 million students. The population the definition excludes generates zero federal dollars. Thomas reads the sentence again. The definition draws the boundary. The boundary draws the funding. The funding draws the policy. The policy addresses the population the count made visible and ignores the population the definition made invisible. The invisibility is not accidental. The invisibility is structural. The definition is the policy.
