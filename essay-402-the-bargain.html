The Bargain

Ninety-seven percent of federal criminal convictions are obtained through guilty pleas. Approximately 94% of state felony convictions. The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to "a speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury." That right is exercised in 3% of federal cases. In 1962, federal district court judges averaged 21 criminal trials per year. By 2015: four. The constitutional right has not been repealed. It has been priced.

The trial penalty is the price. Federal trial sentences are roughly three times higher than plea sentences for the same offense, and sometimes eight to ten times higher. In federal drug cases (2012): average sentence after trial was 16 years; after plea, 5 years and 4 months. First-time drug defendants facing mandatory minimums: trial sentence averaged 117.6 months; plea sentence 59.5 months. The Sentencing Guidelines embed the penalty in their architecture: a two-level offense level reduction for "acceptance of responsibility," granted to 96.3% of those who plead guilty. Going to trial forfeits the reduction. The phrase "acceptance of responsibility" performs contrition — the defendant acknowledges wrongdoing. The phrase functions as a surcharge on the Sixth Amendment. The system does not merely permit lighter sentences for pleas. It structurally punishes the exercise of the constitutional right to trial.

Paul Lewis Hayes forged a check for $88.30. The prosecutor offered five years if Hayes pleaded guilty. If Hayes did not plead guilty and "save the court the inconvenience and necessity of a trial," the prosecutor told him he would refile under the Kentucky Habitual Criminal Act: mandatory life imprisonment. Hayes refused. Hayes went to trial. Hayes was convicted. Sentenced to life. The Supreme Court upheld it in Bordenkircher v. Hayes (1978). The threat — five years or life — was constitutionally permissible. The word the Court used was "bargaining." The leverage was infinity.

The innocence problem follows from the structure. The National Registry of Exonerations documents 839 wrongful convictions — 24% of all documented exonerations — where the defendant pleaded guilty to a crime the defendant did not commit. In Harris County, Texas, 157 people pleaded guilty to drug charges when the substances were not drugs at all. Estimated wrongful conviction rate: 4-6% of all incarcerated people. Pretrial detention is the coercion multiplier: detained defendants plead guilty at significantly higher rates; release before trial reduces the probability of pleading guilty by 10.8-11.8 percentage points. More than 500,000 unconvicted people sit in jails awaiting trial. In misdemeanor courts, innocent people plead guilty to go home. The choice — plead guilty and leave tonight, or maintain innocence and wait months in jail — is presented as voluntary. The Constitution requires it be "voluntary, knowing, and intelligent." The jail cell is what the word "voluntary" conceals.

Racial disparities structure the system at the point of maximum discretion. White defendants are 25% more likely than Black defendants to have their most serious charge dropped or reduced. In misdemeanor cases: 75% more likely to have imprisonment-carrying charges dismissed. Among defendants with no prior criminal history — where prosecutorial discretion is widest and data thinnest — the racial gap is greatest. The prosecutors filling the gap are using race as the available proxy.

The word is "bargain." The word performs a marketplace of equals — offer and counteroffer, buyer and seller, mutual benefit. The function performs coercion: a prosecutor holding mandatory minimums and charge-stacking authority over a defendant who may be innocent, cannot afford bail, and whose lawyer may never have communicated the offer. The process occurs off the record — no transcript, no public scrutiny, no prosecutorial guidelines shared with the defense. Brady v. United States (1970) validated the system but warned: "should the plea bargaining system begin to operate in a manner resulting in a significant number of innocent defendants pleading guilty," the Court would be forced to reexamine its constitutionality. Eight hundred and thirty-nine documented cases suggest the condition has been met. The Court has not revisited it. The word "bargain" performs what the system denies: equality. The parties are not bargaining. One party is threatening. The other is complying.
