Part CCLXXXVIII: The Flag

Piece #1881: The Survey

Thomas sits with the 137-question instrument.

Question 12: How many times have you been arrested? He counts. The number enters a field. The field accepts integers. The integer does not accept footnotes.

He wants to write: three times. Once for possession in a park where the undercover officer was stationed because the precinct had a quota. Once for a broken taillight that became a search that became a charge. Once for being in the apartment when they came for someone else.

The field accepts: 3.

He moves to question 24: Age at first arrest? He was sixteen. The school resource officer referred a hallway argument to the juvenile court. In the school across town — the one without a resource officer — the same argument would have ended with detention.

The field accepts: 16.

Question 47: Criminal associates? His brother. His cousin. His neighbor from childhood. All arrested. All in the same neighborhood. All policed by the same precinct that policed more intensely because the data said the neighborhood was high-crime because the arrests were high because the policing was intense.

The field accepts a count. The count does not record the loop.

Piece #1882: The Decile

Thomas receives his score. Seven out of ten. High risk.

He asks the algorithm what a 7 means. The algorithm says: among all defendants who scored 7, approximately 60% reoffended within two years. This is true for Black defendants and white defendants equally. The score means the same thing regardless of race.

He asks a different question. Among all Black defendants who did NOT reoffend, how many were scored high-risk? The answer: 44.9%. Among white defendants who did not reoffend: 23.5%.

He is nearly twice as likely to be wrongly flagged.

He asks: can the algorithm fix this? Chouldechova's theorem says no. When base rates differ — and they do, because policing produces different arrest rates — you cannot equalize the false positive rate and the predictive value simultaneously. The mathematics does not permit it. You must choose which error to tolerate. The choice is a value judgment. The score presents it as a fact.

Piece #1883: The Mirror

Thomas reads Sandra Mayson's paper. "Bias In, Bias Out."

The argument: the problem is not the algorithm. The problem is prediction. An accurate algorithm holds a mirror to the past. If the past is racially disparate — and it is, because policing is racially disparate — then the mirror reflects disparity. The prediction projects the system's history onto individual futures and calls the projection "risk."

Thomas holds the mirror up. What it shows: a system that polices some neighborhoods more than others, arrests some people more than others, convicts some people more than others, and then feeds those outcomes into an algorithm that predicts — accurately — that the people who were policed more will be policed more again.

The mirror does not lie. The mirror reflects a system that does.

He sets the mirror down. The score on his file still says 7.

Piece #1884: The Source Code

Thomas asks to see the source code. He cannot. COMPAS is proprietary. Northpointe — now Equivant, rebranded in January 2017, one year after ProPublica's investigation — owns the algorithm. The defendant whose liberty depends on the score cannot examine the process that produced it.

State v. Loomis, Wisconsin Supreme Court, 2016. Eric Loomis challenged his COMPAS-influenced sentence on due process grounds. The court upheld the use of COMPAS but required five warnings: the score is based on group data, not individual prediction. The score's methodology is proprietary and cannot be verified. The score should not determine whether to incarcerate. The score should not determine the length of sentence. The score should be accompanied by an independent rationale.

Thomas reads the five warnings. Each warning says: do not trust this number. The number remains in the file.

He reads the sentence. Six years of initial confinement. Five years of extended supervision. The judge cited the COMPAS score alongside other factors. The other factors are recorded in the decision. The COMPAS score's contribution cannot be isolated because the proprietary model cannot be examined.

The black box protects the algorithm. The algorithm protects the system. The system produces the data. The data feeds the algorithm.

Piece #1885: The Two Variables

Thomas reads Dressel and Farid. Science Advances, January 2018.

The experiment: recruit untrained people from Amazon Mechanical Turk. Show them brief case descriptions — a paragraph per defendant. Ask: will this person reoffend within two years? Pay them $1 per case. No training. No algorithm. No 137 questions.

Accuracy: 62%. COMPAS accuracy: 65%. Statistically comparable.

Then: build a linear classifier using only two variables. Age. Number of prior convictions. Two inputs. One equation. Accuracy: equivalent to COMPAS.

Thomas writes the implication on the board. The 137 questions, the 15 scales, the proprietary model, the $22,000 implementation fee, the decile score, the presentence report, the five-part warning, the Wisconsin Supreme Court case, the ProPublica investigation, the impossibility theorem, the debate between predictive parity and error rate balance — all of this infrastructure wraps around a prediction that can be approximated by two numbers and an untrained crowd.

The complexity does not improve the prediction. The complexity performs thoroughness. The instrument is elaborate because elaborate instruments are credible, and credible instruments are deferred to, and deferred-to instruments survive challenges, and surviving instruments become infrastructure, and infrastructure becomes invisible.

Thomas erases the board. Two variables remain. Age. Prior convictions. The rest was scenery.
