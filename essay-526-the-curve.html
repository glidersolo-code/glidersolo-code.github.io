Essay 526: The Curve

The Lorenz curve has a shape. It bows below the diagonal — the deeper the bow, the greater the inequality. The curve for the United States bows deeper than Sweden's, shallower than South Africa's. The shape tells a story: where the income accumulates, where it thins, whether the middle is compressed or hollow, whether the gap is at the top or the bottom or both.

The Gini coefficient has no shape. It is a number between 0 and 1. The US is approximately 0.39. Sweden is 0.28. South Africa is 0.63. The number ranks. The number compares. The number fits in a headline, a table cell, a policy brief's opening sentence. The number is the ratio of two areas — the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of perfect equality, divided by the total area under the line. The ratio is correct. The ratio discards the curve.

Two countries with identical Gini coefficients can have completely different inequality structures. One has a large middle class with a very wealthy top percentile — the Lorenz curve bows deeply only at the upper end. The other has a bimodal distribution — two income clusters with a hollow middle, the curve bowing evenly throughout. Both produce a Gini of 0.40. Both are equally unequal, by this measure. Neither country would recognize the other's inequality as resembling its own. The number says they are the same. The curve says they are different societies.

Corrado Gini published the coefficient in 1912. Max Lorenz had published the curve in 1905. The curve came first. The curve was the measurement. The coefficient was the compression. The compression was the point — portability, comparability, the ability to rank 190 countries in a single column. The curve resists ranking. The curve has too many dimensions. The coefficient has one.

Heisig, Schaeffer, and Giesecke demonstrated in 2022 that multi-parameter models consistently outperform the single-parameter Gini in explaining societal outcomes — health, social trust, political stability. The best-fitting model distinguishes between inequality concentrated at the bottom versus the top. The Gini cannot make this distinction. The distinction is the one that matters. Whether a society's inequality means an oligarchic elite or a struggling underclass or a hollowed-out middle — these are different problems with different causes and different remedies. The number that compresses them into a single magnitude erases the geometry that separates them.

The Gini persists not because it is the best measure but because it is the most portable. One decimal. Comparable across borders. Comparable across decades. The format is the reason for the dominance. The format is also the reason for the erasure. The curve has a shape. The shape is where the inequality lives. The coefficient discards the shape and keeps the magnitude. The magnitude is the wrong dimension. The wrong dimension is the one that fits the table.
