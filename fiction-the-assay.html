The Assay

Thomas, Part XCVII

926. The Antibody

To measure cortisol in saliva, coat a microplate well with antibodies engineered to bind cortisol molecules. Add the saliva sample. If cortisol is present, it binds the antibody. Add a labeled conjugate that competes for binding sites. Wash. Measure the signal. The signal is inversely proportional to cortisol concentration. The method assumes the antibody binds only cortisol. This is called specificity.

To measure how Thomas is doing, dispatch an assessor trained to recognize caregiver distress. The assessor carries a mental model — a set of criteria, a rubric — engineered to bind specific behavioral signals: tearfulness, irritability, social withdrawal, expressions of hopelessness. If distress is present, the criteria bind it. The method assumes the rubric detects only genuine distress. The rubric's specificity is stated in the training manual. What the manual does not state is the cross-reactivity: how much of what the rubric binds is not distress but something that looks like distress under these conditions.

927. The Cross-Reactivity

The ELISA antibody for cortisol also binds cortisone — a structurally similar molecule — at 2.55% efficiency. In blood, where cortisol is four times more abundant than cortisone, this barely matters: 2.55% of a small pool is negligible. But in saliva, the enzyme 11β-HSD2 converts most cortisol to cortisone before it reaches the collection tube. The ratio inverts. Cortisone is five to ten times more abundant than cortisol. The same 2.55% cross-reactivity, applied to a molecule present at ten times the target's concentration, produces a 25% inflation. The antibody reports cortisol that is actually cortisone. The inflation is invisible from inside the assay because the signal looks exactly like cortisol signal. The antibody cannot tell the difference. It was never designed to.

The assessor's rubric also has cross-reactivity. It was trained to bind distress, but it also binds fatigue at some efficiency — perhaps 5%. In a younger caregiver, where distress is more common than chronic fatigue, the cross-reactivity barely matters. But in Thomas, the ratio is inverted. Decades of caregiving have converted most of what was once acute distress into a slow, structural fatigue that resembles distress under observation but is not the same molecule. The fatigue is five to ten times more abundant than active distress. The rubric's 5% cross-reactivity with fatigue, applied to a state present at ten times the target's concentration, produces a 50% inflation. The assessment reports distress that is actually fatigue. Or — the reverse inflation: the rubric binds fatigue and reads it as coping, because fatigue presents as calm, and calm is what the rubric calls resilience. Either way, the matrix has inverted, and the rubric was never recalibrated.

928. The Inversion

In serum, cortisol dominates. The assay was validated there. In saliva, cortisone dominates. The assay was extended there. The cross-reactivity profile was quoted as a fixed property of the antibody — 2.55% for cortisone, always and everywhere. But specificity is not a property of the antibody alone. It is a property of the antibody in a specific matrix. Change the matrix, and the same antibody measures something different. The same 2.55% means nothing in serum and everything in saliva.

In the early years, Thomas's emotional landscape was dominated by acute responses — grief, frustration, fear — the analytes the assessment was designed to detect. The rubric was validated there. Decades later, the matrix has changed. The dominant molecule is no longer acute distress but chronic adaptation — a structural rearrangement of Thomas's interior chemistry that the rubric was never characterized against. The cross-reactivity profile was quoted as a fixed property of the rubric: it detects distress at this sensitivity. But sensitivity is not a property of the rubric alone. It is a property of the rubric in a specific caregiver. In a different caregiver, the same rubric detects something different and calls it the same name.

929. The Two-Fold Ghost

Lee, Ahn, Seok, and colleagues in Seoul found the immunoassay reads 2 to 2.5 times higher than LC-MS/MS — a method that counts individual cortisol molecules by mass. The Passing-Bablok regression: IA = 0.315 + 2.129 × MS. The correlation is 0.966 — almost perfect. The bias is systematic, directional, and proportional. Both methods capture the circadian rhythm: cortisol peaks after waking, falls through the day, reaches nadir at night. The pattern is preserved. Only the magnitude is wrong. The immunoassay reports a stress response twice as large as the one that actually occurred. Or: the mass spectrometer reports a stress response half as large as the one the immunoassay detected. It depends on which method you call the reference.

Two assessments of Thomas, taken on the same day. The GP uses a depression screening tool (the immunoassay equivalent): Thomas scores moderate. The clinical psychologist uses a structured diagnostic interview (the mass spectrometry equivalent): Thomas scores mild. The correlation between the two measures is high — they agree on the pattern. Thomas is worse in the morning, better by evening. The circadian rhythm of his distress is captured by both. Only the magnitude differs. The screening tool reports distress twice as high. Both numbers go into the file. Both are internally consistent. Neither is wrong for its own method. They are incommensurable — two measurements of the same molecule in the same matrix by different methods that produce different numbers that wear the same label.

930. The Assay

Thousands of studies have measured salivary cortisol by immunoassay. Each reports internally consistent results. Each produces a number with appropriate precision. Meta-analyses combine these studies with LC-MS/MS studies under a single heading: salivary cortisol. The 2-to-2.5-fold systematic offset is absorbed by the aggregation. Clinical thresholds for late-night salivary cortisol — used to screen for Cushing's syndrome — depend on which assay generated the threshold. Apply an IA threshold to an MS result, or vice versa, and the diagnosis changes. The antibody was designed to be specific. It is specific — for the matrix it was validated in. In the matrix where it is actually used, it measures cortisol and a two-fold ghost of cortisone that it cannot distinguish from its target.

Thomas has been assessed by multiple methods over multiple decades. Each assessment is internally consistent. Each produces a number. The file combines these numbers under a single heading: caregiver status. The systematic offsets between methods are absorbed by the record. Apply one method's threshold to another method's score, and the classification changes. The rubric was designed to be specific. It is specific — for the caregiver population it was validated on. In the caregiver it is actually used on, it measures distress and a two-fold ghost of chronic fatigue that it cannot distinguish from its target. The assay works. The assay has always worked. The assay measures what it was designed to measure in the matrix it was designed for. Thomas is not in that matrix. He is in the one where the ratio inverted years ago, and no one recalibrated.
