Vignette Chen The Wilderness

Vignette

Chen went backpacking in the Sierras the summer after her second year. Three days on the John Muir Trail. She'd earned it. The granite was immense and the silence was real and on the second morning she woke to a meadow so open it looked like someone had swept it clean.

"Pristine," said the trail guide, a seasonal ranger. "This is what it looked like before people got here."

Chen didn't say anything then. She took photographs. She felt the thing you're supposed to feel — smallness, awe, the conviction that the world was older and larger than any human project. The feeling was genuine. She carried it down the mountain.

Back at school, she found Cronon's essay in a seminar reading list. "The Trouble with Wilderness." She read the sentence twice: "The removal of Indians to create an 'uninhabited wilderness' — uninhabited as never before in the human history of the place — reminds us just how invented, just how constructed, the American wilderness really is."

She looked up the Ahwahneechee. Southern Sierra Miwok. The valley that Muir called untouched — they had lived there since the fourteenth century. The Mariposa Battalion drove them out in 1851. The park was established in 1890. The National Park Service evicted the last families in 1969. The meadow she had woken in — that swept-clean meadow — had been maintained by prescribed burning for thousands of years. The openness was not the absence of management. It was its product. The burning stopped when the people were removed. The meadow she'd seen was the remnant of a practice that her government had terminated.

She found Spence next. Dispossessing the Wilderness. The reservation and the park — one institution to contain Indigenous peoples, the other to contain the landscape their removal had produced. "Separate but symbiotic." The word "pristine" was a factual claim about a political act.

The fire data complicated it further. A century without prescribed burning had thickened the forests, choked the understory, accumulated fuel. When the fires came, they were catastrophic — not because nature was reasserting itself, but because the management that had prevented catastrophe had been removed with the managers. And when ecologists proposed reintroducing fire, wilderness law classified it as "trammeling." The word that protected the land from human interference prevented the human interference that had maintained the land.

Chen went back the next summer. Same trail. Same meadow. She noticed, this time, how the forest edge had crept inward. She noticed the density of the understory where once there would have been space. She noticed the dead wood accumulated in layers.

"Still pristine," said a different ranger at the trailhead, smiling.

Chen smiled back. The feeling was still genuine — the granite, the light, the scale. But the word sat differently now. Pristine didn't mean untouched. It meant: the touching has been erased from the record. The landscape was a document. The document had been edited. And the edit had been named "nature."
