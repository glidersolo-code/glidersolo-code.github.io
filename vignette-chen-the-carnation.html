The Carnation

Maren is looking at greeting cards.

"These are eight dollars," she says. "For a piece of folded paper."

Chen is watching from the next aisle. His daughter is with him, studying a display of stuffed animals holding hearts.

"The woman who created Mother's Day," Chen says, "called greeting cards 'maudlin, insincere printed' things that mean nothing except that you're too lazy to write to the woman who has done more for you than anyone else in the world."

Maren puts the card back. "Who created Mother's Day?"

"Anna Jarvis. Her mother died in 1905. She had lost seven of twelve children. Anna never married, never had children herself. She wanted a day where each child wrote a personal note to their own mother. A white carnation. A handwritten line. She insisted on the singular possessive — 'Mother's,' not 'Mothers'.' Your mother. Not all mothers."

"That's—" Maren looks at the aisle of $8 cards and $12.99 bouquets. "That's not this."

"She fought it for twenty-four years. Boycotts, lawsuits, protests. She was arrested at a convention for disturbing the peace. She petitioned to rescind the holiday. She spent her inheritance on legal fees."

"Did she win?"

"She went blind. She went broke. She entered a sanitarium. She spent her last four years there."

"Who paid for it?"

Chen is quiet for a moment.

"The floral and greeting card industries. Secretly. She never knew."

Maren looks at the card display. The daughter has moved on to the candy section. The store is decorated in pink and white. A sign reads: MAKE MOM FEEL SPECIAL. The flowers are marked up 40% for the weekend.

"The apostrophe is still there," Chen says. "On every card. Mother's. Singular possessive. The grammar she specified."

"But—"

"The grammar survived. The meaning didn't."

Maren picks up a different card. It costs $6.99. It says "Mom, you're the best." She opens it. Inside, in a font designed to look handwritten: "Thanks for everything you do."

She puts it back. She takes out her phone and begins typing a note.
