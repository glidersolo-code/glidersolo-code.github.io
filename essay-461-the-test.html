The Test

Three criteria. Does the film have at least two named women? Do they talk to each other? About something other than a man? Check, check, check. Pass. Or fail.

The Bechdel test performs feminist film analysis through the format of a checklist. The format borrows authority from testing and assessment — criteria, binary outcome, verdict. A film that "passes the Bechdel test" sounds evaluated. A film that "fails" sounds condemned. Databases were built. Statistics were compiled. The Swedish film rating system adopted it in 2013, marking passing films with an "A."

The origin was a punchline. In 1985, Alison Bechdel published a comic strip in Dykes to Watch Out For titled "The Rule." Two women walk to a movie. One explains her three conditions. The other says they haven't seen a film in years. The joke was the scarcity. Even this absurdly minimal threshold eliminated most of Hollywood.

The joke became a metric. The transformation happened not through argument but through format. Three criteria fit a spreadsheet. A pass/fail outcome fit a database. The checklist was portable. The close reading was not.

But the test measures whether a specific conversational configuration occurs — not whether the film takes women seriously. Fifty Shades of Grey passes. Two women discuss an apartment. Gravity fails. One named female character, but the film centers entirely on her competence and survival. The checklist captures the conversation. The checklist cannot capture the framing, the centrality, the power dynamics, the gaze.

The compression is the mechanism. Feminist film criticism is a complex practice — attending to visual framing, narrative structure, character agency, audience address, historical context. The checklist compresses all of this into three binary questions. What survives the compression is a conversational configuration that correlates weakly with the qualities the test is understood to measure. What is eliminated is everything the critique was about.

The format won because the format was more useful to institutions than the critique it replaced. Rating boards need checklists. Journalism needs data. Databases need columns. The close reading — Bechdel's actual critical intelligence, deployed across decades of graphic memoir and cultural commentary — does not fit any of these containers. The checklist does.

A joke about absence became a metric about quality. The checklist performs the evaluation. The evaluation was never in the checklist. It was in the joke — in the recognition that even this floor was too high for most films. The floor became the standard. The standard replaced the recognition. And the format — three criteria, binary outcome, portable and compilable — outlived the critique it compressed, because the format served the institution and the institution did not need the critique. It needed the column.
