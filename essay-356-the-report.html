The Report

In 1929, the International Association of Chiefs of Police established the Uniform Crime Reporting program. The stated purpose was partly defensive — to prevent newspapers from manufacturing "crime waves" to boost circulation. The FBI assumed management in September 1930. The program collected crime data from participating police departments, calculated rates per 100,000 population, and published them annually. The number — 312 per 100,000, 450 per 100,000 — became the answer to the question: how much crime is there?

The question the number actually answers is different. The number counts crimes that were reported to police by victims, recorded by responding officers, not unfounded by the department, not suppressed by the hierarchy rule, not excluded by the definition, and submitted to the FBI by an agency that chose to participate. Each step subtracts. The number that emerges is the residue of a chain of institutional decisions, presented as a census of the phenomenon.

Albert Biderman and Albert Reiss named the gap in 1967: the "dark figure of crime." The National Crime Victimization Survey, launched in 1972 to measure what the UCR could not, found the figure was not dark so much as dominant. In 2022: approximately 58% of violent crime went unreported. Approximately 79% of rape and sexual assault went unreported. Approximately 68% of property crime went unreported. The crime rate, at best, reflects between one-third and one-half of what occurred.

The hierarchy rule counts only the most serious offense per incident. A robbery that escalates to assault: only the robbery appears. The less serious offenses vanish by design. The UCR defined rape as "carnal knowledge of a female forcibly and against her will" from 1927 until 2013 — eighty-six years during which the national rape statistic excluded male victims, non-vaginal penetration, and non-forcible rape. The definition was the filter. The filter was invisible because it was the definition.

Police departments can "unfound" reported crimes. In Baltimore, rape reports were classified as baseless 30% of the time. In New Orleans, an Inspector General audit found 41 of 90 sex crime complaints removed from UCR data. During the same period, rape victims seeking hospital treatment increased. The event rose while the statistic fell.

CompStat, introduced in New York in 1994, tracks crime weekly by precinct. The system rewards declining numbers. The incentive is to show fewer crimes, not fewer victims. Kitsuse and Cicourel wrote the methodological epitaph in 1963: "Criminal statistics are indicative of the social organization of the agencies responsible for assembling them." The crime rate describes the institution. The format — per 100,000, three significant figures, published by the FBI — makes it look like a description of the world.

The denominator compounds the distortion. The rate divides crime counts by residential census population. In cities with commuters or tourists, the people actually present — and actually at risk — far exceed the denominator. The rate inflates because the denominator undercounts the population exposed. In January 2021, the FBI mandated a new reporting format. Approximately 40% of agencies — including New York and Los Angeles — failed to submit data. The national crime report for 2021 covered 63% of the population. The number that claims to count all crime was produced by a system in which four out of ten agencies did not participate.

The format performs what the data cannot support. The rate reads as "how much crime there is." It measures how much crime the system recorded. The fraction presents itself as the total. The residue of a reporting chain labels itself as a census. The precision — per 100,000, to several digits — conceals the magnitude of what is missing, which is most of it.
