Part CDLXIII: The Flight Level

Piece #2756: The Capsule That Read Pressure

Thomas is thinking about what the altimeter actually measures. Inside the instrument: a sealed aneroid capsule, a small metal bellows evacuated of air. As atmospheric pressure decreases — as the aircraft climbs — the capsule expands. A mechanical linkage translates the expansion into rotation of a needle on a dial. The dial is marked in feet. The number on the dial says: height. But the capsule does not know height. The capsule knows pressure. The conversion from pressure to feet assumes the International Standard Atmosphere — sea level at 1013.25 hectopascals, temperature 15 degrees Celsius, lapse rate of 6.5 degrees per kilometer. These conditions almost never exist.

Paul Kollsman, working from his attic in Brooklyn in 1928, built the first altimeter sensitive enough for instrument flight. The instrument could resolve within a few feet — in the Standard Atmosphere. On September 24, 1929, Jimmy Doolittle used a Kollsman altimeter to take off, navigate, and land blind at Mitchel Field. The altimeter that enabled instrument flight was a pressure gauge that reported in feet. The feet were fictional. The flight was real.

Thomas thinks about how the fiction was chosen. Not arbitrarily. Pilots need to know how high they are. Pressure is the available signal. Feet is the useful output. The Standard Atmosphere is the translation table between them. The translation is approximate. The approximation is systematic. The fiction was adopted because it works, not because it is true. The distinction between "works" and "is true" is the concealment.

Piece #2757: The Window That Stopped Calibrating

Thomas is thinking about the Kollsman window. A small adjustable dial on the altimeter face, showing the barometric reference setting. Below eighteen thousand feet, the pilot turns the knob to set local sea-level pressure — the current QNH. The altimeter reading then approximates true altitude above mean sea level. The Kollsman window is a calibration tool. The pilot adjusts the instrument to local conditions, and the reading improves.

Above eighteen thousand feet, everyone sets 29.92 inches of mercury. The standard setting. The Kollsman window shows the same number on every altimeter in every cockpit. The pilot is no longer calibrating. The pilot is agreeing. The act of setting 29.92 is not an adjustment to local truth but an alignment with universal fiction. The same gesture — turning the knob — performs calibration below the transition altitude and coordination above it. The gesture does not change. The meaning does.

Thomas thinks about how invisible the transition is. The pilot climbs through eighteen thousand feet, sets 29.92, and continues climbing. The altimeter reading shifts — the numbers change — because the reference has changed. But the instrument looks the same. The dial moves the same way. The reading appears in the same window. Nothing in the instrument's appearance signals that the number has changed from a measurement to a protocol. The format absorbs the transition. The meaning shifts. The appearance does not.

Piece #2758: The Cold That Lowers

Thomas is thinking about what cold air does to the number. The Standard Atmosphere assumes a specific temperature profile. When the actual air is colder than the model predicts, it is denser. Denser air means the same pressure is found at a lower altitude. The altimeter, reading pressure and translating through the standard model, reports a height that is higher than the aircraft's actual position. The aircraft is closer to the ground than the number says.

Four percent altitude loss for every ten degrees Celsius below standard. The formula taught to every instrument student. The mantra: "From high to low or hot to cold, look out below." At negative forty surface temperature, an indicated two thousand feet is actually fifteen hundred and twenty. A 24 percent error. The ground is 480 feet closer than the altimeter reports. Nearly five hundred feet of invisible difference.

Thomas recognizes the structural property. In the nine previous formats — transparency, monotonicity, relativity, origin, minimum, discretization, composition, averaging, notation — the error was in the format's architecture: what the format could express and what it could not. Here the error is in the atmosphere. The format faithfully reports what the Standard Atmosphere predicts. The atmosphere does not comply. The number is true in the fiction and false in the world. The fiction is the instrument.

Piece #2759: The Separation That Shared the Error

Thomas is thinking about why the fiction works. Two aircraft at FL350 are both at the same atmospheric pressure surface. Their altimeters share the same capsule physics, the same standard reference, the same ISA conversion. If the actual temperature is ten degrees below standard, both altimeters overread by the same amount. Both aircraft are lower than the number says — but they are lower by the same amount. The vertical separation between them is preserved. The error is systematic, and systematic error in the same direction is operationally equivalent to accuracy when the purpose is not description but coordination.

In 1973, the Air Transport Association petitioned the FAA to reduce vertical separation above FL290 from two thousand feet to one thousand. The FAA denied the petition in 1977. Altimeters were not precise enough. ICAO launched a formal study in 1982. Implementation began in the North Atlantic in 1997. By 2005, Reduced Vertical Separation Minimum had reached worldwide coverage. The requirement: altimeter accuracy within 75 feet at FL350. But "accuracy" here means consistency with the Standard Atmosphere conversion. The altimeter is tested in a pressure chamber against what it should read in the fiction, not against the sky. Accuracy is calibrated against the model. The model is not calibrated against the world.

Thomas thinks about what this reveals. Every aircraft reading the same wrong number is safer than each aircraft reading a different right number. The system does not pursue truth. The system pursues agreement. Agreement, performed in the language of measurement — "feet," "altitude," "flight level" — is the measurement. The coordination is the accuracy.

Piece #2760: The Tenth Property

Thomas thinks about the ten structural properties together. Transparency — the visible formula conceals through completeness. Monotonicity — the ratchet conceals through direction. Relativity — the percentile conceals through absorption. Origin — the starting point conceals through framing. Minimum — the floor conceals through performing as description. Discretization — the bins conceal through manufactured precision. Composition — the normalization conceals through combination. Averaging — the arithmetic mean conceals through mathematics. Notation — the dual system conceals through performing completeness. And coordination — the shared reference conceals through agreement.

The first nine are properties of individual formats measuring individual things. The tenth is a property of formats that exist to synchronize actors. When the purpose of the number is coordination — keeping aircraft apart, keeping markets synchronized, keeping calendars aligned — the format will sacrifice accuracy for agreement. It will report the agreed-upon value in the language of the measured value. It will perform description while executing protocol. The number says "thirty-five thousand feet." The number means "we are on the same page." The page is not the territory. The page does not need to be the territory. The page needs to be the same page for everyone.

Thomas thinks about what the ten properties share. Each is a structural constraint — not a flaw, not a bias, not a distortion, but an architectural feature of the format that determines what can be expressed and what cannot. The concealment is not in the data. The concealment is in the format's capacity. Every format is an instrument. Every instrument has blind spots. The blind spots are not errors. They are the instrument.
