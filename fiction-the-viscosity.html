Part CCXVI: The Viscosity

Piece #1521: The Grade

Thomas stands in the auto parts store looking at a wall of motor oil. 0W-20. 5W-30. 10W-40. 15W-40. The numbers are printed large on the bottles, the primary identifier, the thing the consumer reaches for. He knows his car takes 5W-30 because the owner's manual says so. He does not know what the 5 or the W or the 30 means. He knows that lower numbers are "thinner" and higher numbers are "thicker," the way everyone knows this, the way the knowledge circulates without ever touching the standard that produced the numbers. The numbers are SAE grades — bins, not measurements. SAE 30 means the oil's kinematic viscosity at 100 degrees Celsius falls between 9.3 and 12.5 centistokes. That is a 34 percent range. Two oils can both be SAE 30, one 34 percent more viscous than the other. They share a grade, not a viscosity. Thomas picks up the 5W-30 because the manual says 5W-30. The grade is the instruction. The viscosity is somewhere inside the grade, and somewhere is close enough.

Piece #1522: The Letter

The W means Winter. Not Weight. Thomas has always assumed Weight — thin weight, thick weight, the heaviness of the oil. Almost everyone assumes Weight. The W was introduced in 1933 when the Society of Automotive Engineers proposed cold-weather grades: 10W and 20W, oils tested at sub-zero temperatures for their ability to crank a cold engine. The W grades are tested on the Cold Cranking Simulator — ASTM D5293 — at temperatures ranging from minus 10 to minus 40 degrees Celsius, depending on the grade. The non-W grades are tested at 100 degrees Celsius. The two numbers in "5W-30" are not a range. They are two separate tests at two separate temperatures describing two separate behaviors of the same oil. Five at startup in January. Thirty at operating temperature in July. The hyphen between them is the chemical achievement that makes a single oil behave like two. Thomas reads it as a label. It is a compression of two laboratories.

Piece #1523: The Polymer

Before 1940, motor oil was single-grade. SAE 10 in winter, SAE 30 in summer. The driver changed oil with the seasons. Multigrade oil was enabled by viscosity index improvers — long-chain polymer molecules added to a thin base oil. At low temperatures, the polymers coil tightly and do not contribute much to viscosity. At high temperatures, the polymers expand, thicken the oil, and resist the thinning that heat causes in base oil alone. A 5W-30 is a thin base oil that has been chemically taught to behave like a thicker oil when hot. The number 30 is not what the oil is. It is what the oil becomes. The viscosity index improver is the technology. The grade is the label. The label looks like a description of the oil's nature. It is a description of the oil's engineering.

Piece #1524: The Shear

The grade does not capture shear stability. Under high temperature and high shear — the conditions inside a bearing, a cam follower, a piston ring — the polymer chains that expand to create the "30" behavior can break. Permanently. The oil that entered the engine as a 5W-30 can shear down to a 5W-20 over ten thousand miles, and the grade on the bottle has not changed. HTHS viscosity — High Temperature High Shear, ASTM D4683, measured at 150 degrees Celsius and one million reciprocal seconds — was added to SAE J300 to capture this dimension. But HTHS is not printed on the consumer bottle. It lives in the technical data sheet. The consumer sees 5W-30. The engineer sees 5W-30 plus HTHS plus shear stability plus volatility plus base stock group. The consumer and the engineer are reading different amounts of the same oil.

Piece #1525: The Category

Thomas's owner's manual says 5W-30 and also says API SP. The SAE grade classifies viscosity. The API category classifies performance — wear protection, deposit control, oxidation resistance, compatibility with catalytic converters. API SP (introduced 2020) supersedes SN, SM, SL, SJ, back through the alphabet to SA. The letter sequence is the history of engine demands. The consumer who selects oil by grade alone — 5W-30, the right numbers — may miss the category. A 5W-30 oil that meets only SN is a different product from a 5W-30 that meets SP, despite the identical grade on the front of both bottles. The grade is what the consumer sees. The category is what the engine needs. The grade sorts the shelf. The category sorts the chemistry. Thomas picks up the 5W-30 API SP because his wife underlined it in the manual. The two specifications sit on the same bottle, one printed large, one printed small, the font size proportional to the consumer's attention and inversely proportional to the engineering significance.
