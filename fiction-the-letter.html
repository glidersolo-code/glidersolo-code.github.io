Part CDXLV: The Letter

Piece #2666: The Request

Thomas asks his professor for a letter of recommendation. The professor says yes. Thomas does not know what the professor will write. Thomas will never know. Thomas waived the right to read the letter because the application said confidential letters carry more weight.

Thomas considers: the most consequential text in his application is the one text he cannot see, cannot edit, cannot correct. Thomas's GPA is his. Thomas's essay is his. Thomas's test scores are his. The letter is the professor's.

Piece #2667: The Superlative

Thomas's professor writes: "Thomas is one of the most thoughtful students I have taught in the past five years." The professor has taught approximately 500 students in five years. "One of the most thoughtful" could mean top 1% or top 20%. The committee reads: somewhere between the two, closer to exceptional. The phrase performs precision without providing it.

Thomas's classmate asked a different professor. That professor writes: "I recommend Thomas's classmate without reservation." The phrasing is positive. The phrasing is not superlative. The committee reads: competent but not exceptional. The difference between the two letters is not the difference between the two candidates. The difference is the difference between two writers' relationships with superlatives.

Kuncel et al., 2014: r = 0.13 to 0.16. The letter that felt most revealing predicted least.

Piece #2668: The Gender

Thomas reads Trix and Psenka, 2003. Six hundred twenty-four letters for medical faculty positions. Letters for women: shorter, more doubt raisers ("she might," "it appears"), more personal life references, fewer standout adjectives. Letters for men: longer, more agentic language ("ambitious," "confident," "assertive"), fewer hedges.

Thomas considers: the committee member reading these letters does not see gender bias. The committee member sees one letter that is more enthusiastic and another that is more measured. The measured letter performs the writer's assessment. The writer's assessment encoded the candidate's gender through vocabulary the writer may not have chosen consciously. The format — prose, confidential, individually authored — conceals the pattern that only appears in aggregate.

Piece #2669: The Decline

Thomas considers what a negative letter looks like. It doesn't look like anything. Writers who would write a negative letter say no. They say: "I'm not the best person to write this." They say: "You should ask someone who knows your work better." The candidate hears: find another writer. The candidate does not hear: the evaluation happened and was negative.

The result: the committee reads a sample from which negative assessments have been removed by social convention. Every letter is positive. The variation is in degree, not direction. The committee must extract a ranking from a sample that has been pre-compressed into the upper range. The discrimination happens in the adjectives. The adjectives measure the writer.

Piece #2670: The Warmth

Thomas is admitted. The letter helped. Thomas's professor wrote warmly — specific anecdotes, enthusiastic projection, personal knowledge performed on the page. The warmth was the relationship. Three years of mentorship, shared research, weekly meetings. The committee read the warmth as Thomas's quality. The warmth was the professor's investment.

A different candidate, equally talented, asked a supervisor who supervised from a distance. The supervisor wrote accurately but without warmth. The accuracy was read as lukewarm. The candidate was not admitted. What was measured was not the two candidates. What was measured was the distance between each candidate and their letter-writer. The distance was the letter. The letter was the decision.
