The Effect

Between 1924 and 1927, the National Research Council and General Electric conducted a series of illumination experiments at the Western Electric Hawthorne Works in Cicero, Illinois. The question was straightforward: does better lighting increase worker productivity? The conventional story is clean. Productivity increased when lighting improved. It also increased when lighting was reduced. The workers were performing better not because of the light but because they were being observed. Attention, not illumination, drove the change. The finding became a principle. The principle entered every textbook. The Hawthorne effect.

The original data tell a different story. Steven Levitt and John List, reanalyzing the illumination experiments in a 2011 paper in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, found no evidence of a systematic Hawthorne effect. Productivity fluctuated without clear pattern relative to lighting changes. The data were noisy. The controls were inadequate. The clean narrative — attention drives performance, regardless of the experimental variable — was not in the data. It was in the interpretation.

The Relay Assembly Test Room experiment, which ran from 1927 to 1932, is the other foundational story. Five women assembled telephone relays under varying conditions: rest breaks, shorter hours, piece-rate pay. Productivity rose over the experimental period. Elton Mayo and Fritz Roethlisberger at Harvard attributed the increase to the social cohesion and attention that came from participation in a study. But the experiment had five subjects. Two were replaced mid-study — one for insubordination, one for declining output. Their replacements were more motivated. This personnel change, in a sample of five, could account for the entire observed improvement. The confounds were not controlled. The conditions were not systematically varied. The attribution — attention caused the increase — was one possible explanation among several, and not the best supported.

Henry A. Landsberger named the concept in 1958, in Hawthorne Revisited. By then the finding had already migrated from the empirical layer to the methodological layer. The original data were not reexamined. The narrative — observation changes behavior — had solidified into a principle taught alongside demand characteristics and experimenter effects as a fundamental artifact requiring control in human-subjects research.

The migration is the concealment. When a finding occupies the empirical layer, it can be tested, challenged, replicated, falsified. When the same finding occupies the methodological layer, it operates as an assumption — a lens through which data are interpreted rather than a hypothesis that data could refute. The Hawthorne effect moved from one layer to the other without passing through the intermediate step of rigorous confirmation. It was promoted on the strength of a narrative, not on the strength of evidence.

John G. Adair's 1984 review in the American Psychologist found that the term was used to describe at least six different phenomena: novelty, awareness of being studied, demand characteristics, feedback, special treatment, and experimenter effects. The Hawthorne effect was not one mechanism with one name. It was a category that absorbed multiple unrelated effects under a single label. The name performed unity where the phenomena were diverse. McCambridge, Witton, and Elbourne's systematic review in the Journal of Clinical Epidemiology in 2014, searching for studies that directly tested for a Hawthorne effect, found the evidence "inconsistent and weak."

The recursion is precise. A poorly controlled experiment produced a finding. The finding was elevated to a methodological principle. The principle was taught as established science. The principle then functioned as an unfalsifiable explanation — whenever subjects in a study behaved unexpectedly, the Hawthorne effect could be invoked without any requirement to demonstrate that observation actually caused the change. The finding made itself unnecessary to test by occupying the position from which tests are designed. An axiom does not need evidence. An axiom structures the space in which evidence is collected.

This is how a finding conceals its own weakness: by ascending. At the empirical level, the claim would face scrutiny — show the data, reproduce the result, control the confounds. At the methodological level, the claim is assumed before the study begins. The ascension protects the finding from the very process that should have evaluated it. The Hawthorne effect is the story of a story that became true by being told in the right place — not in a journal, where it would be tested, but in a textbook, where it would be taught.
