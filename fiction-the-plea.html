Part CCCXLIV: The Bargain

Piece #2161: The Percentage

Thomas reads a number. Ninety-seven percent.

Ninety-seven percent of federal criminal convictions are obtained through guilty pleas. Three percent go to trial. The Sixth Amendment guarantees "a speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury." The right exists. Three percent exercise it.

In 1962, federal district judges averaged twenty-one criminal trials per year. By 2015: four.

Thomas reads the word. Bargain. The word performs a marketplace. Two parties with comparable leverage. Offer and counteroffer. Mutual benefit. The buyer chooses to buy. The seller chooses to sell.

The buyer is a defendant who may be innocent, cannot afford bail, does not understand the process. The seller is a prosecutor holding mandatory minimums, charge-stacking authority, and pretrial detention.

The word is "bargain." The function is: comply or be destroyed.

Piece #2162: The Check

Thomas reads about Paul Lewis Hayes. He forged a check for $88.30.

Charged with uttering a forged instrument. Two to ten years. The prosecutor offered five years. Plead guilty, the prosecutor said. Save the court the inconvenience and necessity of a trial. Otherwise, the prosecutor told Hayes, he would refile under the Kentucky Habitual Criminal Act.

Hayes had two prior felonies. The Habitual Criminal Act carried a mandatory sentence. Life in prison.

Hayes refused the plea. Hayes went to trial. Hayes was convicted. Hayes was sentenced to life in prison. For an $88.30 check.

The Supreme Court upheld it. Bordenkircher v. Hayes, 1978. The threat was constitutionally permissible. The prosecutor's leverage — five years or life — was not coercion. It was bargaining.

The word was "bargain." The stakes were five years or life. The check was $88.30.

Piece #2163: The Innocent

Thomas reads about the 839. Eight hundred and thirty-nine people documented by the National Registry of Exonerations who pleaded guilty to crimes they did not commit.

Twenty-four percent of all documented wrongful convictions involved guilty pleas. People who said "guilty" to things they did not do.

In Harris County, Texas, 157 people pleaded guilty to drug charges. The substances were not drugs. The testing came after the pleas. The format of the plea — voluntary, knowing, intelligent — had already been performed. The guilty had already spoken the word. The lab results arrived later. The word could not be unsaid.

Thomas reads about the misdemeanor courts. Innocent people plead guilty to get out of jail. The choice: plead guilty and go home tonight, or maintain innocence and wait in jail for months until the trial date. More than 500,000 unconvicted people sit in jails awaiting trial.

The word is "voluntary." The alternative is a cage.

Piece #2164: The Penalty

Thomas reads the numbers. Federal drug cases, 2012. Average sentence after trial: sixteen years. After plea: five years and four months.

Three to one.

First-time drug defendants with mandatory minimums: trial sentence averaged 117.6 months. Plea sentence: 59.5 months. Nearly two to one.

The Sentencing Guidelines build the penalty into their architecture. A two-level offense level reduction for "acceptance of responsibility." Granted to 96.3% of those who plead guilty. Going to trial forfeits the reduction. The guidelines do not merely permit lighter sentences for pleas. They structurally punish the exercise of the constitutional right.

Thomas reads the phrase. "Acceptance of responsibility." The phrase performs contrition — the defendant accepts what was done. The phrase functions as a surcharge on the right to trial. Accept responsibility: two levels off. Exercise your Sixth Amendment right: two levels on.

The word "responsibility" conceals the mechanism. The defendant does not accept responsibility. The defendant accepts the terms.

Piece #2165: The Word

Thomas reads the Sixth Amendment again. "In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to a speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury."

Enjoy. The word the framers chose. Not merely have. Not merely be entitled to. Enjoy. The right is framed as something desirable. Something to be exercised with satisfaction.

Ninety-seven percent decline to enjoy it. Not because they do not want a trial. Because a trial costs three times the sentence. Because life in prison is the alternative to five years. Because the jail cell is the alternative to home tonight.

The system does not deny the Sixth Amendment. The system prices it. The right to trial exists the way a menu in a restaurant exists: everything is available, provided the customer can pay. The trial penalty is the price. Most cannot afford it.

The word was "bargain." The word performed negotiation. The function performed coercion. The parties were not bargaining. One party was threatening. The other was complying. Between the word and the function — between "bargain" and "comply" — ninety-seven percent of all federal criminal cases disappeared into the gap.
