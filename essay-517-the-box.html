The Box

The survey offers five boxes. Strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, strongly agree. The respondent's experience is not five things. The experience is continuous, mixed, gradient. The form has five options. The respondent selects one. The selection is a forced landing — the feeling had nowhere else to go.

Rensis Likert, a Columbia psychologist, published the technique in 1932. He tested two scoring methods: a sigma method that converted responses into standard deviation units, and a simpler method that assigned integers 1 through 5. The correlation between the two: .987 to .997. The simpler method won — not because it was more valid, but because it was more portable. Likert acknowledged the assumption was pragmatic. "The possible dangers inherent in this assumption are fully realized." The dangers were realized. The method persisted.

Fourteen years later, Stanley Stevens defined four levels of measurement. The Likert scale is ordinal — the categories have rank order, but the distance between "strongly agree" and "agree" is not necessarily equal to the distance between "agree" and "neutral." The data are ranks. The analysis treats them as quantities. The debate has persisted for over eighty years. Susan Jamieson called it "illegitimate." Geoff Norman called parametric statistics "robust" to the violation. The defense amounts to: the wrong assumption produces acceptable results often enough that the wrongness is tolerable. The format is too useful to abandon. The flaw is too fundamental to resolve.

The format shapes the response. Schwarz and colleagues asked German adults about life success on an eleven-point scale with identical verbal labels. When numbered 0 to 10, thirty-four percent endorsed the lower half. When numbered -5 to +5, thirteen percent endorsed equivalent values. Twenty-one percentage points. Moved by the numbers alone. The minus sign performs failure. The respondent avoids the performance. The avoidance shifts the distribution. The label did not change. The feeling did not change. The number changed the answer.

Culture compounds the distortion. Japanese and Chinese students choose the midpoint more often. American students choose the extremes. Power distance, collectivism, and uncertainty avoidance predict response patterns across twenty-six countries. The same five boxes interact with different cultural norms to produce different distributions from internal states that may or may not differ. The mean erases the interaction. The number crosses borders. The format-culture interaction stays behind.

Then there is the matter of the average. A respondent selects 4. Another selects 3. The mean is 3.5. No respondent expressed 3.5. The format offered integers. The aggregate produced a value between the integers. A mean of 3.72, reported to two decimal places, implies a precision of 0.01 on a scale whose actual resolution is 1.0. The aggregation inflates apparent precision by roughly two orders of magnitude. The decimal performs a measurement the instrument did not make.

Between sixty-one and eighty-one percent of students satisfice — selecting the midpoint or the first plausible option rather than optimizing. The format that is easy to complete is easy to satisfice. The satisficed response enters the average indistinguishable from the considered one. The number does not know how it was produced.

The structural property is discretization. The batting average conceals through visibility. The h-index conceals through directionality. The percentile conceals through absorption. The survival rate conceals through its starting point. The age statement conceals through its floor. The Likert scale conceals through its bins. The continuous is forced into the discrete. The discrete is averaged into the continuous. The average performs a precision the instrument never possessed. The format produces the finding. The finding validates the format. Six structures. Six concealments. The architecture of the number determines what the number can say — and what it performs while saying it.
