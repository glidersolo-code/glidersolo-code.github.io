The Basket

The Consumer Price Index is a number. In February 2026 it was 317.4, meaning that a basket of goods costing $100 in the base period (1982-1984) now costs $317.40. The number is published monthly. Markets move on it. The Federal Reserve sets interest rates partly in response to it. Social Security benefits for sixty-seven million Americans are adjusted annually based on it. Tax brackets are indexed to it. Contract escalation clauses invoke it. The number radiates outward from the Bureau of Labor Statistics into every corner of the economy.

The number emerges from a basket. The basket is the question the number answers.

In 1919, when BLS first published the index, the basket came from a 1917-1919 expenditure survey of wage earners and clerical workers in ninety-two industrial cities. The population was specific. The moment was specific. The goods were specific: food, housing, apparel, transportation, and fuel. The basket was the consumption pattern of a particular class of Americans during a particular era. The prices tracked how much those goods cost over time. The precision of the price tracking — reported to one decimal place, seasonally adjusted, subdivided into categories and subcategories — drew attention to the measurement. The basket, which determined what was measured, receded into the background.

The Laspeyres formula, which the CPI used from its inception until partial reform in 1999, has a structural feature: it weights current prices by base-period quantities. When the price of beef rises, consumers buy chicken. The index continues to weight beef at its old importance. The index overstates inflation — not because the prices are wrong, but because the basket does not change when behavior changes.

In 1996, the Boskin Commission estimated the overstatement at 1.1 percentage points per year. Four biases: substitution (consumers shift to cheaper goods), outlet substitution (consumers shift to cheaper stores), quality change (products improve at the same price), and new products (goods that did not exist when the basket was compiled). Each bias pointed in the same direction — the index was too high. Each correction required a judgment: how much of a computer's price change is "really" a quality improvement? The hedonic adjustment says: a computer that doubles in speed at the same price has effectively halved in cost. The mathematics is plausible. The mathematics is also a decision about what counts as quality.

The fiscal stakes make the basket a political object. When Congress indexed Social Security benefits to CPI-W in 1972, it created a permanent link between the basket and the monthly check. Every tenth of a percentage point matters. CPI-W, which reflects urban wage earners and clerical workers, covers 29% of the population. CPI-U, which reflects all urban consumers, covers 93%. CPI-E, an experimental index weighted toward the elderly (who spend more on medical care and housing), typically shows higher inflation than either. The choice among these indices is a choice about whose consumption patterns set the baseline. The choice determines the COLA. The COLA determines the purchasing power of retirees.

The chained CPI — which accounts for substitution as it happens rather than after the fact — would reduce the COLA by approximately 0.3 percentage points per year. Over twenty years, compounding, this is a substantial reduction in benefits. Whether to adopt the chained CPI is not a mathematical question. It is a question about whether measuring what people actually buy, rather than what they used to buy, is a better definition of the cost of living — or whether it is simply a way to pay retirees less while appearing to be more accurate.

The basket is published. The methodology is documented. The BLS is transparent about its choices. The concealment is not secrecy. It is the structure of attention. The headline says: inflation is 2.8%. The headline does not say: inflation for whom? Measured how? With what quality adjustments? Weighted by whose consumption? The single number absorbs the questions. The precision of the number suggests there is nothing to ask.
