Part CDXXIV: The Butterfly

Piece #2561: The Wings

Thomas learned the shape of the problem.

Two pages. Left and right — wings of a butterfly. Ten presidential candidates, too many for one page if the type was to be readable. Theresa LePore, Supervisor of Elections for Palm Beach County, Florida, enlarged the font. She spread the names across two facing pages. Punch holes ran down the center spine between them like a row of buttons.

Bush first on the left. Gore second on the left. Buchanan first on the right. First hole: Bush. Second hole: Buchanan. Third hole: Gore. Small arrows pointed from names to holes. The visual logic of the layout was stronger than the arrows. A voter scanning down the left column — Bush first, Gore second — would see the second hole and punch it. The second hole was Buchanan's.

LePore's intention was readability. The consequence was a presidency.

Piece #2562: The Numbers

Thomas counted what the butterfly had done.

Buchanan received 3,407 votes in Palm Beach County. The county had 337 registered Reform Party members. Buchanan's next-highest county total in Florida was less than one-quarter of this. Statistical analysis predicted 371 votes for Buchanan in Palm Beach County — the 99% confidence interval topped out at 1,908.

On election-day ballots — butterfly format — Buchanan's proportion was four times larger than on absentee ballots — standard format. In no other Florida county did his proportion differ between the two formats. The butterfly was the variable. The butterfly was the only variable.

More than 19,000 voters punched two holes. Five thousand three hundred thirty were specifically Gore-Buchanan double punches. Eighty-three percent of those also contained votes for Democrat Bill Nelson for Senate. They were Gore voters. They punched Gore's apparent hole, realized it was wrong, then punched Gore's actual hole. Both punches invalidated the ballot. Both punches were the voter trying to vote correctly. The ballot turned correction into cancellation.

Bush won Florida by 537 votes.

Piece #2563: The Acknowledgment

Buchanan went on NBC's Today Show.

"Almost certainly those are Al Gore's votes and not mine."

Thomas had never seen a beneficiary of a concealment channel identify the channel while standing inside its effect. The man who received the misdirected votes said they were misdirected. The man who received the presidency did not say this. The ballot's influence was visible to everyone except the institution that ratified the result.

Piece #2564: The Order

The butterfly was extreme. The ordinary was quieter and more pervasive.

Jon Krosnick at Stanford: the candidate listed first on any ballot gains a measurable advantage. In Ohio, 2.33 percentage points. In Texas, where ballot order is randomized within counties — providing a natural experiment — Darren Grant found the effect was nearly ten percentage points in down-ballot races. In one Texas Supreme Court race involving two candidates with the same last name, the ballot order effect was nearly twenty percentage points.

The cognitive mechanism is satisficing. When voters have low information — particularly in judicial races, school board races, ballot propositions with confusing language — they accept the first adequate option rather than evaluating all alternatives. The first name on the ballot enters the voter's consideration first. The ballot presents its candidates in a specific order. The order is always someone's advantage. There is no neutral order. The ballot performs neutrality while executing a sequence that has never been neutral.

California tried to distribute the advantage: a randomized alphabet, drawn 82 days before each election, determines name order, then the order rotates across all 80 Assembly districts. The design ensures no single candidate benefits everywhere. It cannot ensure that no candidate benefits anywhere. The advantage moves. It does not disappear. There is no design that eliminates it. There is only design.

Piece #2565: The Window

Thomas sat with the problem's geometry.

A ballot must list candidates in some order. Some candidate must be first. The first position confers an advantage. There is no orderless ballot. A ballot must place races on pages or screens. Some races must be further down. Further down means more rolloff — more voters who stop voting before they reach the race. There is no pageless ballot. A ballot must align names with selection mechanisms — holes, ovals, buttons. The alignment can mislead. There is no alignment that cannot mislead.

The ballot appears to be a window — a transparent surface through which voter intent passes unchanged to the count. The window is a lens. The lens bends the light. The bending is structural. It is not corruption. It is physics. The physics of design. The physics of sequence. The physics of alignment. The physics of attention.

Every election is administered through an interface. The interface is designed. The design influences outcomes. In most elections, the influence is smaller than the margin. In Palm Beach County, November 7, 2000, the design was the margin. The ballot that was supposed to record the will of the people shaped the will of the people. Five hundred thirty-seven votes. Two thousand misdirected. Nineteen thousand invalidated. One presidency. One butterfly. Two wings. One spine of holes. One enlarged font. One intention — readability. One consequence — everything.
