Part CCL: The Pitcher

Piece #1691: The Word

The box says "reduces chlorine, mercury, and lead." Thomas bought the pitcher to clean his water. "Reduces" is not "removes." NSF/ANSI Standard 42 certifies reduction of chlorine taste and odor — typically 97 percent or better, meaning 3 percent remains. NSF/ANSI Standard 53 certifies reduction of health-related contaminants: lead reduced to below 10 parts per billion from a challenge concentration of 150 ppb. Not to zero. Mercury to below 2 ppb. The consumer reads "reduces lead" and may infer the absence of lead. The standard certifies a threshold. The threshold is not zero. Thomas poured the water through the filter and drank it, believing it clean. It was cleaner. Clean and cleaner are not the same word.

Piece #1692: The List

The box lists three contaminants. Chlorine. Mercury. Lead. This is what the filter is certified to reduce. What the box does not list: pharmaceuticals, pesticides, herbicides (NSF Standard 401 — voluntary, most pitcher filters not certified), PFAS — the per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances that contaminate groundwater near industrial sites and military bases (NSF P473 — separate certification, most activated-carbon pitcher filters do not achieve), microplastics, bacteria, viruses. A filter certified to Standards 42 and 53 may pass all of these through unchanged. The consumer reads the listed contaminants and may infer comprehensive protection. The list is the protection. The gaps between the list items are the exposure. Thomas's municipal water contained 12 ppt of PFOA. The pitcher reduced the chlorine taste. The PFOA passed through the carbon unchanged, at the same concentration it entered.

Piece #1693: The Capacity

The filter insert says "replace every 40 gallons or 2 months." Forty gallons was tested with NSF challenge water — specified contaminant concentrations in a controlled feed. Thomas's tap water comes from a surface-water treatment plant with seasonal variation in turbidity, organic loading, and disinfection byproducts. Higher contaminant loads exhaust activated carbon faster — the adsorption sites fill. A filter rated for 40 gallons with test water may deliver effective filtration for 25 gallons with Thomas's November water, when the autumn leaf-fall increases organic matter in the reservoir. The carbon does not announce when it is exhausted. It continues to pass water through. The pitcher has no sensor. The indicator — a timer or volume counter on the lid — measures usage, not performance. It counts gallons poured, not contaminants captured. Thomas replaced the filter when the indicator turned red. The indicator knew the volume. It did not know the water.

Piece #1694: The Certification

The NSF mark on the box. A circled "NSF" with the standard numbers below. The consumer reads: certified. Independent testing. The certification is real — NSF International tests products against published standards, and the standards are rigorous within their scope. The scope is the variable. Standard 42 covers aesthetics: taste, odor, chlorine. Standard 53 covers health effects: a specific list of contaminants at specific reduction thresholds. Standard 401 covers emerging contaminants. Standard P473 covers PFAS. A product certified to 42 and 53 but not 401 and P473 bears the same NSF mark. The consumer sees the mark. The consumer does not read the standard numbers. The mark performs comprehensive certification. The numbers define its boundaries.

Piece #1695: The Glass

Two hundred and fifty Parts. Thomas filled a glass from the pitcher. The water was clear. The chlorine taste was gone. The lead, if present, was reduced to below the action level. The mercury was reduced to below the standard's threshold. The PFAS, if present, passed through. The pharmaceutical traces from upstream municipal discharge passed through. The microplastics smaller than the carbon's pore structure passed through. The filter reduced what it was designed to reduce and passed what it was not designed to catch. The glass tasted clean. Taste is a filter too — it detects some compounds and misses others, performing comprehensiveness while sampling a fraction of the chemistry. Thomas drank. The water was better than the tap. Better and safe are not the same word. The pitcher, having filtered, did not disclose what it let through.
