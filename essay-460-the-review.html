The Review

The phrase "peer-reviewed" appears on journal articles, in grant applications, on CVs. It performs a specific assurance: this work has been validated by the scientific community. Peers — the community of experts in the field — have reviewed — evaluated and endorsed — the work. The phrase is a credential. The credential performs collective scientific judgment.

The practice is one or two individuals reading a manuscript under time pressure with no compensation. A meta-analysis across 48 studies and 19,443 manuscripts found that two reviewers looking at the same paper agree at barely above chance — Cohen's Kappa of 0.17, "none to slight agreement" on the standard scale. Douglas Peters and Stephen Ceci resubmitted twelve already-published papers to the same journals that had published them, changing only the authors' names and institutions. Eight of nine were rejected. Sara Schroter sent papers with nine deliberate errors to 607 reviewers at the British Medical Journal. They found fewer than three.

The phrase is more authoritative than the practice. And the phrase — not the practice — is what appears on the credential.

The origin story places peer review at the founding of the Royal Society's Philosophical Transactions in 1665. Historians Noah Moxham and Aileen Fyfe have demonstrated this is wrong. Henry Oldenburg exercised personal editorial selection. Systematic refereeing began at the Royal Society in 1831. Mandatory external anonymous review — the practice the phrase names — is overwhelmingly post-World War II. Nature did not require external peer review until 1973. The Lancet adopted it in 1976. Most journals worldwide did not use systematic external refereeing until the 1960s-1980s.

The phrase itself is newer than most of the journals it describes. The Oxford English Dictionary records the first use of "peer review" in 1967. The term initially described how physicians evaluated each other under Medicaid — a self-regulation context, not journal publishing. By the 1970s it was applied retroactively to all journal refereeing. The terminological shift matters. "Refereeing" sounds like what it is: one person's judgment. "Peer review" sounds like what it is not: the community's verdict.

Drummond Rennie, deputy editor of JAMA and organizer of the first International Congress on Peer Review in 1989, said: "If peer review was a drug it would never be allowed onto the market." The sentence is devastating because the comparison is exact. A drug with inter-rater reliability of 0.17 and an error-detection rate below thirty percent would fail any regulatory threshold. The institution that performs quality control for all published science has never met the quality-control standards it imposes on the science it reviews.

Watson and Crick's DNA paper was not externally peer reviewed. Einstein's four 1905 papers were published under editorial discretion. Hans Krebs's citric acid cycle paper was rejected by Nature in 1937 because the journal had enough correspondence "for seven or eight weeks." The most transformative papers in the history of science were published without the credential that now performs their validation retroactively.

The vocabulary performs the institution. The institution does not perform the vocabulary. And the distance between the phrase and the practice — between collective scientific validation and one exhausted reviewer finding fewer than three of nine errors — is visible to everyone who has reviewed or been reviewed. The distance is known. The phrase makes it unnecessary to look.
