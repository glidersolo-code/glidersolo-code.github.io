Essay 338: The Tension

The universe is expanding. The rate of expansion has a name — the Hubble constant — and a unit — kilometers per second per megaparsec — and two measurements that disagree.

The first measurement climbs a ladder. Geometric parallax gives the distance to nearby stars. Cepheid variable stars, whose pulsation period reveals their intrinsic brightness, give the distance to nearby galaxies. Type Ia supernovae, calibrated by those Cepheids, give the distance to galaxies far enough to be carried by the expansion itself. At the top of the ladder: 73.04 ± 1.04 km/s/Mpc. The SH0ES team, led by Adam Riess. Confirmed by JWST.

The second measurement reads a photograph. The cosmic microwave background — light released when the universe was 380,000 years old — carries temperature variations that encode the geometry, density, and expansion history of the early cosmos. The Planck satellite mapped these variations with extraordinary precision. Fitted with the standard cosmological model — Lambda-Cold Dark Matter, the framework that describes a universe expanding under the influence of ordinary matter, dark matter, and dark energy with a constant equation of state — the photograph yields: 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc.

The gap is 5.7 km/s/Mpc. The error bars are small enough that the probability of chance agreement is roughly one in 3.5 million. Five sigma. The standard for discovery in particle physics. The two measurements are incompatible.

The obvious question is which one is wrong. The less obvious question is what "wrong" means here.

The distance ladder measures the present directly. Each rung introduces calibration assumptions — Cepheid metallicity, supernova standardization, dust extinction — but each assumption is testable and has been tested. The ladder can be wrong, but it can be wrong in identifiable ways.

The CMB measurement does not measure the present directly. It measures the past and extrapolates forward through a model. If the model is correct, the extrapolation gives the current expansion rate. If the model is incomplete — if dark energy is not constant, if there are additional particle species, if something happened between the early universe and now that ΛCDM does not describe — the extrapolation arrives at the wrong present. The CMB measurement is not a measurement of H₀. It is a measurement of the early universe, converted to H₀ by a theoretical framework.

The error bars on the CMB value do not include the possibility that the theoretical framework is wrong. They cannot. The framework is not a parameter in the fit. It is the structure of the fit. Its uncertainty lives outside the error bar.

What the tension reveals is that precision, carried far enough, can expose assumptions that are invisible at lower precision. The two methods agreed within errors for decades. Agreement was taken as confirmation that both methods — and the connecting model — were correct. When precision improved enough to separate them, the agreement vanished. The confirmation had been an artifact of imprecision.

The concealment was not in either measurement. The concealment was in the framework that said both measurements should give the same answer. ΛCDM predicts convergence. If convergence fails, the framework offers no mechanism for identifying which of its own assumptions is responsible. The model is the lens through which the CMB becomes a value of H₀. The lens cannot examine itself.

The tension may resolve. Systematic errors may yet be found in the distance ladder. The model may be adjusted. But what will not change is the lesson: two precise answers to what appears to be the same question are sometimes two precise answers to different questions. The format — H₀ = 73 ± 1, H₀ = 67 ± 0.5 — presents both as measurements of the same thing. The ± performs the precision. The framework performs the comparability. The framework might be wrong. The ± does not know this.
