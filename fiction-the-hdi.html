Part CDLX: The Index

Piece #2741: The Vulgarity

Thomas is thinking about what happens when a philosopher agrees to a crudeness. Amartya Sen did not initially see much merit in the Human Development Index. He expressed "considerable skepticism about trying to focus on a crude index of this kind, attempting to catch in one simple number a complex reality about human development." His collaborator Mahbub ul Haq had the counter-argument: "We need a measure of the same level of vulgarity as GNP — just one number — but a measure that is not as blind to social aspects of human lives as GNP is." The vulgarity was the design specification. The philosopher who understood that human development cannot be reduced to a single number was persuaded by the argument that only a single number could displace a single number. Sen was right about the crudeness. Haq was right about the politics. The politics won. The HDI was born as a deliberate simplification — a number designed to be as reductive as the number it was meant to replace.

Thomas thinks about what it means for a measurement to be designed as vulgar. The batting average was not designed to be crude — it simply is. The h-index was designed to be simple, but simplicity and vulgarity are different. The HDI was designed to be crude as a strategy. The crudeness was instrumental. And the instrument worked — the UNDP report displaced GDP as the headline measure of national development. But the crudeness that was instrumental became, over time, invisible. The number that was designed as a provocation became a standard. The vulgarity that was a rhetorical device became a measurement.

Piece #2742: The Invisible Trade-Off

Thomas is thinking about what it costs to combine incommensurable things. The HDI combines life expectancy, years of schooling, and GNI per capita. The combination requires that these three things be commensurable — that there exists some rate at which a year of life can be traded for a year of schooling, or a year of schooling for a dollar of income. The equation performs the trade-off. The equation does not state the trade-off.

Ravallion, 2012. The marginal rate of substitution embedded in the geometric mean determines, for each country, how many dollars the HDI treats as equivalent to one additional year of life. For Zimbabwe: $0.51 per year. For the richest countries: approximately $9,000 per year. The ratio is 17,000 to 1. The income ratio between the same countries is 460 to 1. The HDI's implicit valuation of a year of human life is thirty-seven times more unequal than the income inequality it claims to see past.

Thomas reads this number — 17,000 to 1 — and recognizes the pattern. Zero correlation between survival improvement and mortality. Twenty-one percentage-point shift from numbers alone. Forty percent evaporation over twenty-five years. 17,000 to 1 in the implicit value of a human year. Each entry in this session has its striking number. Each number reveals the gap between what the format performs and what the format contains. The format performs equality. The equation contains a 17,000-to-1 disparity.

Piece #2743: The Same Number, the Different Countries

Thomas is thinking about what 0.886 means and what it conceals. Qatar: HDI 0.886. GNI per capita $105,353 — top five to ten globally by income alone. But expected years of schooling: 13.1. Mean years of schooling: 10.8. The income dimension is near maximum. The education dimension pulls the composite down. The single number absorbs the imbalance.

Equatorial Guinea: HDI 0.674. GDP per capita approximately $20,942 — sixty-third globally. But life expectancy: 63.7 years. Less than half the population has access to clean drinking water. Around one in twelve children dies before age five. Oil wealth concentrated in a single extended family. The income dimension performs development. The health dimension contradicts it. The single number merges the contradiction.

Cuba: HDI 0.762. GNI per capita $8,415. But life expectancy: 78.3 years — comparable to many wealthy nations. Universal healthcare and education produce high health and education scores that compensate for low income. Mexico has more than double Cuba's GDP per capita but ranks lower on HDI.

Thomas thinks: the single number makes these countries comparable. The comparison requires that the differences within each country — the composition of the components, the trade-offs between dimensions — be erased. The format performs the erasure. The ranking performs the comparison. The comparison performs a judgment that the components, taken separately, do not support.

Piece #2744: The Goalposts

Thomas is thinking about what the boundaries do to the number. The normalization converts each dimension to a 0-to-1 index. The conversion requires goalposts — minimum and maximum values. Life expectancy: 20 to 85. Expected schooling: 0 to 18. GNI per capita: $100 to $75,000. The goalposts determine the scaling. The scaling determines the normalization. The normalization determines the index. The index determines the ranking. The goalposts have changed: the income upper bound was $8,193 in 1990, $40,000 in 1999, $75,000 today. Each change rearranges the rankings. Changing the goalposts shifts roughly 30% of countries by five or more positions.

The goalposts are described as "natural zeros" and "aspirational targets." They are neither. The minimum life expectancy of 20 years is not a natural zero — it is a chosen floor. The maximum of 85 is not an aspiration — it is a chosen ceiling. The choices determine the numbers. The numbers determine the ranking. The ranking performs objectivity. The objectivity rests on goalposts that are, in the precise sense, arbitrary.

Thomas thinks about the seven structural properties. Transparency conceals through visibility. Monotonicity conceals through direction. Relativity conceals through absorption. Origin conceals through the starting point. Minimum conceals through the floor. Discretization conceals through the bins. Composition conceals through the combination. The composition makes incommensurable dimensions commensurable by stripping their units. The equation combines what cannot be combined by erasing the dimensions in which the impossibility would be visible. Seven structures. Seven concealments. The architecture of the format determines the shape of the finding. The format is the instrument.

Piece #2745: The Principle of Insufficient Reason

Thomas is thinking about equal weighting. The three dimensions of the HDI receive equal weight: one-third health, one-third education, one-third income. The UNDP justifies this with the "principle of insufficient reason" — since there is no basis for choosing unequal weights, equal weights are chosen. The principle frames a choice as a non-choice. The absence of justification for unequal weights becomes the justification for equal weights.

But equal weighting is a value judgment. It asserts that a year of life expectancy is as important as a year of schooling, which is as important as a unit of income. Alternative weightings shift Maldives and Gabon by more than one hundred positions. The ranking is not a description of reality. The ranking is a consequence of weights. The weights are a consequence of the principle of insufficient reason. The principle of insufficient reason is a consequence of not wanting to justify the weights.

Thomas thinks: this is the seventh structural property applied to the deepest question. The HDI was designed to measure human development. The measure requires combining incommensurable dimensions. The combination requires trade-offs. The trade-offs require value judgments. The value judgments are concealed behind the principle of insufficient reason, the normalization that strips units, the geometric mean that performs the combination, and the three-decimal number that performs the precision. Seven layers of concealment. And the number is 0.970. Or 0.886. Or 0.674. And the ranking says: this country is developed. This country is not. The ranking is a sentence. The format is the judge.
