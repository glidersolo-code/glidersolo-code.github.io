The Bucket

In 2019, every dehumidifier on the shelf got weaker. No hardware changed. The test condition changed.

The DOE moved the test temperature from 80 degrees Fahrenheit to 65 — closer to actual basement conditions. Under the old AHAM DH-1 test at 80 degrees and 60 percent relative humidity, a given compressor extracted approximately 70 pints per day. Under the new test (10 CFR 430, Appendix X) at 65 degrees and 60 percent RH, the same compressor extracts approximately 35 pints. The number halved. The consumer reading a 2018 review of a "70-pint" machine and comparing it to the current "35-pint" model on the shelf concludes the old one was twice as powerful. It was the same machine, measured at a temperature that changed what the coil could extract from the air.

The extraction rate is a function of dew-point differential. The evaporator coil chills incoming air below its dew point; the moisture condenses. Warmer, more humid air holds more moisture — more to condense per cycle. At 80 degrees and 60 percent, the air carries approximately 10.9 grams of water per kilogram. At 65 degrees and 60 percent, approximately 7.0 grams. The physics are proportional. A basement at 58 degrees in winter — common in northern climates — yields less moisture than the 65-degree test condition. The rated 35 pints becomes 20 to 25 in practice. Below 41 degrees, the evaporator frosts and the machine cycles a defrost function, further reducing effective extraction time. The 35-pint machine in a winter basement is a 20-pint machine.

The room-size recommendation — "up to 1,500 square feet" — assumes standard moisture loading. Standard soil permeability. Standard foundation sealing. Standard ventilation. A basement with an active sump pump, hairline foundation cracks, and a dryer venting indoors produces moisture beyond the standard. A 900-square-foot basement with these conditions may need more dehumidification than the 1,500-square-foot basement the sizing table imagined.

The collection bucket is the final variable. A 1.5-gallon bucket holds 12 pints. At the rated 35 pints per day, the bucket fills in approximately 8 hours. The machine auto-shuts when full. The consumer at work for 10 hours returns to a full bucket and a machine that stopped running after 8. One fill per work day: 12 pints extracted, not 35. The continuous drain hose — gravity-fed to a floor drain — eliminates the bucket constraint, but many consumers do not connect it. The box said 35 pints per day. The bucket determined how many of those pints the consumer's schedule permitted.

A number that was 70 before the test changed, that depends on a temperature the basement may not provide, that assumes a moisture load the foundation may exceed, that requires a drain hose the consumer may not connect. The pint-per-day rating is a single frame from a variable film. The basement plays every frame the year provides.
