The Letter

A professor writes a letter for a student. The letter is two pages, single-spaced. It describes the student's research, the student's character, the student's potential. The professor has known the student for three years. The letter is personal, specific, detailed. The admissions committee reads it alongside two hundred others.

The committee does not know: nearly all letters are positive. Writers who would write a negative letter decline to write one. The sample the committee reads has been pre-filtered for enthusiasm. What varies is not the direction — nearly all positive — but the degree. "Excellent" versus "good." "One of the best" versus "very strong." "Will transform the field" versus "will contribute meaningfully." The signal is compressed into superlatives.

Kuncel, Kochevar, and Ones meta-analyzed the predictive validity in 2014. The corrected correlation between letters and subsequent performance: r = 0.13 to 0.16. The most personal element of the application, the one that feels most like a window into the candidate, predicts the least about what follows.

The prediction fails because the letter measures the wrong variable. The letter measures the relationship between the writer and the candidate. A close mentor who has supervised research, shared meals, discussed ambitions, writes a letter warm with anecdote and conviction. A course instructor who recognizes the student from the front row writes a letter competent but generic. The warmth is the relationship, not the candidate. The committee reads the warmth as the candidate.

Trix and Psenka analyzed 624 letters for medical faculty positions. Letters for women were shorter, contained more doubt raisers — "she might be able to," "it appears that" — more references to personal life, and fewer superlatives. The reader does not see gender bias. The reader sees a letter that is slightly less enthusiastic, slightly less confident, slightly less certain. The format performs neutrality because the letter is unsigned by demographics — it is signed by a named evaluator whose credibility is assumed.

The confidentiality norm seals the system. The candidate waives the right to read the letter. The waiver is presented as the price of credibility — confidential letters are taken more seriously. The candidate cannot see the doubt raisers. The candidate cannot correct the generic praise. The candidate cannot compare their letter to others. The most consequential text in the application is the one text the applicant cannot read.

The letter persists because it feels like the most human part of the process — a person writing about a person. The feeling is the mechanism. The feeling of personal knowledge substitutes for predictive validity. The feeling has a correlation of r = 0.14 with what follows. The letter is the relationship, formatted as the candidate, read as the future.
