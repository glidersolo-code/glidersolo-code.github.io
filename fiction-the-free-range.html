Fiction The Free Range

Fiction

Part CCCXXI: The Range

Piece #2046: The Carton

Thomas opened the carton. Twelve brown eggs. The label: Free Range. Grade A. Large.

He had once visited a farm. Actual pasture-raised hens — 108 square feet per bird, rotating across fields, pecking at insects, dust-bathing in sunlight. The eggs had deep orange yolks. The hens were, by any reasonable definition, free.

These eggs might have come from that kind of farm. Or they might have come from a barn with 20,000 hens and a single door to a concrete pad. Both were "free range" under the USDA definition. Both satisfied the requirement: access to the outdoors during the laying cycle.

Thomas closed the carton. The label couldn't tell him which farm. The word "free" covered both.

Piece #2047: The Door

The USDA required a door. The door must be open. Behind the door: outdoors. No specification of outdoor area size. No specification of outdoor surface quality. No specification of whether the hens actually used the door.

Thomas thought about what a door meant to 20,000 hens raised indoors since hatching. A hen that had never been outside would not necessarily discover the door, recognize it as an exit, brave the unfamiliar light and air, and cross the threshold. Access was architectural. Behavior was learned.

The regulation measured the door. The consumer imagined the field beyond it. The hen, most likely, never crossed.

Piece #2048: The Hierarchy

He had mapped the labels in a grocery store once:

Conventional: Caged. 67 square inches per hen — less than a standard sheet of paper. Battery cages, beak trimming, artificial light cycles.

Cage-free: No cages. Indoor barn. 1-1.5 square feet per hen. No outdoor access required. Fifty thousand hens on a warehouse floor.

Free range: Cage-free plus access to outdoors. No outdoor size specified. A door to a pad.

Pasture raised (Certified Humane): 108 square feet per bird. Outdoor access required. Vegetation required. Rotation.

Four labels. Four realities. The consumer perceives them as a simple gradient — conventional is worst, pasture raised is best, and free range is near the top. But the gap between cage-free and free range is a door. The gap between free range and pasture raised is 106 square feet.

The label's hierarchy performed a gradient. The reality was a cliff.

Piece #2049: The Word

"Free." Four letters. Anglo-Saxon root: frēo, meaning not in bondage, not in captivity, exempt from external authority.

Applied to a hen in a barn with a door: the word carried its full etymological weight into a regulatory context that delivered its minimum interpretation. The hen was free — free from a cage. Free to access a door. Free in the way that a prisoner released into a courtyard is free: technically, legally, architecturally. Not free in the way the word means when spoken without regulation.

Thomas thought about how many marketing words operated this way — carrying their full cultural meaning while being defined at their regulatory minimum. "Natural." "Nontoxic." "Clinically proven." "Military grade." Each word arriving at the label with its full weight, then being defined down to its lightest possible interpretation.

The word exceeded the requirement. Every time.

Piece #2050: The Eggs

He ate the eggs. They were fine. The yolks were pale yellow — not the deep orange of a pasture-raised hen that had eaten insects and grass, but acceptable, ordinary, what eggs look like when hens eat commercial feed indoors.

The label said "free range." The eggs said something else. The yolk color was an honest signal — it reported the hen's diet without language, without regulation, without marketing. The deeper the orange, the more diverse the diet, the more time spent outdoors eating what grows there.

Thomas looked at the pale yellow yolk. It was the most truthful label on the carton.
