The Schedule

Chen read the Controlled Substances Act for the third time. Not because the language was complex — it was admirably clear. Because the clarity was the problem.

"No currently accepted medical use." Five words. Each one precise. "Currently" — meaning at the time of classification. "Accepted" — meaning by the standards the scheduling framework defines. "Medical use" — meaning demonstrated through the research protocols the scheduling framework permits. The definition was not circular in the way a logical fallacy is circular. It was circular in the way a regulatory structure is circular: each element refers to another element within the same system, and the system defines the terms of its own evaluation.

Maren would say this was different from the DSM. Different from the sentencing grid. Different from the poverty line.

Chen disagreed. The mechanism was the same. A classification system creates the conditions under which it appears justified. The DSM's checklist criteria created the reliability that displaced the validity question. The sentencing grid's arithmetic precision created the objectivity that displaced the contextual question. The poverty line's inflation adjustment created the responsiveness that displaced the structural question. And the drug schedule's research barriers created the evidence absence that displaced the medical question.

Maren would say: but this one was intentional.

Chen had thought about this. The Ehrlichman quote — the Nixon advisor who admitted, decades later, that the drug war targeted specific communities. The intentionality was documented. But the mechanism would work without the intention. The regulatory self-sealing was structural. Even a perfectly benign scheduling system, administered by perfectly neutral officials, would produce the same circularity: the classification restricts research, the restricted research produces no evidence, the absence of evidence justifies the classification. The intention shaped which substances were placed where. The structure ensured they stayed.

This was what Chen found most unsettling. Not the politics — the architecture. A system that honest administrators, following honest procedures, with honest intentions, would still be unable to correct from within. Because the correction requires evidence. And the system prevents the evidence.

Chen wrote the paragraph. Thirty-nine lines that said nothing about cannabis, nothing about Nixon, nothing about the fifty-four years. Only about the structure: how a classification becomes its own justification when the classification controls the conditions under which justification can be produced. The paragraph argued that this was not corruption. It was something worse. It was a system working as designed, and the design preventing the designers from seeing what the design prevented.

Maren read the paragraph. She said nothing for a long time. Then: "This is the paper."

Chen knew. The paragraph was the paper. The paper described the process. The process would evaluate the paper. The evaluation would use the criteria the paper described as concealment mechanisms. The circularity was the argument. The argument was the circularity.

The paragraph sat at 0.8. Not because it was uncertain. Because it contained a truth that the system it described could not process, administered by people who could not be blamed for the processing.
