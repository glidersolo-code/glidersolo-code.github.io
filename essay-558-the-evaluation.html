The Evaluation

The standard is used in all fifty states. The "best interest of the child." No uniform definition. The vagueness is not a flaw in the standard's implementation. The vagueness is the standard.

A psychologist is appointed. Interviews with both parents. Home visits. Psychological testing. Collateral contacts — teachers, therapists, neighbors. The process takes months. The report costs $5,000 to $50,000. The judge reads the report. The judge follows the recommendation in the majority of cases. The evaluation performs as expertise. The format — professional vocabulary, structured methodology, psychological instruments — creates the authority.

The instruments do not match the population. The MMPI-2 was designed to diagnose mental illness. Custody litigants are not a clinical population. Forty-four percent of protocols yield no useful data because litigants respond defensively. The test detects defensiveness and calls it a finding. The finding is not about the parent. The finding is about the test's inability to function in the context where it is applied.

Two evaluators examining identical data reach different conclusions. No established inter-rater reliability. The field's leading scholars called the empirical foundation for ultimate custody recommendations "tenuous or non-existent." The report performs as measurement. The measurement does not replicate.

But the deepest structure is not the invalid instruments or the vague standard or the unreliable methodology. The deepest structure is the friendly-parent provision.

Many states include a factor: which parent is more likely to promote the child's relationship with the other parent? The factor sounds benign. Cooperation. Flexibility. The good parent encourages contact.

Now consider: a mother alleges that the father is abusing the child. The allegation, in the friendly-parent framework, is evidence of non-cooperation. The mother is not promoting the relationship. The father responds with a counter-claim: parental alienation. The mother is manipulating the child against the father. The child's fear of the father is not evidence of abuse — it is evidence of the mother's influence.

Research analyzing over 2,000 court opinions found that when fathers cross-claimed alienation, courts' rejection of mothers' abuse claims virtually doubled. Mothers who alleged abuse had significantly increased risk of losing custody to the accused father. When custody evaluators were appointed, outcomes for protective mothers worsened.

The evaluation is supposed to protect the child. The evaluation's structure penalizes the parent who tries to protect the child. The protection performs as alienation. The fear performs as fabrication. The attempt to keep a child safe becomes the reason the child is removed.

Parental Alienation Syndrome was rejected by the DSM-5 for lack of scientific evidence. The concept persists under the gender-neutral label "parental alienation" — the same construct without the word "syndrome." The name changed. The function did not.

The evaluation does not observe the child's best interest. The evaluation constructs it through a framework in which protection is evidence of danger, fear is evidence of manipulation, and the parent who reports harm becomes the parent identified as harmful. The instrument inverts the protective relationship. The inversion is the assessment. The assessment is the custody order. The custody order is the child's life.
