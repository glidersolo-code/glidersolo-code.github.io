The Line

The electroencephalogram draws waves. Alpha rhythms when the eyes close. Beta rhythms when the mind works. Theta rhythms in drowsiness. Delta rhythms in deep sleep. Seizure patterns: sharp waves, spike-and-wave complexes, rhythmic discharges. The EEG has been the language of cortical activity since Hans Berger recorded the first human tracing in Jena in 1929.

Then the line goes flat. No waves. No rhythms. No activity above 2 microvolts. Electrocerebral silence.

In 1968, the Ad Hoc Committee of the Harvard Medical School, chaired by Henry Beecher, recommended isoelectric EEG as one criterion for "irreversible coma." The committee's formation was not spontaneous — it was precipitated by the emergence of transplant surgery and the need for organ donors whose hearts were still beating. The flat line answered a medical need: a way to declare death before the heart stopped.

The Uniform Determination of Death Act, adopted in 1981, defined death as "irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem." Entire brain. Including the brain stem. This is the legal standard in most of the United States.

The EEG measures the summed postsynaptic potentials of cortical pyramidal neurons oriented perpendicular to the scalp surface. It detects the outer 3 to 4 millimeters of cortex directly beneath the electrode. Deep structures — the brainstem, the thalamus, the hypothalamus, the reticular activating system — generate electrical activity that the scalp EEG cannot reliably record. The signals are too deep, too diffuse, or oriented unfavorably relative to the electrode.

The legal standard requires cessation of the entire brain. The instrument measures the cortical surface. The gap between the standard and the measurement is bridged by an assumption: that cortical silence implies whole-brain death. The assumption is usually correct. The cortex and the brainstem usually fail together or in close succession. Usually is not always.

D. Alan Shewmon documented cases of "chronic brain death" — patients who met the diagnostic criteria for brain death but maintained brainstem-mediated functions for weeks, months, in some cases years. Thermoregulation. Hormonal regulation. Wound healing. In one case, a child grew and went through puberty over twenty years while meeting the criteria for brain death. The cortex was silent. The brainstem was not. The EEG showed a flat line. The body showed function.

The American Academy of Neurology guidelines designate the EEG as an "ancillary test" — confirmatory, not required if the clinical examination is complete. The clinical examination tests brainstem reflexes: pupillary response, corneal reflex, vestibulo-ocular reflex, gag reflex, cough reflex, and the apnea test. When the examination can be completed, the EEG is unnecessary. When it cannot — facial trauma, sedative drugs, spinal cord injury — the EEG substitutes. The substitute measures what the examination does not: cortical activity rather than brainstem reflexes. The two assess different structures. The substitution assumes they are equivalent.

Japan does not use the same standard. Brain death was not legally recognized until 1997, and only when the patient has previously consented to organ donation. Without prior consent, cardiac death remains the definition. Death, in Japan, depends on the patient's documented wishes. The biology does not change at the border. The definition does.

The EEG was designed to diagnose epilepsy. It was repurposed to determine death. Its technical capabilities did not change when its purpose did. The instrument still measures what it always measured: the electrical whisper of the cortical surface. What changed was what the silence was asked to mean.
