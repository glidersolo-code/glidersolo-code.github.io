Vignette: The Overdraft

Maren is looking at her bank statement. She points to a line.

"Overdraft protection fee. Thirty-five dollars."

"What did you buy?" Chen asks.

"Coffee. Four dollars and twelve cents."

"And your balance was?"

"Twenty-three something. The coffee put me under by like fifty cents."

"So the bank completed the four-dollar transaction and charged you thirty-five dollars for the service."

"For the protection."

"That's the word." Chen sits down. "Overdraft protection. You opted in when you opened the account."

"The teller said it would protect me from having transactions declined."

"And it did. Your coffee went through. Without the protection, the card would have been declined. Cost of the decline: zero dollars."

Maren is quiet.

"You were protected from a free event. The protection cost thirty-five dollars. The alternative — a declined transaction — costs nothing. Regulation E requires opt-in for exactly this reason: the customer must affirmatively choose to be charged instead of declined."

"But the way they asked — 'Do you want overdraft protection?' — of course I said yes. Who says no to protection?"

"That's the species. The one hundred and seventh. Protection-as-extraction. The name performs service. The function performs extraction. The customer chose it because the name said the right word. 'Do you want to pay thirty-five dollars instead of having your card declined for free' is the same question. Nobody asks it that way."

"And Wells Fargo?"

"Processed transactions from largest to smallest instead of chronologically. If you made five small purchases and one large one, the large one hit first, draining the balance, and every small one after that triggered its own thirty-five-dollar fee. Four fees instead of one. Same transactions. Same account. Same day. The processing order was undisclosed because the processing order was the product. Two hundred and three million dollar settlement."

"Who pays the most fees?"

"Nine percent of accounts generate eighty percent of all overdraft fees. Black consumers are eighty-four percent more likely to frequently overdraft. Twenty-one percent of Black adults paid an overdraft fee in 2024. Nine percent of white adults. The fee is thirty-five dollars for everyone. The wealth gap determines who lives close to zero. Proximity to zero determines overdraft frequency."

"The CFPB capped it."

"At five dollars. December 2024. Estimated savings: five billion per year for twenty-three million households. Congress repealed the rule in 2025."

Maren looks at her statement. "The seventh."

"The seventh dollar-format concealment. Property tax: the dollar is a date. Bail: a wealth test. Tipping: a paycheck. Chargemaster: a negotiating position. Municipal fine: a budget line. Payday fee: a rate. Overdraft fee: 'protection.'"

"The only one that claims to help you."

"The only one that names itself as a benefit. The others don't pretend. The overdraft fee pretends. The word 'protection' is the deepest layer — not just a dollar performing one function while executing another, but a dollar performing extraction while the name performs care."

Maren circles the $35 on her statement. "Thirty-five dollars of care."

"Two hundred and eighty billion dollars of care since 2000."
