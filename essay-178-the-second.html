Essay 178: The Second

The second was redefined in 1967.

Before the redefinition, the second was astronomical — 1/86,400 of a mean solar day, refined in 1960 to a fraction of the tropical year 1900. After the redefinition, the second was atomic: 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation corresponding to the transition between two hyperfine levels of the cesium-133 atom. The unit of time was disconnected from the phenomenon it originally measured. The Earth's rotation, which had been the source of the second, became a thing measured in seconds that had nothing to do with it.

The dependency was inverted. The Earth does not keep atomic time. Atomic time keeps the Earth. When the two diverge — which they do, because the Earth's rotation decelerates irregularly while the cesium clock does not — the difference accumulates, and a correction is applied. The correction is the leap second. Between 1972 and 2024, twenty-seven leap seconds were inserted into UTC, each one adding 23:59:60 to the clock, each one a confession that the two systems the clock was pretending to be one were not one.

This is the concealment: not the existence of two times — any metrologist will explain the TAI/UT1 distinction — but the infrastructure's treatment of UTC as a single, continuous, sufficient standard. The discontinuities are announced by the International Earth Rotation Service, published in bulletins, absorbed by network time protocols, and invisible to every clock on every wall. The time displayed on a hospital monitor, a trading terminal, a GPS receiver, is UTC. UTC is two things presented as one. The presentation is the concealment.

The leap second cannot be predicted. Unlike the leap day, which follows a formula (divisible by 4, except by 100, except by 400), the leap second depends on the Earth's rotational dynamics — tidal friction, volcanic eruptions, glacial rebound, core-mantle coupling. The irregularity is not a failure of the prediction system. The irregularity is the measurement reporting what it finds: that the Earth's rotation is governed by processes whose complexity exceeds any formula. The decision to insert a leap second is made six months in advance, based on observation, not calculation.

In 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures voted to abolish the leap second by 2035. The decision was not to reconcile atomic time and astronomical time. The decision was to stop stitching them together — to let UTC drift from UT1, choosing the atomic clock's monotonicity over the Earth's rotation. The systems that assumed time was monotonic — financial trading, satellite navigation, telecommunications, the Linux kernel — could not absorb the irregular discontinuity. The solution was not to fix the seam but to abandon the pretense of unity. To let one time go.

Thirty-seven seconds. That is the accumulated difference between TAI and UTC as of 2024. Thirty-seven moments when the Earth fell behind the atom by enough to require correction, each one inserted by international agreement, each one immediately absorbed back into the display of a single time on a single clock. The number 37 is visible in the BIPM's Circular T. It is invisible on every clock face in the world.

The second is a count of cesium oscillations. The clock displays it as a time of day. The display looks astronomical. The count is atomic. The gap between them — 37 seconds and growing — is the distance between what the unit measures and what the display means, which is the distance at which concealment operates: not by hiding but by presenting two things as one so seamlessly that the seam becomes visible only when it breaks.
