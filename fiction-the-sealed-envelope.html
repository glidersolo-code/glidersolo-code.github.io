Part CDXCVIII: The Sealed Envelope

Piece #2931: The Envelope

Thomas holds a sealed envelope.

Not a letter. Not a document. A collection. Inside: swabs, slides, hair, blood, fibers from clothing. A woman's body translated into biological evidence across four to six hours of examination. Photographed head to toe. Combed. Drawn. The most invasive medical procedure many will ever endure.

The envelope enters a shelf.

The shelf is in a warehouse in Detroit. The warehouse holds 11,341 envelopes. Some have been there since 1984. The woman who endured the exam believed the evidence would be used. The system that collected the evidence decided otherwise.

Thomas does not open the envelope. He notes that the envelope's existence performs as response. The collection happened. The investigation stopped. The space between those two sentences is twenty-five years.

Piece #2932: The Warehouse

Thomas counts the warehouses.

Detroit: 11,341 kits. Memphis: 12,000. Cleveland: 4,800. Los Angeles: 12,600. Nationally: somewhere between 200,000 and 400,000. The number is uncertain because no one counted the uncounted. The backlog was invisible to itself.

Each kit represents a body. Each body endured the exam. Each exam was supposed to generate an investigation. The kits generated nothing. They sat on shelves accumulating dust while the offenders they contained accumulated victims.

When Detroit tested all its kits, 841 suspected serial offenders emerged across forty states. The number is not a statistic about evidence. The number is a measure of how many people were harmed after the evidence was collected and before it was processed. The warehouse was not a storage facility. The warehouse was a policy.

Piece #2933: The Decision

Thomas finds the decision that created the warehouse.

Not a policy memo. Not a directive. A series of individual judgments, recorded nowhere, about which kits to submit for testing. A DOJ-funded study found that victim credibility assessments — subjective judgments about believability — were more influential than resource constraints.

Eighty-six percent of the victims were people of color. Eighty-one percent were Black women. The prosecutor said: the value of a life decreases as skin gets darker.

Every dollar spent on testing returns eighty-one. Testing all samples doubles CODIS-eligible profiles. The economics never blocked the processing. What blocked it was a judgment about whose body produced evidence worth examining.

Thomas notes that the judgment was never written down. It existed as a practice. The practice existed as a backlog. The backlog existed as a number — clearance rate, prosecution rate, serial offender identification rate — that looked like a property of crime rather than a property of the decision.

Piece #2934: The Destruction

Thomas reads a report: between 2010 and 2018, twenty-five agencies in fourteen states destroyed rape kits before the statute of limitations expired.

To make space.

The evidence was collected from a body. Sealed. Labeled. Stored. Neglected. Destroyed. The instrument's lifecycle was the justice system's lifecycle for the crime. Collection performed as response. Storage performed as preservation. Neglect performed as institutional delay. Destruction performed as space management. At no point did the vocabulary describe what was actually happening: the elimination of evidence before the law ran out.

Thomas notes that the statute of limitations is a clock. The kit is evidence. The destruction is a decision to let the clock beat the evidence. The decision was made by the same institution that collected the evidence. The institution collected the evidence from the body, stored the evidence on a shelf, and then destroyed the evidence before the clock expired. The institution was both the investigator and the obstacle to investigation.

Piece #2935: The Opening

Thomas imagines the moment a twenty-five-year-old kit is opened.

Inside: the swabs, the slides, the hair. Still viable. The DNA profile enters CODIS. A match appears. The match connects to another match. The matches connect to a pattern. The pattern reveals a serial offender who committed eleven assaults across two decades while the evidence sat in a warehouse.

The opening of the kit did not discover the serial offender. The non-opening of the kit created the serial offender — not the person, who existed regardless, but the serial pattern, which was invisible as long as each case sat unconnected on a separate shelf.

Thomas notes that the kit constitutes what it claims to observe. Without testing, there is no match. Without a match, there is no pattern. Without a pattern, there is no serial offender. The offender exists. The pattern does not exist until the instrument creates it. The instrument sat on a shelf for twenty-five years. The pattern accumulated victims during every one of those years. The instrument's silence was not the absence of information. The instrument's silence was the condition for the pattern to continue.
