Part CLIX: The Clarity

Piece #1236: The Disk

A white circle lowered on a rope. Twenty centimeters across. It descends into the water. At some depth, it disappears — the eye can no longer distinguish it from the surrounding lake. The depth is recorded. This is the Secchi disk measurement, invented aboard the papal yacht in 1865 by Angelo Secchi, a Jesuit astronomer who was studying the Mediterranean, not freshwater lakes. The measurement is a function of particle density, dissolved organic matter, algal concentration, sun angle, wave action, and the observer's visual acuity. The measurement is recorded as a property of the lake. The observer vanishes from the data. The disk's disappearance, which depends on who is looking and when, becomes a fact about the water.

Piece #1237: The Index

Robert Carlson, Kent State University, 1977. "A Trophic State Index for Lakes," published in Limnology and Oceanography. Three variables: Secchi depth, chlorophyll-a concentration, total phosphorus. Each transformed logarithmically to a scale of 0 to 100. Below 40: oligotrophic. Between 40 and 50: mesotrophic. Above 50: eutrophic. The three indices need not agree. A lake can be oligotrophic by chlorophyll and eutrophic by phosphorus. Carlson intended the divergence as diagnostic — the gap between indices reveals which processes dominate. In practice, agencies report one number. Which number depends on which variable they measured. Which variable they measured depends on which parameter the monitoring budget covered. The lake's classification is a function of the agency's funding decisions, encoded as a fact about the ecosystem.

Piece #1238: The Origin

Einar Naumann, Lund, 1919. August Thienemann, Plön, 1921. Two limnologists describing the lakes around them in northern Germany and southern Sweden. Nutrient-poor lakes with low productivity: oligotroph. Nutrient-rich lakes with high productivity: eutroph. The binary was descriptive and regional — it captured what these researchers saw in temperate, seasonally stratified, relatively deep lakes. The binary traveled. By mid-century it was applied worldwide. By 1977 it was formalized into a numerical index. The index was calibrated for North American temperate lakes. Tropical lakes, shallow lakes, polymictic lakes, saline lakes — all have different nutrient-productivity relationships. William Lewis documented the systematic differences in 2000: tropical lakes have faster nutrient cycling, shorter water residence times, different light regimes. The TSI boundaries were never recalibrated. A system designed for the lakes of northern Europe and temperate North America is the global standard for categorizing every lake on Earth.

Piece #1239: The Directive

The European Union Water Framework Directive, 2000/60/EC. Twenty-seven member states. All surface water bodies classified by ecological status: high, good, moderate, poor, bad. The classification depends, in part, on trophic state indicators — phytoplankton biomass, nutrient concentrations, transparency. The boundary between "good" and "moderate" triggers regulatory consequences: action plans, funding allocations, discharge restrictions, monitoring requirements. The boundary is set using reference conditions — the state the water body would be in without human disturbance. Reference conditions are estimated, not measured: they are back-calculated from minimally impacted sites of the same lake type, or modeled, or borrowed from expert judgment. The regulatory boundary — the line between compliance and non-compliance, between a lake that requires intervention and a lake that does not — is anchored to an estimate of a condition that no longer exists, measured by an index calibrated for a different geography.

Piece #1240: The Color

Humic lakes are brown. Dissolved organic matter absorbs light. The water is dark, the Secchi depth is low, the TSI says eutrophic. But the algal biomass is low — light limitation prevents photosynthesis despite abundant nutrients. The lake is dark and clear of algae. The lake is oligotrophic by biology and eutrophic by index. The system sees the darkness and classifies it as productivity. The system was designed for clear-water lakes where transparency and productivity are inversely related. In humic lakes, the relationship breaks down. The index cannot tell the difference between water darkened by algae and water darkened by dissolved tannins. The darkness means two different things. The index has one word for both.
