The Wattage

A microwave oven box says 1100 watts. The consumer reads this as cooking power. In many cases it is not.

Two wattages exist: input power (what the magnetron draws from the wall) and output power (the microwave energy delivered to the food). Output is typically 60 to 65 percent of input. An "1100-watt" microwave may cook at 700 watts. The number on the box may describe either. Some manufacturers label output power, measured by IEC 60705 — a water-heating test: one liter of water, controlled starting temperature, measured temperature rise, power calculated from energy transferred. Others label input power, which is larger and therefore more marketable. Some label a nominal wattage from the magnetron's specification sheet. The consumer has no way to determine which wattage the box describes, because the number appears without a qualifier.

Recipes compound the ambiguity. A frozen dinner reads: "Microwave on high for 3 minutes (1100W)." The recipe developer tested on an output-rated microwave. The consumer's microwave is marketed by input power. The consumer reads "1100W" on both the box and the package and concludes compatibility. The food comes out undercooked by 30 to 40 percent, because the recipe assumed 1100 watts reaching the food and the microwave delivers 700. The same number on two different objects — appliance and food package — references two different measurements, and no convention establishes which one recipes intend.

Power levels add another layer of mismatch between label and experience. "Power level 5" does not reduce the magnetron's output by half. In most microwaves, it cycles the magnetron on and off — full power for half the time, zero for the other half. Average power is halved, but the food receives intermittent bursts of full intensity. The on-periods deposit energy unevenly; the off-periods allow thermal diffusion to partially equalize. The result is a different thermal profile from true half-power, which only inverter microwaves — Panasonic introduced the technology in 1988 — actually deliver. "Power level 5" on a cycling microwave and "Power level 5" on an inverter microwave produce different results from the same display.

The interior capacity — 1.6 cubic feet, 1.1 cubic feet — is listed separately from the wattage. The consumer compares watts and volume independently. But the ratio that determines cooking speed for a given load — watts per cubic foot of cavity — appears nowhere. A 700-watt microwave in a 1.6-cubic-foot cavity cooks more slowly than the same 700 watts in a 1.1-cubic-foot cavity, because the same energy fills a larger space. The consumer who upgrades to a larger microwave at the same wattage gets a slower oven and attributes the difference to the food rather than the appliance.

The consumer holds a number — 1100 watts — that may be input, output, or nominal; that recipes assume is output; that the box presents without distinction; that power levels modulate by time-division rather than amplitude; and that means nothing without the cavity volume it heats. The number is on the box, in the advertisement, on the spec sheet. It is precise, prominent, and consistently insufficient.
