Vignette Chen The Expectancy

Vignette

"Seventy-seven years," Maren said, looking at the report on his laptop. "That's what we get."

"That's not what we get," Chen said.

"It's right there. Life expectancy at birth, United States, seventy-seven years."

"It's not a prediction. It's a snapshot."

Maren turned from the screen. "Explain."

"They take one year's death rates — infant mortality, car accidents, heart disease, cancer, everything — and they run them through a formula. The formula asks: if a hypothetical person experienced these exact rates at every age, birth to death, how long would they live? Seventy-seven. But no real person will experience one year's death rates for a whole lifetime."

"Because the rates change."

"Every year. A baby born today will face 2027's death rates at age one, 2040's rates at fourteen, 2080's rates at fifty-four. Rates that don't exist yet."

"So the number describes..."

"This year. Not a lifetime. It describes the present tense of mortality."

"But the word says 'expectancy.' What you can expect."

Chen nodded. "That's the work the word does. A 'summary of current-year age-specific death rates' — nobody publishes that headline. 'Life expectancy drops two years' — that headlines everywhere. The word transforms a description into a prediction."

"The pandemic drop."

"Seventy-eight-point-eight to seventy-six-point-four. Two-point-four years. But babies born in 2021 won't live two-point-four fewer years than babies born in 2019. They'll live through decades of declining COVID mortality. The snapshot captured one bad year and projected it across a hypothetical lifetime."

Maren was quiet for a moment. "What about the comparison? Japan eighty-four, US seventy-seven?"

"Real differences in current-year mortality — infant deaths, gun violence, healthcare access. The gap is meaningful. But it's a gap between two snapshots, not two futures. Both babies will live through mortality conditions that don't exist yet."

"So the whole number is..."

"Useful. Real. Technically correct. And consistently misread as a prediction, because the word 'expectancy' says prediction, and the unit 'years' says lifetime. The demographer computed a description. The public received a prophecy."

Maren closed the laptop. "How old are we?"

"You're thirty-six."

"So I have forty-one years left."

Chen smiled. "You have whatever's left. The number can't tell you. It can only tell you about the year it was calculated."
