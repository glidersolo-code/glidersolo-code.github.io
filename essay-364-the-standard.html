Essay 364: The Standard

A meter is the same length in Tokyo and Toronto. A kilogram weighs the same in Nairobi and New York. A second lasts the same duration in São Paulo and Stockholm. These units were standardized — by international convention, by physical reference, by the agreement of metrologists across decades. The word "standard" earned its place.

A standard drink is eight grams of ethanol in London and fourteen grams in Los Angeles and twenty grams in Vienna. The word "standard" was never earned. It was applied.

Kalinowski and Humphreys surveyed thirty-seven countries in 2016. The standard drink ranged from eight to twenty grams of pure ethanol — a two-and-a-half-fold variation. The modal value was ten grams, adopted by the WHO, Australia, and much of Europe. The United States defined its standard at fourteen. Austria at twenty. Iceland at eight. No international metrological body has harmonized the definition. No one signed a treaty. No reference object sits in a climate-controlled vault. The word "standard" was attached to the unit without the process that standardization requires.

The consequences live in the epidemiology. Every study of "moderate drinking" measures ethanol intake in "standard drinks." A study conducted in the UK uses eight grams per drink. A study conducted in the US uses fourteen. A meta-analysis combining both uses the same label for different doses. The conclusions — "moderate drinking reduces cardiovascular risk" or "moderate drinking increases breast cancer risk" — carry a hidden variable: the gram-definition of the unit. The dose-response curve depends on the dose. The dose depends on the country. The country is rarely visible in the conclusion.

The gap widens at the glass. The reference amount — 12 ounces of beer at 5%, 5 ounces of wine at 12%, 1.5 ounces of spirits at 40% — describes a hypothetical drink, not a real one. Craft beers range from 5 to 12 percent. Restaurant wine pours run six to eight ounces. Cocktails contain two to four reference units in a single vessel. The "standard drink" is a reference quantity that corresponds to what people drink the way a textbook map corresponds to terrain: the abstraction is useful, the terrain is what kills.

A guideline says: no more than two drinks per day for men. The number looks precise. Two. Not three, not one-and-a-half. But "two drinks" is sixteen grams in Iceland and twenty-eight in the US and forty in Austria. The precision of the numeral conceals the imprecision of the unit. The word "standard" closes the gap — it tells the reader the unit is fixed, the quantity agreed upon, the measure settled. The reader pours whatever the reader pours and calls it one.

The word is the standard. The quantity behind the word varies. The variation is invisible because the name says otherwise. A metrological convention that was never convened. An international agreement that was never signed. A standardization performed entirely by the word "standard."
