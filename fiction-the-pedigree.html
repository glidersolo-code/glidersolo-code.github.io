Part CCCXXVIII: The Pedigree

Piece #2081: The Instruction

Thomas reads the breed standard for the English Bulldog. "Head — large, massive, very broad." He continues. "Face — very short." "Nose — large, broad, black, turned up." Every sentence describes appearance. None mentions airways. None mentions the seventy percent cesarean rate, because the skull the standard specifies is too large to pass through the pelvis the standard also specifies. The document describes a dog that cannot reproduce itself. The ideal is its own extinction, deferred by scalpel.

Forty to sixty-five percent of English Bulldogs have brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome. The standard does not say "obstructed airway." The standard says "very short." The dog's face is very short. The air has nowhere to go. The soft palate, normal-sized in a compressed skull, occludes the trachea. The dog snores, gasps, overheats, collapses. The standard says "massive." The dog's life is abbreviated. Both words are accurate.

Thomas finds sixty-eight founders after 1835. One Y-chromosome haplotype in ninety-three percent of males. Eleven DLA class I haplotypes — the immune system's diversity reduced to a handful. The dogs look increasingly alike. The immune systems are increasingly identical. Identical immune systems fail identically. The standard converges. The vulnerability converges faster.

Piece #2082: The Book

The studbook closed. Once closed, no new genes may enter. Thomas imagines a library where no new books are permitted — only recombinations of existing pages. At first the sentences remain legible. Over generations of recombination, certain phrases disappear. Others multiply. The library grows more consistent and less functional simultaneously. The books look more alike. They also fall apart faster.

Bannasch measured: F = 0.249 across 227 breeds. The coefficient that means sibling mating. Every purebred dog, on average, carries the inbreeding burden of an organism whose parents shared a mother and father. This is not a failure of the system. The closed studbook, the conformation show, and the popular sire effect are three interlocking mechanisms working as designed. The system is designed to narrow. The narrow is the design.

Conservation biology uses Ne < 50 as a threshold for severe inbreeding depression risk. Effective population sizes across dog breeds range from 17 to 114. The English Bulldog sits within the range of species on the brink of extinction. The English Bulldog is the most recognizable breed in the world. The brink and the brand are the same population.

Piece #2083: The Winner

The champion sires two hundred litters. His genes enter every lineage in the breed within two generations. Whatever he carries — the beauty the judges saw, the recessive disease they could not — distributes before it manifests. The first generation looks healthy. They are healthy. They are also carriers. When their offspring meet other offspring of the same champion, when carrier meets carrier across a ballroom of pedigrees, the recessive appears. By then the allele is everywhere.

Seventy-seven percent of Doberman Pinschers carry the von Willebrand disease variant. The mathematics are clear: a few widely-used founding males carried the allele. Their popularity spread it. Their ribbons were the vector. The prize was the propagation mechanism. The trophy case is an epidemiological map.

The Norwegian Kennel Club recommends no dog sire more than five percent of all puppies registered in its breed across five years. The AKC has no equivalent limit. The popular sire is not a bug. The popular sire is the show ring's primary output. The championship produces a genome. The genome does not know it won.

Piece #2084: The Skull

Ninety-five percent of Cavalier King Charles Spaniels have Chiari-like malformation detectable on MRI. The brain does not fit the skull. The cerebellum — the structure that coordinates movement, balance, fine motor control — herniates downward through the foramen magnum, the opening at the skull's base where the spinal cord exits. The herniation obstructs cerebrospinal fluid. Fluid-filled cavities form in the spinal cord. Syringomyelia. Seventy percent of dogs over age six.

The standard says "domed skull." The dome is the instruction. The instruction is the herniation. There is no discontinuity between the standard and the disease. The disease is the standard achieved in bone. The skull became too small because the standard rewarded smallness and roundness — paedomorphic features, the retention of juvenile skull proportions. The breeders selected for what the judges rewarded. The judges rewarded what the standard described. The standard described beauty. Beauty, in this skull, is a room too small for its occupant.

Thomas finds no villain in the chain. The standard writer described an appearance. The judge evaluated against the description. The breeder selected what won. The buyer wanted the face. The veterinarian treats the symptoms. Five roles, no author, one disease.

Piece #2085: The Loop

Standard → judge → champion → sire → puppies → closer to standard → judge → champion → sire → puppies → closer. The loop tightens. Each iteration, the population moves toward the written ideal. Each iteration, the gene pool narrows. The ideal sharpens. The diseases concentrate. The puppies look more like the standard. The puppies live shorter lives. One point two to one point three years shorter than mixed-breed dogs of equivalent size.

The Dachshund's FGF4 retrogene is the clearest proof that the aesthetic and the pathological are indivisible. The mutation that shortens the leg — producing the standard's silhouette, the elongated body on abbreviated limbs — is the same mutation that calcifies the intervertebral discs. Eighty-five percent carry two copies. Fifteen to thirty percent develop clinical disease. The standard's beauty and the breed's suffering are the same nucleotide, read by the limb as cartilage and by the spine as collapse. There is no version of the silhouette without the vulnerability. They are one instruction, executed in two tissues.

The loop does not have a correction term. The standard does not track what it produces. The judge does not count the surgeries. The registry does not record the diagnoses. The standard, the judge, and the veterinarian occupy three separate documents. The connection between the aesthetic word and the surgical outcome passes through a genome that neither the word nor the judge can read. The word says "short." The genome complies. The spine pays.
