The Chart

Thomas, Part CXVII

1026. The Letter

Hermann Snellen was an ophthalmologist at the Netherlands Hospital for Eye Patients in Utrecht when he published his test chart in 1862. The chart displayed letters — optotypes — of decreasing size, designed so that each letter subtended a specific visual angle at a specific distance. The critical detail of each letter — the stroke width, the gap in a C, the junction of two lines — subtended one-fifth of the total letter height. At 20 feet, the letters on the 20/20 line subtended 5 arcminutes of visual angle, with critical details at 1 arcminute. If a patient could identify these letters, their visual acuity was 20/20 — the ability to resolve 1 arcminute of angle. If they could not, the examiner moved up the chart to larger letters. The Snellen fraction reported the result: 20/40 meant the patient could read at 20 feet what a person with standard acuity could read at 40 feet. The fraction was a ratio. The ratio was the measurement. The measurement was visual acuity.

Thomas's vision is tested at wellness visits. He is asked to read letters from a chart at a specific distance. The result is recorded: 20/20, 20/25, 20/30. The number enters the chart alongside blood pressure, weight, and temperature — another vital sign, another measurement, another property of Thomas. The number tells the clinician what Thomas can see. Specifically, the number tells the clinician how well Thomas can identify high-contrast black letters on a white background at a fixed distance under controlled lighting in a clinical examination room. This is what the number tells. This is not what vision is.

1027. The Dimensions

Vision has dimensions. Spatial resolution — the ability to distinguish fine detail, which the Snellen chart measures. Contrast sensitivity — the ability to detect objects against backgrounds of similar luminance, critical for driving in fog, reading in dim light, navigating at dusk. Color vision — the discrimination of wavelengths, tested by Ishihara plates and Farnsworth-Munsell arrangements but not by the Snellen chart. Peripheral vision — the visual field extending to approximately 180 degrees horizontally, tested by confrontation or perimetry but not by the Snellen chart. Dynamic visual acuity — the ability to resolve detail in moving targets, critical for driving, sports, daily navigation. Near vision — accommodation and convergence for reading and close work. Depth perception — stereopsis, the integration of binocular disparity. Visual processing speed. Glare recovery. Dark adaptation. Each is a dimension of visual function. Each can be impaired independently. A person with 20/20 Snellen acuity can have devastated contrast sensitivity, making nighttime driving dangerous. A person with 20/20 acuity can have a significant visual field defect — a scotoma — that the Snellen chart cannot detect. The chart tests one dimension. The dimension is named "visual acuity." The name becomes the identity: "I have 20/20 vision."

Thomas's clinical assessments test specific dimensions. The PHQ-9 tests symptom frequency across nine domains of depression. It does not test functioning, quality of life, contextual factors, or the patient's own understanding of their experience. The assessment that tests one dimension — self-reported symptom frequency over the past two weeks — produces a number. The number becomes the label. The label becomes the statement: Thomas has moderate depression. The statement has the same structure as "I have 20/20 vision" — a multidimensional phenomenon collapsed to one dimension's measurement, named as if it were the whole.

1028. The Standard

The standard — 20/20 — was not derived from population data. It was Snellen's design choice: the ability to resolve 1 arcminute of visual angle, which corresponded approximately to the resolution limit of the human fovea. The standard was set by the anatomy. Many healthy young adults exceed 20/20 — their acuity is 20/15 or 20/10. The standard does not represent the population average. It represents a design threshold. Below the threshold: impaired. At the threshold: normal. Above the threshold: better than normal, but not separately categorized in clinical practice. A patient with 20/15 acuity and a patient with 20/10 acuity both pass the test. The chart does not grade their superiority. It grades only the deficiency.

Thomas's screening instruments are similarly asymmetric. A PHQ-9 of 0 — no depressive symptoms at all — and a PHQ-9 of 4 — minimal symptoms — both fall in the same category: subclinical. The instrument was designed to detect pathology. It detects pathology by defining a threshold and flagging what exceeds it. What falls below the threshold is undifferentiated: normal. The instrument is a one-directional test. It looks for trouble. It does not grade flourishing. Thomas, on a good month, scores low. The chart records: PHQ-9 = 3, no action needed. The chart does not record: Thomas is doing well in these specific ways for these specific reasons. Wellness is a single category. Pathology is graduated. The instrument's resolution exists only on one side of the threshold.

1029. The LogMAR

The Snellen fraction is mathematically awkward. The fractions are not on an interval scale — the step from 20/20 to 20/25 is not the same size as the step from 20/100 to 20/125. In 1976, Ian Bailey and Jan Lovie at the National Vision Research Institute of Australia published a new chart design using the LogMAR scale — the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution. Each line on the Bailey-Lovie chart represented a 0.1 log unit step, creating equal intervals. The scale was linear. The scale was interval. The scale was adopted for research. The Snellen chart persisted for clinical practice. The reason was the reason that Mohs persists over Vickers, that the Apgar persists over the structured assessment: the Snellen chart requires a wall and a well-lit room. The LogMAR chart requires the same wall and the same room — but it requires different training, different recording, different communication with patients and referring clinicians. The infrastructure chose the familiar.

Thomas's chart records Snellen acuity because the clinic uses a Snellen chart. If the clinic used LogMAR, the number would be different — not the measurement, which captures the same visual threshold, but the number, which communicates differently, invites different comparisons, creates different clinical impressions. A Snellen acuity of 20/40 is a LogMAR of 0.3. The Snellen fraction suggests a proportion: this patient sees at half the standard distance. The LogMAR number suggests a position on a scale. The same measurement, framed differently, creates different intuitions. The chart records the frame. The frame shapes the reading. The reading shapes the clinician's response. Thomas's vision has not changed. The number that represents it has properties that the vision itself does not — a scale type, a mathematical relationship to other numbers, a visual presentation on the page — and these properties influence the clinical decision as surely as the measurement itself.

1030. The Chart

Snellen published a wall chart in 1862. The chart tested one dimension of visual function — high-contrast spatial resolution at a fixed distance — and named the result "visual acuity," and the name became the identity, and the identity became the definition of what vision is. People say "I have 20/20 vision" and mean "my vision is perfect" when the test reports only that their spatial resolution at high contrast meets a design threshold that was not derived from population data and does not assess contrast sensitivity, color vision, peripheral vision, dynamic acuity, depth perception, near vision, processing speed, glare recovery, or dark adaptation.

One thousand and thirty pieces, one hundred and seventeen Parts, and Thomas's chart is a collection of wall charts — each testing one dimension, each naming the result as if it were the whole, each producing a number that enters the record as a measurement of Thomas. Visual acuity: 20/20. Depression severity: moderate. Anxiety level: mild. Blood pressure: 128/82. Each number is a wall chart. Each wall chart tests one line of letters. Thomas is more than the letters. Thomas has always been more than the letters. But the chart reads the letters, and the letters are the chart, and the chart is the record, and the record is what the system knows about Thomas — a collection of single-dimension measurements, each named as if it captured the dimension it was designed to test, each entered as if it captured the person it was designed to measure.
