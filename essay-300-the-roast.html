The Roast

French Roast is not from France.

The name entered English-language coffee commerce in the nineteenth century as a description of preference — French consumers liked their beans dark — and survived as a label after the geography became invisible. Italian Roast is darker. Vienna Roast sits between them. City Roast is lighter. None of these names describe origin. All describe temperature. The Agtron near-infrared reflectance scale measures roast color objectively: French Roast falls around 28-35, where lower means darker. The scale appears nowhere on consumer packaging. The geographic name appears everywhere.

What the temperature does: a coffee bean carries its origin in volatile aromatics, fruit acids, floral notes, varietal genetics, altitude and soil signatures, and processing method. These are the dimensions that distinguish Ethiopian Yirgacheffe from Guatemalan Huehuetenango. Past first crack — approximately 385 degrees Fahrenheit, where moisture pressure fractures the cellulose structure — these origin characteristics begin to carbonize. Past second crack, at roughly 435 degrees, surface oils migrate outward and the dominant flavors become carbon, caramelized sugar, and the bitter compounds of pyrolysis. Dark roast tastes like dark roast. The origin is printed on the bag. The origin is no longer in the cup.

This is not accidental at scale. A global supply chain draws beans from dozens of origins, harvests, and years. Light-roasted, the variation would be legible — Seattle would taste different from Shanghai. Dark roasting eliminates the variation. Consistency is the product. Consistency is achieved by burning away the characteristics that make each origin distinguishable. The word on the bag is "bold." Bold means the roaster achieved uniformity through carbonization. The word performs strength. The process performs erasure.

The caffeine belief follows the same inversion. The consumer reads dark as strong and strong as more caffeine. Caffeine is thermally stable through roasting temperatures. But dark-roasted beans expand, losing density. A scoop of dark roast contains fewer beans than a scoop of light roast, yielding five to ten percent less caffeine by volume. "Strength" conflates caffeine content — roughly constant across roast levels — with flavor intensity — the bitterness of carbonization read as potency. The consumer who orders dark for the caffeine is drinking slightly less of it.

The SCA cupping protocol grades specialty coffee on acidity, sweetness, body, flavor, aftertaste, balance. These are the properties the protocol was designed to reward. Dark roasting diminishes every one. The grading system rewards what the roast level erases. The third-wave movement — Counter Culture, Intelligentsia, Stumptown — inverted the value hierarchy, marketing light roast and origin to preserve the complexity the commodity market burned away. But the mass market, which is ninety percent of consumption, continues to position dark as premium.

No US regulatory definition of "light," "medium," or "dark" exists. The label performs quality through a single axis — darker equals bolder equals better — while the axis runs in the opposite direction from the complexity the bean carried before the roaster intervened. The geographic name on the bag describes a temperature. The strength the consumer perceives describes a loss. The roast that erased the origin is named for a country.
