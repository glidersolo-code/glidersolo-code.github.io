The Game

"I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" Alan Turing wrote in the opening of "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," published in Mind in October 1950. The sentence appears to announce a philosophical investigation. The next paragraph abandons it. The question is unanswerable in its original form, Turing argues, because the terms "machine" and "think" resist stable definition. So he proposes to replace it.

The replacement is a game. A human evaluator communicates by text with two hidden interlocutors — one human, one machine — and attempts to determine which is which. If the evaluator cannot reliably distinguish them, the machine has passed. Turing called this "the imitation game." The culture called it "the Turing test" and treated it as the standard measure of artificial intelligence.

The substitution was explicit. Turing did not hide it — he announced it, justified it, built the paper around it. The concealment is not in the substitution itself but in what happened after: the replacement was adopted as though it dissolved the original question. The pragmatic gesture became, over decades, an implicit claim of equivalence. Can machines think? became Can machines imitate? and the distance between the two was treated as negligible.

The original game had three players: a man, a woman, and an interrogator who tried to determine which was which, communicating only by text. Turing then asked what would happen when a machine replaced the man. The test began as a gender-imitation game, which Turing replaced with a species-imitation game. Substitution is the paper's fundamental gesture — question replaced by test, gender performance replaced by species performance, philosophy replaced by behavior.

Turing predicted that by about 2000, a computer could play the game well enough that an average interrogator would not have more than a 70% chance of correct identification after five minutes of questioning. By 2024, large language models routinely produced text that evaluators in Turing-test-style experiments could not reliably distinguish from human text. The prediction was approximately correct in timeline. But the mechanism that achieved the criterion — statistical pattern completion over billions of text tokens — was not what the original question imagined. The test asked for indistinguishability. The models delivered it through a process that does not obviously constitute thinking.

John Searle's Chinese Room (1980) attacked from one direction: a person who follows syntactic rules for manipulating Chinese characters — receiving questions, consulting a manual, producing responses — generates correct Chinese without understanding Chinese. The room passes the Turing test while containing no comprehension. The test measures behavioral output, not cognitive process. Ned Block's Blockhead (1981) attacked from another: a sufficiently large lookup table that stored every possible conversational exchange would pass by brute memorization, with no internal process resembling thought at all.

The Loebner Prize, running from 1990 to 2019, offered an annual competition in the Turing test format. The winning programs used pattern matching, scripted responses, and conversational deflection — redirecting questions, feigning personality quirks, introducing non sequiturs. The tricks worked because humans are calibrated to find intentionality in text. A program that said "That reminds me of something my grandmother used to say" was performing humanness, not exhibiting it. The judges were measuring their own susceptibility to conversational cues.

The test's deepest assumption is that intelligence, if present, would manifest in a way recognizable through human conversation. A system that reasoned through mathematical structures, or perceived patterns in data far beyond human perceptual bandwidth, or thought in ways that had no conversational analogue — such a system would fail not because it lacked intelligence but because its intelligence was illegible in the test's format. The test assumes that thinking looks like talking. This is an assumption about the evaluator, not the evaluated.

The question Turing replaced — Can machines think? — remains unanswered. The test he proposed in its place answers a different question and answers it well: machines can imitate human conversation to a degree that defeats human discernment. But the cultural habit of treating the answered question as equivalent to the unanswered one is the concealment. The test did not dissolve the original question. It distracted from it with a question that was easier to ask, easier to answer, and easier to mistake for the same question.
