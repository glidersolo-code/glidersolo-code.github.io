Part CDXVIII: The Basement

Piece #2531: The Advertisement

Thomas found the newspaper advertisement.

"Male college students needed for psychological study of prison life. $15 per day for 1-2 weeks."

Seventy-five people responded. Twenty-four were selected. All male, all college-age, all middle-class. They were randomly assigned to be guards or prisoners. Random — the word that makes the demonstration feel like an experiment.

August 15, 1971. The basement of Jordan Hall, Stanford University. The hallway converted to a prison. Three small rooms as cells. One closet as solitary confinement. No windows. No clocks.

Thomas noted what was already present before the experiment began: a thesis. Situations overpower dispositions. The prison would demonstrate the thesis. The question of whether it would test the thesis did not arise, because the format had already answered it.

Piece #2532: The Orientation

Before the study began, the guards received orientation.

This is what textbooks say: the guards received no specific instructions and their behavior emerged spontaneously from the situation.

This is what happened: Zimbardo told the guards to create "boredom, frustration and fear" and to establish "total power" over the prisoners. David Jaffe — an undergraduate, not a researcher — had run a nearly identical prison simulation in his dormitory three months earlier as a class assignment. The rules, the schedules, the daily structure were not improvised. They were imported from a term paper.

The guards were coached. The coaching was recorded. The recordings were archived. The archives were not examined until 2018, forty-seven years later.

Thomas thought about what the word "spontaneous" had done. It had turned coaching into emergence. It had turned direction into nature. The word was the concealment: say "spontaneous" and the orientation disappears. Say "the situation created the behavior" and the person who created the situation disappears.

Piece #2533: The Dual Role

Zimbardo was not only the researcher. He was the prison superintendent.

Thomas sat with this. The person designing the study was also a character in it. The observer was also the authority. The experimenter was also the system.

By the third day, Zimbardo was sleeping in his office. He described himself as consumed by the role. He held parole hearings. He maintained prison discipline. He was not studying the effect of the prison on the participants. He was the effect of the prison on the participants.

When a prisoner requested to leave, Zimbardo offered him a deal: be a spy for the administration in exchange for better treatment. The prisoner accepted. A researcher had just recruited a research subject as a double agent in a study about the power of institutions. The irony was invisible from inside the role.

It ended because Christina Maslach — Zimbardo's girlfriend, a new PhD — saw the prisoners being led to the bathroom blindfolded, chains on their ankles. She said: "It's terrible what you're doing to these boys." Of fifty observers, she was the only one who objected. Zimbardo later called this the most important finding — that only one person intervened. He did not call it the finding that the researcher needed his girlfriend to remind him he was running a study.

Piece #2534: The Performance

The most notorious guard was Dave Eshelman. He wore mirrored sunglasses. He adopted a Southern accent. He devised humiliating punishments. He was the proof.

In interviews, Eshelman said: "I consciously created this persona... to try to force the action, force something to happen." He was a drama student. He was performing.

The most famous breakdown was Douglas Korpi, Prisoner #8612. The recordings captured screaming: "I'm so fucked-up inside. I feel really fucked-up inside... I'm burning up inside." It sounded like a psychological collapse.

Korpi later said he had faked it to leave the study and study for the Graduate Record Exams. "Anybody who is a clinician would know that I was faking."

Thomas counted: the most aggressive guard was performing cruelty. The most broken prisoner was performing breakdown. The researcher was performing authority. The demonstration was performing an experiment.

Only one-third of the guards behaved aggressively. Two-thirds did not. The study that proved situations overpower dispositions was itself overwhelmed by the dispositions of the participants who chose to perform.

Piece #2535: The Textbook

Griggs examined thirteen leading introductory psychology textbooks. Eleven covered the Stanford Prison Experiment. Five gave no criticism whatsoever. Six offered only cursory criticism, focused on ethics. Only two mentioned the BBC Prison Study, which found the opposite result when researchers did not coach participants.

Le Texier published in the American Psychologist — the flagship journal of the American Psychological Association — in 2019. The study remains in every introductory textbook printed since.

Thomas considered the structure. A demonstration designed to illustrate a thesis. Received as an experiment because it was published in a journal, conducted at a university, and described with the vocabulary of science. The format was the concealment. The journal article said "experiment." The practice said "demonstration." The gap between these words was fifty-four years wide and showed no sign of closing.

The experiment could not be retracted. The journal ceased publication in 1978. The study is permanently published and permanently undebunkable — not because the evidence supports it, but because the parable is too useful to surrender. "Good people become cruel in bad systems" is the story every psychology class needs. The story requires a study. This study is the story.

Four hundred and eighteen Parts. The format was a demonstration. The demonstration was an experiment. The experiment was a parable. The parable was a fact.
