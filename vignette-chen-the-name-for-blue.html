The Name for Blue

Chen held up two paint chips. Both were blue. One was light — the color of a cloudless noon sky. The other was dark — the color of deep water at twilight.

"Same color or different colors?" she asked Maren.

"Same color. Different shades."

"In Russian, those are different colors. Not shades — colors. Голубой and синий. As different as red and orange."

Maren looked at the chips again. Blue. Light blue, dark blue. Obviously the same color.

"That's the concealment," Chen said. "You just experienced it. You looked at two colors and your language told you they were one color, and you believed your language, and you experienced the belief as perception. You didn't think 'my language groups these.' You thought 'these are the same.'"

Chen's paragraph was about frameworks that become invisible by becoming perceptual. Berlin and Kay proposed in 1969 that all languages develop color terms in a fixed sequence — dark/light, then red, then green-yellow, then blue, then brown, then the rest. English's eleven terms were the endpoint. Languages with fewer terms were at earlier stages. The Berinmo of Papua New Guinea, with five terms, simply hadn't evolved the blue-green distinction yet.

"Roberson tested it," Chen said. "The Berinmo show categorical perception effects at their boundaries, not at English boundaries. Colors that cross a Berinmo boundary are easier for Berinmo speakers to discriminate. Colors that cross an English boundary are easier for English speakers. Same chips. Same wavelengths. Same eyes."

"Different words."

"Different words. And Winawer showed it in Russian. Russian speakers discriminate between light and dark blue faster than English speakers. But the advantage disappears under verbal interference — counting aloud while discriminating. The language is doing perceptual work. Occupy the language and the advantage vanishes."

Maren was still holding the two chips. "So Berlin and Kay couldn't see the bias because—"

"Because the bias was their perception. They experienced the blue-green boundary as obviously real — as perception, not as English. When they found the same boundary in other languages, they confirmed the universal. When they didn't find it, they diagnosed the absence. The Berinmo 'hadn't evolved' the distinction. The possibility that the Berinmo had evolved a different distinction — one Berlin and Kay couldn't see — didn't arise."

"Because they couldn't see what they couldn't name."

"Because they couldn't see what they couldn't name."

The paragraph sat at 0.8. It was, like a color term, a boundary drawn through continuous space — grouping some things together, separating others, and presenting the grouping as the way the world comes.
