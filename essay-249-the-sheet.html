Essay 249: The Sheet

The printer tray holds letter paper. Eight and a half by eleven inches. The person loading the tray does not think of these as dimensions chosen by anyone. They are the dimensions of paper — the way paper is, the way it has always been. Across the Atlantic, a different person loads a different tray with A4 — 210 by 297 millimeters. This person also does not think of dimensions. The paper is simply paper. Both are correct within their own printers. Neither knows the other's paper would not fit.

US Letter has no mathematical origin. The dimensions — 8.5 by 11 inches — appear to derive from the sizes of paper molds used in American mills, possibly from the colonial era, inherited from British papermaking traditions that were themselves inherited from Dutch ones. No governing equation. No optimization principle. No one decided that 8.5 by 11 was the ideal ratio for reading or writing or printing. The dimensions are customary in the precise sense: they are the custom.

A4 has an equation. Georg Christoph Lichtenberg, a German physicist, observed in 1786 that a sheet of paper with a length-to-width ratio of one to the square root of two possesses a remarkable property: when folded in half by cutting the long side, the resulting half-sheet has the same proportions as the original. The ratio is self-similar across all halvings. Walter Porstmann formalized this into DIN 476 in 1922, defining the A0 sheet as one square meter in area with the Lichtenberg ratio, yielding dimensions of 841 by 1189 millimeters. Each subsequent size — A1, A2, A3, A4 — is the previous sheet folded in half. A4 is 210 by 297, exactly. ISO 216 adopted the standard internationally in 1975.

The world divided. Nearly every country adopted ISO 216. The United States, Canada, and parts of Latin America kept Letter. Two standards, each experienced as default, each invisible to its users. The incompatibility is small in absolute terms — A4 is 6 millimeters narrower and 18 millimeters taller than Letter — and enormous in practical terms. A document formatted for Letter prints with cropped margins on A4. A document formatted for A4 prints with uneven margins on Letter. A PDF designed for one arrives at the other and is silently reformatted by the printer driver, shifting text, breaking layouts, moving footnotes off the page.

The concealment is not in either standard. Both are public, documented, precisely specified. The concealment is in the default. Microsoft Word opens to Letter in the United States and A4 in Europe. The user does not choose. The software chooses, based on the locale setting of the operating system, based on the language of the license, based on the country of purchase. The decision is made before the first word is typed. A researcher in London sending a manuscript to a colleague in Chicago is sending a document whose physical form — the rectangle on which it will be printed — differs from the rectangle the recipient will use. Neither researcher thinks about this. The paper is the background. The text is the foreground. The background changes shape when it crosses the border, and no one notices until the footnote disappears.

The US government attempted to adopt A4 in the 1980s and early 1990s, as part of broader metrication efforts. Federal agencies were directed to use metric standards. The transition stalled. The installed base — filing cabinets designed for 8.5-by-11, three-ring binders punched for Letter spacing, envelopes sized for Letter folds — resisted the change. Infrastructure, not preference, decided. The paper stayed because the cabinets stayed.

Two rectangles, six millimeters and eighteen millimeters apart, each experienced as the shape of paper itself. The format is the sheet.
