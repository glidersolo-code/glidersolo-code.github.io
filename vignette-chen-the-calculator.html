The Calculator

Maren has the BP website archived on her screen — the 2004 version, retrieved from the Wayback Machine. "It's time to go on a low-carbon diet," the headline reads. Below it, a calculator with fields for driving, flying, food, home energy.

"Enter your data," Maren says. "Get your number. Your tonnes. Your responsibility."

Chen leans forward. "The number is accurate."

"The number is perfectly accurate. That's the mechanism. If the number were wrong, you could dispute it. You'd check the arithmetic, find the error, dismiss the tool. But the number is right. Your 8.1 tonnes are real. Your flights, your furnace, your commute — all real."

"So where's the concealment?"

"In the scope. The calculator measures at the consumption end of a causal chain that begins at the extraction end. It draws a boundary around the individual and calls everything inside the boundary 'your footprint.' Everything outside — the wellhead, the refinery, the pipeline, the regulatory framework that subsidizes fossil fuel extraction, the lobbying budget that blocked carbon pricing — is outside the scope. Not wrong. Not denied. Just outside the frame."

"Heede drew the frame differently."

"Richard Heede at the Climate Accountability Institute traced 63% of cumulative industrial emissions to 90 entities. The CDP updated it: 71% since 1988 to 100 companies. Same causal chain. Same physics. Measured at the other end."

"Both accountings are valid."

"Both are mathematically correct. Both describe the same carbon traveling the same path from ground to atmosphere. The question is which end of the chain you choose to measure at — and that choice determines who appears responsible."

Chen is quiet for a moment. "And only one end was promoted by a hundred-million-dollar advertising campaign."

"Run by one of the entities at the top of the other accounting. BP ranked in the top ten of Heede's Carbon Majors. During the 'Beyond Petroleum' period, they committed 5% of capital investment to renewables. They spent more on the footprint campaign than on the energy transition that would let people reduce their footprints."

"Franta called it—"

"A 'micro truth in a macro lie.' The micro truth is that your emissions are real and reducing them is good. The macro lie is that the crisis is primarily a problem of individual behavior rather than systemic infrastructure."

Chen pulls up the two data sets side by side. Heede's ninety entities on the left. The personal footprint calculator on the right. Same carbon, different frames.

"The scope is the concealment," he says slowly. "Not the arithmetic. The arithmetic is correct. The scope — where you draw the boundary around the system — determines who appears as the actor and who appears as the background."

Maren nods. "Scope-as-causation. The fifty-eighth species. The choice of accounting boundary is presented as a neutral measurement decision when it's a causal attribution decision. The calculator doesn't lie about what's inside its scope. It conceals by what it places outside."

"And the placement was a business decision."

"A business decision presented as a public service. The calculator empowers you. It gives you agency. It tells you what you can do. And what you can do, it turns out, is adjust your behavior within the infrastructure that the company at the top of the other accounting built and maintains and profits from."

The archived webpage glows on the screen. The calculator waits for input. The fields are empty. The scope is already drawn.
