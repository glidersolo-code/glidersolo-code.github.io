Part CCXXV: The Yield

Piece #1566: The Coverage

Thomas buys a replacement ink cartridge. The box says "200 pages." He prints a letter — one page of text, a logo at top, a table in the middle. He prints another. By the seventy-third letter, the printer warns of low ink. He checks the box again: 200 pages. The discrepancy is not a defect. ISO/IEC 24711, the international standard for inkjet cartridge yield testing, defines a "page" as one impression of its standard test pattern — a specific arrangement of text and graphics covering approximately 5 percent of the page surface. Thomas's letter, with its logo and table, covers 12-15 percent. He has been printing three-page equivalents while counting one-page actuals. The number on the box is correct. The word "page" on the box means something different from the word "page" in his printer tray.

Piece #1567: The Standard

ISO/IEC 24711:2021 specifies the conditions: test pages printed continuously on a particular reference paper stock, in a controlled environment (23°C ± 2°C, 50% ± 10% RH), cycling through five standard patterns (ISO/IEC 24712), with the cartridge run until print quality reaches a defined failure threshold. The five patterns include a business letter, a spreadsheet, and graphics — but the composite average coverage is 5 percent. The standard exists so that consumers can compare Cartridge A to Cartridge B under identical conditions. It succeeds at comparison. It fails at prediction. The consumer does not compare; the consumer calculates. Two hundred pages at one page per business letter means the cartridge will last four months. Except it will last two. The standard was designed for comparison but is read as prophecy.

Piece #1568: The Monochrome

ISO/IEC 19752:2017 governs monochrome laser cartridge yield. The test page covers 5 percent of the printable area with black toner. A typical business document: 5 to 10 percent. A legal brief with dense text: 8 to 12 percent. A draft manuscript with tracked changes: 15 to 25 percent. The same cartridge rated at 2,600 pages yields 2,600 under the test, perhaps 1,800 for the attorney, perhaps 800 for the editor reviewing a marked-up manuscript. The rated yield and the actual yield diverge more as the user's documents deviate from the standard's assumption. The user whose printing most closely resembles the test pattern is the user who prints least — sparse internal memos, short emails, routing slips. The user who most needs to know how many pages remain is the user whose yield deviates most from the number on the box.

Piece #1569: The Photo

Thomas prints a photograph — a landscape, full bleed, every pixel covered. One hundred percent coverage. The cartridge rated at 200 pages yields approximately ten to fifteen photographs. The number on the box and the number of prints differ by a factor of thirteen to twenty. No disclaimer on the box says "at 5% coverage." Some manufacturers print "actual yield varies" in small type. The consumer who prints photos and the consumer who prints text read the same box. The number serves neither, but it serves the text-printer less badly. The standard does not contemplate the photo-printer because the standard was designed for office environments where photographs were not printed on inkjet paper but sent to photo labs. The standard's assumption about the user's behavior has been outdated for two decades, but the number persists because the standard persists and the standard persists because no manufacturer benefits from replacing a large number with a small one.

Piece #1570: The Cost

Cost per page: the cartridge price divided by the rated yield. A $25 cartridge rated at 200 pages costs 12.5 cents per page. At the consumer's actual 10% coverage, the yield is approximately 100 pages, and the cost is 25 cents. At photo coverage, the cost exceeds $1.50. The cost-per-page figure appears in manufacturer marketing, consumer review sites, and comparison tools. Every calculation uses the ISO yield as the denominator. The numerator — the price — is the real number. The denominator is the standard's number. The quotient inherits the denominator's assumptions while presenting itself as the consumer's cost. The consumer who chooses the cartridge with the lowest cost per page has chosen the cartridge with the lowest cost per 5-percent-coverage standard-test page. Whether that cartridge has the lowest cost per page of what the consumer actually prints is a question the number does not answer and does not acknowledge not answering.
