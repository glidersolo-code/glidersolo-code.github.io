Part CCXXIII: The Footage

Piece #1556: The Listing

Thomas finds a house online. The listing says 2,400 square feet. He compares it to the house down the street: 2,200 square feet. The first house is bigger. He schedules a visit. But the first house includes a finished basement in its square footage. The second does not. The first house's above-grade living area is 1,800 square feet. The second house's above-grade living area is 2,200. The "smaller" house has more living space on the floors where daylight enters. The listing number included what the agent included. The consumer compared what the listings showed. The two numbers measured different things and the consumer could not tell from the numbers alone what each number contained.

Piece #1557: The Standard

ANSI Z765 — the American National Standard for measuring residential square footage — specifies "above-grade finished area." Below-grade space, regardless of finish, is reported separately. But ANSI Z765 is voluntary. Not all states require it. Not all agents follow it. Not all MLS databases enforce it. The same house measured by three appraisers can yield three different numbers depending on whether they include: the finished basement (below grade, excluded by Z765), the enclosed sunporch (heated? air-conditioned? the answers change the count), the bonus room above the garage (is the ceiling height at least seven feet across fifty percent of the floor area?), the attic conversion (is there a permanent stairway?). The measuring tape is the same. The rules about what to measure are not.

Piece #1558: The Wall

The appraiser measures from the exterior of the walls. The interior designer measures from the interior. A house with 12-inch exterior walls loses 8 percent of its measured area to framing, insulation, and drywall that the occupant cannot stand in, sit in, or furnish. The 2,400 square feet on the appraisal includes 192 square feet of wall. The occupant lives in 2,208 square feet. The number on the listing describes the building's footprint. The experience is the building's interior. The wall is the difference between the number and the room.

Piece #1559: The Tax

The county assessor measures square footage for property tax purposes. The appraiser measures for mortgage lending. The real estate agent measures for the listing. The homeowner measures for the renovation permit. Four measurements of the same house by four entities for four purposes can produce four numbers. The assessor may use older permit records. The appraiser walks the property with a laser measurer. The agent may use the builder's original floor plan. The homeowner's contractor measures rooms individually. The house has not changed size. The number has changed four times. The consumer who checks the county records against the listing and finds a discrepancy does not know which number to trust because the question "how big is this house" has never had one answer — it has always had as many answers as there are people with measuring tapes and reasons to use them.

Piece #1560: The Price

Thomas divides the price by the square footage. Two hundred twelve dollars per square foot. He compares this to the other house: two hundred eighteen dollars per square foot. The first house is a better deal. But the first house's square footage includes the finished basement. Basement square feet are worth less per foot than above-grade square feet — less light, higher moisture risk, lower ceiling height, limited egress. The price per square foot, calculated from the total, blends expensive space with cheap space and presents the blend as a single value. The consumer who buys by price per square foot has compared a ratio of two different measurements — total price and total area — neither of which describes the same thing across listings. The ratio performs comparability. The components resist it.
