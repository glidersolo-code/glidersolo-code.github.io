Essay 250: The Charge

The phone has a 5,000 milliamp-hour battery. The consumer reads capacity — how long the phone will last, how much power it holds, how far from the charger one can travel. Five thousand is a large number. Last year's phone had 4,500. The upgrade is eleven percent. The consumer expects eleven percent more battery life. The consumer may or may not get it, and the unit on the box cannot say.

Milliamp-hours measure charge, not energy. A milliamp-hour is the amount of electric charge transferred by a current of one milliamp flowing for one hour. It says how many electrons the battery can push, but not how hard it pushes them. That depends on voltage. A 5,000 mAh battery at 3.7 volts stores 18.5 watt-hours. A 5,000 mAh battery at 7.4 volts stores 37 watt-hours — twice the energy, same number on the label. The unit that would capture actual energy — the watt-hour — is printed rarely, in small type if at all, because it produces smaller numbers that are harder to compare across products with different voltages.

The marketing logic is clean. Milliamp-hours produce four-digit numbers. Watt-hours produce two-digit numbers. 5,000 is more impressive than 18.5. The consumer who compares 5,000 to 4,500 is comparing charges at the same nominal voltage and reaches a roughly valid conclusion — about eleven percent more. But the consumer who compares a phone's 5,000 mAh to a laptop's 5,000 mAh is comparing charges at different voltages and reaches no valid conclusion at all. The laptop battery, operating at 11.4 volts, stores 57 watt-hours — more than three times the energy. The mAh are equal. The batteries are not.

Power banks compound the problem. A power bank marketed as 20,000 mAh stores energy at its internal cell voltage — typically 3.7 volts, yielding 74 watt-hours. But it delivers power at 5 volts (USB) or higher. The voltage conversion loses energy — typically 15 to 30 percent, depending on circuitry and cable quality. The deliverable capacity at 5 volts is roughly 14,800 mAh, not 20,000. The number on the box describes the internal charge, not the usable charge. The consumer who expects to charge a 5,000 mAh phone four times from a 20,000 mAh power bank discovers it manages three and a half. The math is correct. The number was not what it appeared to be.

The aviation industry does not use milliamp-hours. The TSA limit for lithium-ion batteries in carry-on luggage is 100 watt-hours — the energy unit, not the charge unit — because energy, not charge, determines the severity of a thermal runaway. A 100 Wh limit corresponds to approximately 27,000 mAh at 3.7 volts. The regulator uses the unit that measures what matters. The marketer uses the unit that produces the larger number. Both are printed on the battery, if the consumer knows where to look.

A unit that measures charge presented as a unit that measures capacity, producing numbers three hundred times larger than the energy equivalent, persisting because the larger number is the one the market compares. The format is the charge.
