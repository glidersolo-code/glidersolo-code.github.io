Vignette Chen The Fragrance

Vignette

"Read me the ingredient list," Chen said.

Maren turned the bottle. "Water, sodium lauryl sulfate, cocamidopropyl betaine, glycerin, fragrance, citric acid."

"How many ingredients?"

"Six."

"Count again."

Maren counted. "Six. I can see them."

"Five of those words are ingredients. One of those words is a container."

"Fragrance?"

"The average fragrance formula contains fourteen compounds. Complex ones contain fifty or more. Solvents, fixatives, stabilizers, aromatic compounds, preservatives, UV absorbers. The International Fragrance Association lists over three thousand possible ingredients. Any combination of them can be folded into that one word."

"Why?"

"Trade secret protection. The Fair Packaging and Labeling Act requires ingredient listing. 21 CFR 720.8 exempts fragrance formulations from disclosure. The law creates both the obligation to list and the exemption from listing. Both operate on the same label."

"So I'm reading a label that shows me everything except the one thing that could contain allergens."

"Twenty-six known contact allergens — limonene, linalool, coumarin, cinnamaldehyde — can be inside the word 'fragrance' without appearing on the label. If you're allergic to linalool, you can read an American cosmetic label and find no linalool listed. It may be there. You can't tell."

"Europe is different?"

"EU Regulation 1223/2009. Twenty-six allergenic fragrance substances must be individually listed when present above threshold concentrations. The trade secret is partially maintained — the full formula stays proprietary. Only the allergens are disclosed. The consumer sees enough to protect themselves. The formula sees enough protection to remain secret."

"A compromise."

"A compromise. The American system doesn't compromise. It seals the container completely. One word. Same font as every other ingredient. Same position in the descending order. The format performs transparency. The word performs concealment."

Maren looked at the bottle. "So this is different from 'natural.'"

"Different mechanism. 'Natural' has no definition — vacancy-as-plenitude. 'Fragrance' has a definition: it's a legal authorization to not disclose. 'Natural' is unregulated. 'Fragrance' is deliberately shielded. The concealment isn't an accident. It's a feature. The government that requires ingredient disclosure also permits ingredient concealment, in the same legal framework, on the same surface, in the same font."

"Authorized concealment."

"The eighty-first species. Not a gap. Not a failure. Not a vacancy. A permission."
