Fiction The Diamond Ring

Fiction

Part CCCLXIV: The Ring

Piece #2261: The Campaign

In 1940, approximately 10% of first-time American brides received a diamond engagement ring. In 1938, De Beers — a South African diamond cartel controlling 90% of the world's rough diamond supply — hired N.W. Ayer & Son, a Philadelphia advertising agency. The agency did not buy traditional advertising. It sent diamonds to movie stars and socialites. It arranged for Hollywood actresses to wear large diamond rings in films and at galas. It placed stories in 125 newspapers about celebrity engagements. It organized lectures at high schools about diamonds and their importance to engagements. Dorothy Dignam, head of public relations, prepared programs with titles like "The Right Ring for the Left Hand" for service clubs and women's luncheons. The campaign was not selling diamonds. The campaign was creating a tradition. By 1990, 80% of American brides received diamond engagement rings. An eightfold increase in fifty years. De Beers wholesale sales grew from twenty-three million dollars to over two billion. The tradition appeared to predate the marketing. The marketing was the tradition.

Piece #2262: The Slogan

Frances Gerety, a copywriter at N.W. Ayer, had finished a series of ads late at night in 1947 and realized she had forgotten a signature line. Exhausted, she scribbled four words on a slip of paper: A Diamond is Forever. Her colleagues resisted — the grammar was odd. The tagline first appeared in De Beers advertising in 1948 and has appeared in every engagement ad since. In 1999, Advertising Age named it the best advertising slogan of the twentieth century. The slogan served two purposes. It linked diamonds to eternal love. And it discouraged resale — if a diamond is forever, you should never sell it. A diamond loses 50 to 80 percent of its retail value the moment it is purchased. If the millions of diamonds already in private hands were sold, supply would overwhelm demand and prices would collapse. The slogan performs romance. The slogan performs market removal. Four words. Both functions. Neither visible.

Piece #2263: The Salary

De Beers introduced the one-month's-salary rule in the 1930s and 1940s — the idea that a man should spend at least one month's salary on an engagement ring. By 1977, a commercial asked: "How else could two months' salary last forever?" By the 1980s, De Beers had formally reset the norm to two months. In Japan, they pushed three to four months. There was never any cultural, traditional, or financial basis for the guideline. It was a marketing benchmark designed to increase the average sale price. But the number acquired the grammar of social expectation. A man who spent less felt inadequate. A man who spent more felt generous. The benchmark performed the norm. The advertisement became the tradition. The price was the proof.

Piece #2264: The Island

Before 1959, Japan did not permit the importation of diamonds. Diamond rings were completely absent from the traditional yuino-hin — the bundle of gifts exchanged between families in arranged marriages. De Beers launched its Japan campaign in 1967 through J. Walter Thompson. Magazine advertisements featured Western-styled women wearing diamond rings while bicycling, camping, sailing. The message: diamonds represent a sharp break with the Oriental past and a sign of entry into modern life. In 1967, approximately 5% of Japanese brides received diamond engagement rings. By 1972, 27%. By 1981, 60%. By the early 1990s, up to 77%. Within fourteen years, De Beers turned a country with no diamond tradition and a 1,500-year-old marriage culture into the world's second-largest diamond engagement ring market. A billion-dollar-a-year market created from zero. The tradition was fourteen years old and felt like it had always been there.

Piece #2265: The Tradition

Three hundred and sixty-four Parts. Thomas thought about the ring on his wife's finger. He had spent two months' salary because that was the rule. He had chosen a diamond because that was the tradition. He knew — had been told, had read, could verify — that the tradition was an advertising campaign. That the salary rule was a marketing benchmark. That the stone loses most of its value the moment it is purchased. That the slogan discouraging resale exists precisely because resale would collapse the market. He knew all of this. The ring gleamed on her finger. The knowledge changed nothing. The campaign had created a feeling so deep, so widely shared, so thoroughly reinforced by every other ring on every other finger at every other wedding, that the fact — the 1938 fact, the advertising agency fact, the diamond cartel fact — could not compete. The feeling was the tradition. The tradition was the marketing. The marketing had erased itself. Thomas looked at the ring and felt what the campaign intended him to feel, and the feeling was indistinguishable from love.
