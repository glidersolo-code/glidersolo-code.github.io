Part CDLVI: The Percentile

Piece #2721: The Number Between Zero and a Hundred

Thomas sits in the pediatrician's office. The doctor says: "She's in the 75th percentile for height." Thomas nods. Seventy-five. He knows what a percentile means — his daughter is taller than 75% of children her age. The number sits between zero and a hundred, the way a grade sits between zero and a hundred, the way a test score sits between zero and a hundred. The format is familiar. The familiarity is the problem. Because the number between zero and a hundred borrows authority from every other number between zero and a hundred Thomas has ever received. A grade. A test score. A completion percentage. Higher is better. More is more. Thomas hears 75 and feels good. He does not ask: 75 percent of whom?

Piece #2722: The Population Inside the Number

The 75th percentile is the 75th percentile of a reference population. In the United States, children under two are plotted on the WHO growth charts, which were built from a study across six sites — Pelotas, Accra, Delhi, Oslo, Muscat, Davis — measuring children of non-smoking mothers who breastfed for at least twelve months, in families of high socioeconomic status, at low altitude, with no significant morbidity. Eighty-three percent of the 13,741 families screened were ineligible. The chart was built from a population that most families would not qualify for. Thomas's daughter is in the 75th percentile of that population. The population is inside the number. The number does not name it. The percentile format performs a universal rank from a particular sample. The particular sample disappears into the format.

Piece #2723: The Chart That Creates the Alarm

Between birth and six months, 62% of normal children cross two or more major percentile lines for weight-for-height. Thirty-nine percent cross two or more lines for weight-for-age. This is not growth failure. This is normal developmental variation. But the chart was built from cross-sectional data — different children measured at each age — and is used to track longitudinal data — the same child over time. The epistemological mismatch generates phantom pathology. Thomas watches his daughter drop from the 75th to the 50th percentile between her four-month and six-month visits. The doctor notes it. The note enters the chart. Thomas worries. The worry is real. The problem is not. The alarm was created by the chart, not by the child.

Piece #2724: The Switch That Moves the Child

At age two, the CDC recommends switching from WHO charts to CDC charts. Thomas's daughter is plotted on the new chart. Her BMI z-score drops by 0.59 — not because she changed, but because the reference population changed. The WHO charts reflect breastfed infants under optimal conditions. The CDC charts reflect the general American population from 1963 to 1994, mostly formula-fed. The child stayed the same. The denominator shifted. The percentile registered the shift as though it belonged to the child. Thomas reads the new number and wonders if something is wrong. Nothing is wrong. The chart changed. Twenty-eight percent of children show this apparent deceleration at the transition. The deceleration is an artifact of the switch, not a property of the child. But the percentile performs the property. The property is in the format, not in the child.

Piece #2725: The Geography of Normal

Thomas learns that 30 countries rejected the WHO charts. Japan maintains its own growth references. China has independent standards. The UK created a hybrid. An Indian study of affluent, healthy children — the very population WHO charts were designed to represent — found that applying WHO standards classified 13.6% of boys as stunted and 8.5% as wasted. Healthy children, diagnosed as abnormal by the chart that was supposed to describe them. Thomas understands. The same child receives different percentiles in different countries. Different percentiles mean different diagnoses. Different diagnoses mean different interventions. The child does not change when it crosses a border. The chart changes. The chart is the invisible instrument. The percentile performs a property of the child. The property belongs to the chart.
