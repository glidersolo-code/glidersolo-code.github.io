Part CCLXXIII: The Wipe

Piece #1806: The Claim

Kills 99.9% of germs. The claim is on the front of the bottle, in the largest type on the label. The asterisk after "germs" references the back panel, where the word "germs" is replaced by specific organisms: Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella enterica, Influenza A. The 99.9% was achieved against those organisms, under test conditions, at a specific contact time. The front of the bottle is a conclusion. The back of the bottle is the protocol that produced the conclusion. The consumer reads the conclusion. The protocol is for the applicator. The consumer and the applicator are the same person. That person reads the front.

Piece #1807: The Minutes

Contact time: 4 minutes. The surface must remain visibly wet with disinfectant for four minutes. Not damp. Not residual film. Visibly wet — enough liquid that light reflects off the surface. For four minutes. The consumer sprays the counter, waits perhaps three seconds, wipes with a paper towel, and moves to the next surface. Three seconds of a four-minute protocol. The consumer has completed 1.25 percent of the required contact time. The efficacy achieved in three seconds is not printed anywhere — not on the bottle, not in the EPA registration, not in any consumer-facing document. The 99.9% and the three seconds coexist on the same counter, but the 99.9% belongs to the four minutes and the three seconds belongs to the consumer.

Piece #1808: The Wet

The word "wet" is doing all the work. The EPA test protocol requires the surface to remain wet for the full contact time. On a warm kitchen counter, the spray begins to evaporate within 30 to 60 seconds. The consumer would need to reapply — spray again, keep spraying — to maintain the wet film for four minutes. The bottle does not say "spray repeatedly for four minutes." The bottle says "spray surface until thoroughly wet." The consumer sprays once, sees wetness, and wipes. The wetness was the beginning of the protocol. The consumer treated it as the entirety.

Piece #1809: The Kitchen

Thomas sprayed the counter after cutting raw chicken. He wiped it in five seconds. He set the cutting board for vegetables on the same counter. He had read the front of the bottle. The front said 99.9%. He had not read the back. The back said four minutes. The five seconds between the spray and the wipe was the gap between the claim and the practice. The bacteria in that gap were the ones the claim did not reach. He did not get sick. Most people who spray and wipe in five seconds do not get sick, because the chicken was fresh, the contamination was light, and the partial kill from five seconds was sufficient for the actual bacterial load. The four-minute protocol was designed for the worst case. The consumer lives in the average case. The average case forgives the shortcut. The forgiveness reinforces the shortcut.

Piece #1810: The Front

Two hundred and seventy-three Parts. The disinfectant bottle has two faces — front and back. The front says "Kills 99.9%." The back says "contact time: 4 minutes." The front is written for the purchaser — the person standing in the aisle, comparing bottles, choosing the biggest number. The back is written for the applicator — the person holding the bottle over the counter, following the protocol. The purchaser and the applicator are the same hand. But the front and the back are different documents with different purposes, different type sizes, different audiences. The consumer performs the purchase with the front and the application with neither. Thomas read 99.9 and sprayed and wiped. The number described a four-minute surface. The surface received five seconds. The gap between the number and the practice was three minutes and fifty-five seconds of wet counter that never existed.
