Part CDXLII: The Number

Piece #2651: The Question

Thomas receives a survey. One question. "On a scale of 0 to 10, how likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague?" Thomas considers. The flight was fine. The seat was narrow. The attendants were professional. The meal was acceptable. Thomas selects 7.

The 7 vanishes. Thomas's 7 is a "passive." The system discards it. Thomas's entire assessment of the experience — adequate, professional, narrow, acceptable — does not exist in the final number. The system asked Thomas a question, recorded Thomas's answer, and then deleted it.

Piece #2652: The Thresholds

Thomas examines the scoring. 9-10: Promoter. 7-8: Passive. 0-6: Detractor. Thomas notices that a customer who selects 0 — who would actively warn friends away — receives the same classification as a customer who selects 6 — who is mildly indifferent. The categories erase magnitude.

Thomas looks for the derivation. Where were these thresholds validated? What statistical procedure determined that 7 and 8 should be discarded while 6 and 9 should be counted? Thomas finds Reichheld's 2003 article. The thresholds were intuitions. The intuitions were formatted as a methodology. The methodology was trademarked.

The threshold is the argument. The precision of the number — NPS = 42 — performs the derivation the thresholds lack.

Piece #2653: The Replication

Thomas reads Keiningham et al., 2007. Twenty-one firms. Over 15,500 interviews. The question: does NPS predict revenue growth better than other customer metrics? The answer: no. The American Customer Satisfaction Index outperformed NPS. The "one number you need to grow" did not replicate.

Thomas considers what happened after the non-replication. NPS adoption continued to increase. By 2020, two-thirds of Fortune 1000 companies used it. The number survived the disconfirmation because the number was never adopted for its predictive power. The number was adopted for its format. One number. One slide. Up or down. The format fit the boardroom. The boardroom adopted the format. The adoption became the validation.

Piece #2654: The Culture

Thomas learns that in Japan, the average NPS is -52. He pictures a boardroom in Tokyo. "Our NPS is -52." The executives confer. In Japan, giving a 9 or 10 to any business is considered immodest. A 7 is high praise. The 7 is discarded by the system as "passive."

The threshold was calibrated to American generosity. Thomas considers: every multinational comparing its NPS across markets is comparing response cultures. The same customer satisfaction, expressed through Japanese politeness, produces a score that in American terms indicates catastrophic failure. The number performs universality. The number is American.

Piece #2655: The Five Hundredth

Thomas has been reading about numbers for a long time. Five hundred essays. Five hundred numbers that performed properties they did not possess. Thomas considers what the Net Promoter Score shares with all of them.

The number is simple. The simplicity is not a limitation. The simplicity is the value. The boardroom does not want to understand the customer relationship. The boardroom wants to see the customer relationship on one slide. The number provides the slide. The slide provides the feeling of understanding. The feeling is the product.

Two-thirds of Fortune 1000 companies. The adoption is the evidence. The evidence is the adoption. Thomas selects 7. The 7 vanishes. The number that remains is the number the system needed. Thomas's experience is not the number. The number is not Thomas's experience. The distance between them is the five hundredth thing Thomas has learned about numbers.
