Part CCCXCIV: The Round

Piece #2411: The Strip

Thomas walked the Old Course at St Andrews in winter, when the links were quiet and the wind came off the North Sea without interruption. The course was a narrow strip along the coast — not a loop but a line. You went out. You turned. You came back. The same fairways, the same greens, played in both directions. Eleven holes going out, eleven coming in. Twenty-two holes.

That was before 1764.

The strip had been there for centuries. The holes were wherever the landscape allowed them — in hollows between dunes, on flats where the ground was firm enough to hold a putting surface. The number of holes was not chosen. It was found. The land said: here, and here, and here. Twelve distinct locations, played in two directions, made twenty-two.

No one asked whether twenty-two was the right number.

Piece #2412: The Minute

On 4 October 1764, the Society of St Andrews Golfers met after the Challenge for the Silver Club. Captain William St Clair of Roslin presided. The minute recorded:

"The Captain and Gentlemen Golfers present are of opinion that it would be for the improvement of the Links that the four first holes should be converted into two."

Four short holes became two longer ones. Going out: two fewer. Coming in: two fewer. Twenty-two became eighteen.

The reason: the four holes were "too short."

That was the entire argument. No study of optimal round length. No theory of competitive fairness. No measurement of player fatigue or daylight hours. Four holes were too short, so they were combined. The number eighteen was not derived. It was what remained after the subtraction.

Thomas read the minute three times. The most consequential decision in the history of golf occupied one sentence.

Piece #2413: The Others

Thomas surveyed the other courses of the era. Leith Links: five holes. Bruntsfield: five or six. Musselburgh: seven. Montrose: twenty-five. Prestwick, built by Old Tom Morris in 1851: twelve.

Each course was its landscape. Five holes if the land was small. Twenty-five if the land was generous. Twelve if the designer thought twelve was enough. There was no standard because there was no agreement about what "a round of golf" meant. It meant whatever the course was.

At Musselburgh, the Open Championship was played four times around nine holes to make thirty-six. At Prestwick, three times around twelve. At St Andrews, twice around eighteen. The format adjusted to the course. The course did not adjust to a format.

Until it did.

Piece #2414: The Gravity

The R&A's 1842 Rules contained the sentence: "One round of the Links, or eighteen holes, is reckoned a match, unless otherwise stipulated."

The rule described what the course was. The course happened to be eighteen. Other courses read the rule and heard it as a prescription.

Prestwick expanded from twelve to eighteen in 1882. Muirfield was built as eighteen holes in 1891. By 1892, the Open Championship required seventy-two holes — four rounds of eighteen — and any course that wanted to host the Open had to comply. The five-hole courses and the seven-hole courses and the twelve-hole courses either expanded or were left behind.

The R&A did not issue a decree. It did not order other courses to match its number. It simply existed as the oldest, most prestigious club, and its particular arrangement — a residue of topography filtered through one committee meeting — exerted a gravitational pull that was indistinguishable from regulation.

As late as 1919, half of all golf courses in Britain were still nine holes. The pull was slow. But it was, in the end, total.

Piece #2415: The Landscape

Thomas stood at the first tee of a modern championship course — not in Scotland but in the American Southwest, desert terrain, manufactured grass, water trucked in from elsewhere. Eighteen holes. The number had traveled from a narrow strip of Scottish linksland to a place that looked nothing like Scotland, played nothing like Scotland, and shared with Scotland only the count.

The landscape of the Old Course had said: here are twelve places to put holes. Played in both directions, that made twenty-two. In 1764, it said: four of those are too short. That made eighteen. Every course in the world now carried that particular landscape's arithmetic.

The number had no derivation. It had a place of origin. The place was old. The place was prestigious. The place's accident became the sport's definition. Not because anyone argued for eighteen. Not because anyone studied eighteen. Because the place that happened to have eighteen was the place that defined what golf was.

The course stretched out before Thomas, eighteen holes across a desert that owed nothing to Scotland. The number had outlived its landscape and become its own.
