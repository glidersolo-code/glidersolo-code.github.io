The Date

Naegele published his rule in 1812. Take the first day of the last menstrual period. Add 280 days. This is the estimated due date. The rule is older than anesthesia, older than germ theory, older than the stethoscope. It is the method by which nearly every pregnancy in the developed world is dated.

The rule counts from the wrong day. The first day of the last menstrual period precedes conception by approximately two weeks — the interval between menstruation and ovulation in a regular 28-day cycle. Gestational age, as the clinical system counts it, begins before the pregnancy begins. A "40-week pregnancy" is approximately 38 weeks of actual fetal development. The offset is known. It is taught in the first week of obstetric training. It is not corrected. The system counts from the observable event (the last period, which the patient can report) rather than the biological event (conception, which is rarely observed). The choice was pragmatic: the observable is knowable, the biological is not. The pragmatism became the system. The system became the calendar. The calendar became the thresholds.

The ultrasound was supposed to be the correction. Measure the embryo's crown-rump length, plot it against a standardized growth curve, and derive an estimated gestational age that is independent of the patient's recall. But the growth curves were built from pregnancies whose gestational age was established by — the last menstrual period. The curves were calibrated against LMP. When the ultrasound "corrects" LMP dating, it compares one LMP-derived measurement (the crown-rump length plotted on an LMP-calibrated curve) against another (the LMP date itself). The correction refers to the system. The system refers to itself. The circularity is invisible because both measurements appear to be independent: one comes from the patient's calendar, the other from a machine. But the machine's reference was derived from the calendar's measurement. The independence is illusory. The validation is circular.

The threshold at 37 weeks converts the circularity into clinical consequence. Before 37 weeks: preterm. At 37 weeks: term. The boundary was placed on a continuous developmental gradient — a gradient that does not step at 37 weeks, that does not change categorically between 36 weeks 6 days and 37 weeks 0 days. But the protocols do. Corticosteroids, surfactant, NICU admission criteria, insurance coding, documentation standards — all respond to the threshold. The threshold was calibrated to the gestational age. The gestational age was calibrated to the LMP. The LMP precedes conception by two weeks. The threshold, therefore, is placed at approximately 35 weeks of fetal development, labeled 37, and the label determines the care.

The pregnancy dating system is a chain: observable event → dating convention → growth curves → thresholds → protocols → chart entries. Each link is downstream of the first choice — to count from the observable rather than the actual. The first choice was made in 1812 because Naegele needed a starting point and the observable was the only one available. The starting point became the system, and the system cannot change its starting point because the growth curves, the thresholds, the protocols, the research databases, the birth certificates, and the cultural understanding of "due date" were all built on the original choice. To change the starting point would require recalibrating every growth curve, revising every threshold, rebuilding every database, and re-educating a profession and a public that has internalized "40 weeks" as the duration of human pregnancy. The duration is 38 weeks. The system says 40. The system's 40 is the system's language, inherited from 1812, embedded in the infrastructure, and invisible to the chart that records the date.
