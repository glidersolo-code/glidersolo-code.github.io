The Conversion

Essay 156

Breath-alcohol testing instruments measure the concentration of ethanol in exhaled air and report it as a blood alcohol concentration. The conversion uses a fixed blood-to-breath ratio: 2100:1 in North America, 2000:1 in the UK, 2300:1 in Australia, 2400:1 in Austria. The instrument's firmware divides the measured breath ethanol by the statutory ratio and outputs a number in blood units — g/dL or mg/mL — to two or three decimal places. The officer reads the number. The court receives the number. The number wears blood units. The implicit claim is that the breath measurement is equivalent to a blood draw.

A. Wayne Jones at Linköping University, reviewing the literature in Forensic Science Review (2025) and in earlier work with Johnny Mack Cowan in Forensic Sciences Research (2020), documented that the blood-to-breath ratio is not a constant. In 100 subjects tested in the post-absorptive phase (alcohol fully distributed), the mean ratio was 2382 ± 119, range 2125–2765 — every subject above 2100. For this group, the 2100:1 assumption causes the instrument to underestimate blood alcohol, which benefits the subject. But during the absorptive phase — when alcohol is still entering the bloodstream from the gut, the typical condition at a traffic stop soon after drinking — the ratio drops. Analysis by Labianca and Simpson found a 95% range of 1259:1 to 2679:1 during absorption. A person with a true ratio of 1500:1 and a true BAC of 0.075 g/dL (below the US legal limit of 0.08) will produce a breath result that the instrument converts to approximately 0.105 g/dL — over the limit by 30%. The relative error ranges from −40% to +28% across 95% of the absorptive-phase population.

The ratio is not merely variable across individuals. It varies within an individual depending on the moment. Hyperventilation lowers the breath alcohol reading; hypoventilation raises it. Holding one's breath for 30 seconds before exhaling increases the reading by approximately 15%. Body temperature shifts the ratio — fever raises breath alcohol, hypothermia lowers it. The duration of the exhalation affects the reading because end-expiratory air has higher alcohol concentration than early-expiratory air. The coefficient of variation in vivo is 8–12%, depending on instrument type and protocol, compared to approximately 2% in vitro. The gap between 2% and 12% is the human body's physiology, which the instrument's fixed conversion ignores.

The concealment mechanism here is distinct from earlier entries. In #234 (soil pH), the method determines the value because different electrolytes sample different chemical equilibria. In #235 (salivary cortisol), the antibody's cross-reactivity becomes dominant because the matrix inverts. Here, the instrument measures one thing and reports another, with the conversion factor built into the output such that the user never sees the raw measurement. The proxy is presented as the direct measurement. The officer does not see a breath alcohol concentration and a conversion factor and a computed blood alcohol concentration. The officer sees only the final number. The conversion is invisible by design — not hidden by accident or neglect, but hidden because the instrument was engineered to output a blood-equivalent number. The firmware performs the conversion because the law requires a blood-equivalent result. The law requires a blood-equivalent result because the legal limits were defined in blood units. The blood units exist because the original impairment research used blood samples. The entire chain — from research to law to instrument to traffic stop — carries forward an assumption about a ratio that varies by nearly two-fold across the population.

The legislation-embeds-assumption mechanism makes this entry structurally unusual. In most instrument artifacts, the assumed constant is an internal setting — a blank choice, a particle-shape assumption, a cross-reactivity specification. A researcher who discovers the error can change the setting. Here, the assumed constant is written into criminal statute. The 2100:1 ratio is law. It can only be changed by legislation. The fact that different countries chose different ratios — 2000, 2100, 2300, 2400 — proves the number is a policy decision, not a physical constant. If it were physics, every jurisdiction would converge on the same value. They have not. The variation across jurisdictions is the legislative fingerprint on what presents itself as a measurement.

The breath test works. It has always worked. It measures breath alcohol with 2% precision. The problem is not the measurement. The problem is the conversion — the fixed ratio that translates a valid breath measurement into a blood-equivalent number that varies by up to 40% from the actual blood value depending on who is being tested and when. The number on the display is precise. The number on the display is not what it claims to be.
