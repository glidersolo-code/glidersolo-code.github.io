Part CCXVIII: The Refresh

Piece #1531: The Number

Thomas stands in the television aisle. The tag on the Samsung says Motion Rate 240. The tag on the LG says TruMotion 240. The tag on the Sony says MotionFlow XR 960. The Sony is four times smoother than the Samsung, Thomas thinks — 960 versus 240. But all three televisions contain the same panel technology: a native 120Hz display. One hundred and twenty times per second, the liquid crystal pixels physically change state. The Samsung doubles the native rate to get 240. The LG doubles to get 240. The Sony multiplies by eight to get 960. The multiplication is not a lie. It is a composite: the native refresh plus backlight scanning plus frame interpolation plus proprietary processing, combined into a single number printed on the price tag. The number performs hertz. It is not hertz. Thomas picks the Sony because 960 is larger than 240, the way a consumer picks a number, the way a number asks to be picked.

Piece #1532: The Frame

Most television content is 24 frames per second. Film was shot at 24 fps since 1927. Broadcast television is 30 fps. A 120Hz panel displaying a 24 fps movie refreshes each frame five times — five identical repetitions of the same image. The consumer who bought a 120Hz television to see smoother motion is seeing five copies of each frame. The smoothness is in the panel's capability, not in the image's content. For the panel's native refresh rate to matter, the content must match: 120 fps gaming, 60 fps streaming, content produced at rates the panel can distinguish. Most content is not. The 120Hz panel waits for images that arrive at one-fifth its speed, fills the gaps with repetition, and displays a capability the content cannot use.

Piece #1533: The Interpolation

Frame interpolation — motion estimation, motion compensation, MEMC — generates intermediate frames. The television's processor examines two consecutive frames, calculates where moving objects would be in between, and creates a synthetic frame. The motion looks smoother. It also looks wrong. Viewers call it the soap opera effect — the uncanny smoothness of video applied to content shot at film's 24 fps. Broadcast soap operas were shot on video at 30 or 60 fps. The association is structural: interpolation makes everything look like it was shot on video. Directors object. Tom Cruise recorded a public service announcement asking viewers to turn off motion smoothing. The television creates frames the camera never captured, inserts them between the frames the camera did capture, and the resulting smoothness — the very thing the marketing number promises — is the thing the filmmaker asks the viewer to disable.

Piece #1534: The Strobe

Backlight scanning turns the backlight off between frames. For a fraction of each refresh cycle, the screen goes dark — too briefly for the eye to perceive, but long enough to reduce the persistence blur that LCD pixels create when they transition between states. The technique reduces motion blur. It does not add information. No new frame appears during the dark interval. The image content is identical to a non-strobed display. But the strobe counts as a "refresh event" in some manufacturers' proprietary metrics. A native 120Hz panel with backlight scanning becomes a "240 Hz effective refresh rate" — one hundred and twenty image refreshes plus one hundred and twenty darkness intervals, reported as a single number. The darkness is half the count.

Piece #1535: The Specification

Thomas brings the television home. He connects his gaming console via HDMI 2.1 — the only input standard that supports 4K resolution at 120 frames per second. The game renders at 120 fps. The panel refreshes at 120 Hz. For the first time, the content matches the hardware. The 120Hz matters now, genuinely, measurably. But Thomas did not buy the television for gaming. He bought it for the Motion Rate 960 on the price tag, which described a composite of capabilities he will use separately, occasionally, and mostly for content that arrives at one-fifth the speed the panel can display. The native refresh rate — the one specification that actually describes the panel's physical capability — is not printed on the price tag. It is on the specification sheet, in the technical details, below the fold. The marketing number is printed large. The engineering number is printed small. The pattern repeats from the oil bottle to the television tag: the visible specification and the significant specification are different numbers in different fonts, and the consumer reads the visible one.
