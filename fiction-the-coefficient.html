Part CCLXXXVI: The Coefficient

Piece #1871: The Equation

Thomas writes the CKD-EPI 2009 equation on the board.

eGFR = 141 × min(Scr/κ, 1)^α × max(Scr/κ, 1)^−1.209 × 0.993^Age × [1.018 if female] × [1.159 if Black]

He circles the last term. The one that isn't like the others.

Serum creatinine: measured by an instrument. A molecule in blood, its concentration read by spectrophotometry. Age: counted in years from a birth certificate. Sex: recorded in a medical chart, a physiological variable affecting creatinine production through muscle mass and hormonal differences.

Race: a checkbox. Self-reported. One option: Black or not Black.

In the equation, all four variables occupy the same syntactic position. All are multiplied by coefficients. All contribute equally to the output. The equation does not mark which inputs were measured by instruments and which were reported by the patient. The mathematics makes them indistinguishable.

Piece #1872: The Cohort

Thomas reads the development paper. Levey et al., Annals of Internal Medicine, 1999. The MDRD equation.

The cohort: 1,628 patients with chronic kidney disease. 88% White. 12% Black — approximately 197 people. The researchers used stepwise regression to find which variables predicted measured GFR. Race was statistically significant. Black patients had a coefficient of 1.212.

Thomas reads the biological rationale. Three sentences in the methods section. Black persons have, on average, greater muscle mass. Creatinine is a byproduct of muscle metabolism. Therefore the same creatinine level implies more filtration.

He reads the citations supporting this claim. Cohn et al. (1977): approximately 40 adults. Harsha et al. (1978): approximately 240 children. Worrall et al. (1990): approximately 60 hospital workers. All used visual determination of race. The combined sample: roughly 340 people. From this, a coefficient was derived that would be applied to millions.

Piece #1873: The Multiplier

Thomas works through what the coefficient does.

A 55-year-old woman presents with serum creatinine of 1.2 mg/dL. If not Black: eGFR ≈ 50. Stage 3b CKD. Referral to nephrology indicated. If Black: eGFR ≈ 58. Stage 3a CKD. Monitoring. Come back in six months.

Same blood. Same creatinine. Same kidneys doing the same work. The coefficient says: this creatinine, in this body, means something different. It means health, not disease. It means wait, not act.

Thomas writes the clinical thresholds. eGFR < 20: eligible for transplant waitlist. eGFR < 30: referral to nephrology. eGFR < 45: medication adjustments. Every threshold is a gate. The coefficient lifts the Black patient's number above the gate. The gate stays closed.

Ahmed et al. found the median delay: 1.9 years to transplant eligibility. Not a rounding error. Not a marginal adjustment. Nearly two years of kidneys failing while the equation says they're fine.

Piece #1874: The Syntax

Thomas returns to the equation. The thing that interests him most is not the coefficient itself but its position in the formula.

In a regression equation, every predictor variable is syntactically equivalent. The mathematics treats each term the same way: multiply by coefficient, adjust the output. This is the power of regression — it handles diverse inputs through a single framework. This is also the concealment.

A creatinine level of 1.2 mg/dL is a measurement. It exists in the blood. An instrument detected it. The number corresponds to a physical quantity in the world.

"Black" is not a measurement. It does not exist in the blood. No instrument detects it. It is a social category — one that means different things in different centuries, different countries, different contexts. Two people who both check "Black" on a medical form may share almost no genetic ancestry. A Black patient from rural Ghana and a Black patient from Brooklyn share the checkbox but not the body composition, diet, environmental exposure, or creatinine metabolism the coefficient claims to describe.

But in the equation, "Black" and "1.2 mg/dL" look the same. They are both inputs. They both have coefficients. The output — a single number, reported to one decimal place — contains no trace of the distinction. The number inherits the authority of the creatinine measurement without revealing that one of its inputs was a checkbox.

Piece #1875: The Costume

Thomas draws two columns. On the left: what the coefficient claims to represent (biology — muscle mass, creatinine metabolism, physiological difference). On the right: what the coefficient actually encodes (a statistical association in a specific cohort, confounded by diet, poverty, stress, environmental exposure, and every social determinant of health that correlates with race in the United States).

The regression cannot distinguish between these. It finds the association and reports it as a coefficient. The coefficient enters the equation as biology. Clinicians use it as biology. Computers apply it as biology. The patient whose transplant is delayed by 1.9 years experiences it as biology.

Thomas thinks about what it means for a social category to become a biological parameter. The transformation happens at one specific moment: when the regression assigns a coefficient. Before that moment, race is a demographic variable — contested, contextual, socially constructed. After that moment, race is 1.159 — a number indistinguishable from the numbers beside it. The regression is the mechanism. The equation is the costume. The output is the naturalization, performed automatically, at every bedside, for every patient, until someone looks at the equation and asks: which of these inputs was measured, and which was imposed?

Naomi Nkinsi, a medical student at the University of Washington, asked in 2018. She looked for the pathway connecting melanin to glomerular filtration. She found none. The coefficient had been in the equation for nineteen years.
