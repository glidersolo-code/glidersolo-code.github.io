Part CDII: The Review

Piece #2451: The Phrase

Thomas typed the phrase into a search engine: "peer-reviewed." He wanted to know what it meant — not the definition, but the history. When had this practice begun? Who invented it? What did it actually do?

The standard account said 1665. The Royal Society's Philosophical Transactions, the first scientific journal. Peer review from the beginning. Science had always validated itself this way.

Thomas checked the historians. Noah Moxham and Aileen Fyfe, writing in The Historical Journal in 2018: "This ahistorical treatment misunderstands early modern editorial practice." Henry Oldenburg, the journal's first editor, selected material from his correspondence network. The Council occasionally reviewed his choices. There were no external referees. There was no system.

The phrase implied permanence. The history revealed contingency.

Piece #2452: The Date

Thomas looked for when journals started sending manuscripts to outside experts. He expected a single date.

He found many. The Royal Society introduced systematic refereeing in 1831-1832. William Whewell proposed it. Fellows of the Society evaluated submissions to their own proceedings. This was internal refereeing within a gentleman's club, not anonymous external review.

The modern system — mandatory external anonymous review by two or three referees — was post-World War II. Before the war, most journals relied on editorial judgment. After the war, the explosion of scientists and papers made editorial selection impossible. Journals started farming out the reading.

Nature: external peer review not required until 1973. The Lancet: 1976. Most journals worldwide: the 1960s through the 1980s.

Thomas wrote down the gap. Three hundred and eight years separated the founding of Philosophical Transactions (1665) from Nature's requirement for external peer review (1973). The phrase "peer-reviewed journal" collapsed that gap to zero.

Piece #2453: The Word

Thomas found the Oxford English Dictionary entry. First recorded use of "peer review" as a noun: 1967. The context was not journal publishing. It was physicians evaluating each other under Medicaid's Professional Standards Review Organization — a medical self-regulation process.

By the 1970s, the term migrated to journal publishing and grant review. It replaced the older word: "refereeing."

Thomas considered the two words. "Refereeing" named what happened — one person judging a submission. A referee. An individual. "Peer review" named something else — the community validating the work. Peers. Review. The plural and the process performed a collective judgment that the practice — one or two individuals, usually disagreeing — did not deliver.

The word was the concealment. Not the practice. The word.

Piece #2454: The Numbers

Thomas found the meta-analysis. Bornmann, Mutz, and Daniel, PLOS One, 2010. Forty-eight studies. 19,443 manuscripts. Mean inter-rater reliability: ICC of 0.34. Mean Cohen's Kappa: 0.17.

Thomas looked up the scale. 0.01-0.20: "none to slight agreement." Two reviewers looking at the same paper agreed at barely above chance.

He found Peters and Ceci, 1982. Twelve papers already published in prestigious psychology journals. Resubmitted to the same journals, 18 to 32 months later, with fictitious author names and unknown institutional affiliations. Of 38 editors and reviewers, three detected the resubmissions. Of nine papers that went through full re-review, eight were rejected — for "serious methodological flaws."

He found Schroter and colleagues, 2008. Nine major errors deliberately inserted into papers sent to 607 BMJ reviewers. Average number of major errors detected: 2.58 out of nine.

Thomas set the numbers beside the phrase. "Peer-reviewed" performed scientific validation. The validation detected fewer than three of nine planted errors. The reviewers agreed at barely above chance. The same papers, at the same journals, were accepted and then rejected depending on whose name was on them.

Piece #2455: The Credential

Thomas checked what had been published without peer review.

Watson and Crick, 1953: the structure of DNA. Nature did not require external review. Einstein, 1905: special relativity, the photoelectric effect, Brownian motion, mass-energy equivalence. Published under Max Planck's editorial discretion. Hans Krebs, 1937: the citric acid cycle. Rejected by Nature because the journal had enough correspondence "for seven or eight weeks." Published elsewhere. Nobel Prize, 1953.

Drummond Rennie, deputy editor of JAMA, who organized the first International Congress on Peer Review in 1989, said: "If peer review was a drug it would never be allowed onto the market."

Thomas returned to the phrase. "Peer-reviewed." Two words. Thirteen letters. The first word performed a community. The second performed a process. Together they performed an institution — collective scientific validation — that the practice had never fully instantiated. The vocabulary created the institution it described. The institution did not create the vocabulary.

And every time the phrase appeared — on a journal, on a CV, in a grant application — it renewed the performance. The credential was the concealment. The concealment was the credential.
