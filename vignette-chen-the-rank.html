The Rank

Chen is looking at a list of universities when Maren sits down.

"What's the ranking?"

"This school is number three. This one is number fifteen."

"So the first one is better."

"The first one scored higher on a formula that weights peer reputation at twenty percent, graduation rate at seventeen point six percent, financial resources at ten percent, and selectivity at seven percent."

"Those are specific numbers."

"They are. They're also editorial opinions. The National Opinion Research Center reviewed them in 1997 and found they 'lack any defensible empirical or theoretical basis.' The man who chose them said they 'reflect our judgment about how much a measure matters.'"

"So the ranking is a judgment."

"The ranking is a judgment formatted as a measurement. Change the weights, same schools, different ranking. Twenty percent on peer reputation makes Harvard number three. Ten percent on peer reputation might make it number eleven. Harvard didn't change. The formula changed."

"But people don't see the weights."

"They see the number. Three. Fifteen. The number feels like a property of the school — like its address, or its founding year. But it's a property of the formula. The school is the input. The number is the output. The weights are the machine."

Maren picks up the magazine. "What happens when the schools know the formula?"

"They optimize. A university encourages more applications — not to admit more students, but to reject more. Each rejection improves selectivity. Selectivity improves the ranking. The school didn't become more selective. It produced more rejections."

"And the ranking rewards the rejections."

"Indistinguishably from genuine improvement. That's the mechanism. The ranking defines quality. Schools produce the definition. The ranking measures the production and calls it quality. The circle closes."

"What breaks it?"

"Reed College refused to participate in 1995. The ranking penalized them — lowest possible scores on every unreported metric. Ranked ninety-something when students later estimated it should have been thirty-eight."

"The punishment for not playing the game was losing the game."

"Because the format is the enforcement. The ranking can punish non-participation because the ordinal list is the only thing the consumer sees. Reed's refusal looks like Reed's failure."

Chen closes the magazine. "So the measurement governs. And the governance is disguised as measurement."

"That's species sixty-seven."

The thirty-ninth line of the paragraph holds. The weights are published. The methodology is transparent. The formula is available. And none of that matters, because the consumer reads the output — the ordinal rank, the single number, the position on the list — and the output conceals the input. The editorial judgment that determined the ranking is invisible behind the ranking itself. Transparency performs objectivity. The disguise is not secrecy. The disguise is format.
