Essay 191: The Air

Six pollutants, one number.

The US Air Quality Index converts concentrations of six substances — fine particulate matter (PM2.5), coarse particulate matter (PM10), ground-level ozone (O₃), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), and carbon monoxide (CO) — into a single integer between 0 and 500. The conversion uses breakpoint tables established by the Environmental Protection Agency under the Clean Air Act. For each pollutant, a concentration range maps to an index range. The overall AQI is the maximum of the six individual sub-indices. The worst pollutant determines the number. The number determines the color. The color determines the recommendation.

The maximum operation selects one pollutant and discards the rest. A city with AQI 150 from PM2.5 smoke and a city with AQI 150 from ozone have the same number, the same color (red: unhealthy), the same headline. The health effects are different. PM2.5 penetrates deep into lung tissue, crosses into the bloodstream, causes cardiovascular damage over hours and decades. Ozone irritates airways, reduces lung function, peaks on hot afternoons. The protective actions are different: for PM2.5, seal windows and run filtration; for ozone, avoid outdoor exertion during peak hours. The number communicates urgency. It does not communicate specificity. The specificity is destroyed by the aggregation that produces the number.

The color simplifies further. Six bins: green (0-50), yellow (51-100), orange (101-150), red (151-200), purple (201-300), maroon (301-500). AQI 51 and AQI 100 are both yellow. The difference — nearly double the index value — is invisible within the category. The boundary between orange and red is arbitrary in the same way that p = 0.05 is arbitrary: the air at AQI 150 is not categorically different from the air at AQI 151. The threshold is a design choice about communication, presented as a fact about safety.

The breakpoints are evidence-informed and not evidence-determined. The PM2.5 breakpoint for "good" was 12.0 µg/m³ until 2024, when the EPA tightened the annual standard to 9.0 µg/m³. The revision acknowledged that the previous "good" had included air that was harmful. The people who had breathed that air had done so under a number that told them they were fine. The number was wrong — not because the arithmetic was wrong but because the standard was insufficiently protective. The revision was correct. The corrected standard will eventually be revised again, and the current "good" will be revealed as insufficiently protective, and the cycle will continue because the relationship between concentration and harm is continuous and any threshold imposed on it is a simplification.

The dimensional reduction — from six pollutants with different chemistries, sources, health effects, and spatial distributions to one integer — is the concealment's architecture. The index was designed for communication: a government needed to tell millions of people something useful about the air they were breathing, in a format compatible with weather reports and phone screens. The single number is useful. The single number is also wrong, in the specific sense that it destroys information that some people need: which pollutant is the problem, what protective action to take, whether the air is bad in the way that affects them specifically. The usefulness and the wrongness coexist because the communication that a single number enables is the same communication that a single number prevents.

The air does not have an index. The air has particles — varying by size, composition, source, hour, and block. A rooftop sensor measures a different air than a child breathes at ground level. The concentration is a spatial average, a temporal average, a mass average. The index is a number about the average. The average is a number about the air. The air is not a number. The distance from air to index is three abstractions, each necessary, each lossy, each concealing the complexity that the next abstraction compresses further.
