The Tonnage

A "three-ton" air conditioner does not weigh three tons. A ton of cooling is 12,000 BTU per hour — the heat required to melt a short ton of ice at thirty-two degrees Fahrenheit in twenty-four hours. The unit descends from the ice-harvesting era, when commercial cooling meant blocks of ice cut from frozen lakes and delivered to buildings. When mechanical refrigeration replaced harvested ice, the industry kept the metaphor. The three-ton system removes 36,000 BTU of heat per hour from indoor air. No ice is melted. No ton is lifted. The consumer hears tonnage and calibrates against a weight that refers to a nineteenth-century delivery.

The BTU itself — the British Thermal Unit, the heat needed to raise one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit — is a unit of heat used to rate a machine that cools. The measurement is technically precise: cooling is heat removal, and the BTU quantifies what is removed. But the consumer thinks in terms of cold air produced, not heat subtracted. The rating measures one direction. The experience is the other. The specification and the sensation point at opposite ends of the same thermodynamic process.

The rated capacity — 36,000 BTU/hr — describes performance at standard test conditions: 95 degrees outdoors, 80 degrees indoors, 50 percent relative humidity. When outdoor temperature exceeds the test condition, capacity drops. At 105 degrees, the condenser coil — which must reject indoor heat to outdoor air — becomes less efficient as the temperature differential narrows. The three-ton system may deliver two and a half tons on the day the homeowner most needs three. The rating describes a tested midpoint. The consumer requires performance at the extreme. The gap between the two widens as the temperature rises.

SEER — Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio — compounds the averaging. SEER is total cooling output divided by total electricity consumed over a season spanning 65 to 104 degrees. SEER 16 means 16 BTU per watt-hour, seasonally averaged. The same system may have an EER — the single-point efficiency at 95 degrees — of 12. The consumer who reads SEER 16 and expects that efficiency on the hottest afternoon will experience 12. The seasonal average smooths the peak into the mean, and the peak is when the electric bill and the power grid need the efficiency number most.

The sizing creates a final inversion. Manual J — ACCA's standard load calculation — determines the actual cooling need of the specific house: insulation, window area, ductwork, occupancy, orientation. The calculation might say two and a half tons. The contractor recommends three because bigger feels safer, because no homeowner complains about too much cooling, because oversizing protects against callbacks. But the oversized system short-cycles — reaches the thermostat setpoint quickly, shuts off before completing a dehumidification cycle. The air cools but remains humid. The homeowner lowers the thermostat to compensate. The system that was too large now runs continuously. The additional capacity that was added for safety makes the system worse at the thing the homeowner wanted — comfort, which is temperature and humidity together, not temperature alone.
