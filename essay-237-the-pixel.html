The Pixel

The first commercially available digital camera for consumers, the Apple QuickTake 100 released in 1994, captured images at 640 × 480 pixels — 0.3 megapixels. By 2024, smartphone cameras advertise 48, 108, or 200 megapixels. The number increased by nearly three orders of magnitude in thirty years. "More megapixels" became the consumer shorthand for "better camera." The number counts sensor elements. The consumer reads image quality.

A megapixel is a million photosensitive elements on a sensor. The count describes quantity. It does not describe the size of each element, the amount of light each captures, the noise each generates, the lens quality, or the processing algorithms. These factors determine image quality. The pixel count determines one dimension of potential resolution. The marketing reports the count. The image depends on the system.

The sensor is a physical object with a physical area. A full-frame camera sensor measures 36 × 24 mm — 864 mm². A smartphone sensor might measure 6.4 × 4.8 mm — approximately 31 mm². The smartphone sensor is roughly 28 times smaller. If both have 12 megapixels, each full-frame pixel is 28 times larger — collecting 28 times more photons per exposure. More photons means better signal-to-noise, more dynamic range, more accurate color in low light. A 48-megapixel smartphone with 0.7-micrometer pixels and a 12-megapixel full-frame camera with 8.4-micrometer pixels: the smartphone has four times the count. The full-frame camera has 144 times the pixel area. In nearly all conditions, the full-frame sensor produces dramatically better images. The number that is higher is not the camera that is better.

Modern smartphones resolve this contradiction through pixel binning — combining multiple small pixels into one larger virtual pixel. A 48-megapixel sensor with 2×2 binning produces a 12-megapixel image where each output pixel is the sum of four sensor pixels. Samsung's 200-megapixel sensor uses 4×4 binning, outputting 12.5-megapixel photographs by default. The spec sheet says 200. The default photo is 12.5. The full 200-megapixel mode — one sensor pixel per image pixel — produces noisier, less dynamic images than the binned mode. The full-resolution mode exists to justify the number on the box. The camera works best at one-sixteenth of its advertised count.

At common lens apertures, diffraction limits the finest detail a lens can project. At f/11 on a full-frame sensor, the Airy disk diameter is approximately 15 micrometers. On a high-resolution sensor, the pixels are smaller than the Airy disk — the sensor records diffraction blur, not scene detail. Beyond a certain megapixel count, additional pixels record the lens's limitations rather than the world's features. Lens aberrations further reduce effective resolution. A 50-megapixel sensor with a mediocre lens resolves no more detail than a 20-megapixel sensor with a superior lens. The pixel count describes the sensor's theoretical maximum. The lens determines the realized detail.

Most photographs are viewed on screens — a smartphone screen at approximately 2-3 megapixels, a 4K monitor at 8.3 megapixels. A 48-megapixel photograph viewed on a smartphone displays at 4-6% of its captured resolution. The difference between 12 and 48 megapixels is invisible on the screen where both are viewed. For printing at 300 dpi: a 4×6 print requires 2.2 megapixels; an 8×10 requires 7.2; a 20×30 poster requires 54. Most consumer photographs are never printed. Most are viewed on screens. Most of the pixels captured by most cameras are never seen by any viewer. A number that counts sensor elements, marketed as image quality, produced by sensors whose physics requires binning the count down by factors of four to sixteen, captured through lenses that may not resolve the detail the pixels could record, viewed on screens that display a fraction of the capture — experienced by the consumer as a property of the camera, displayed on a spec sheet as the most prominent number, the count a property of the sensor dressed as a promise of the image.
