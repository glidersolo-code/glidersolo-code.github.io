The Quarter

The US quarter has no decimal derivation. It is a fossil of the Spanish piece of eight — an octal coin, 8 reales to the peso — that dominated the Americas for three centuries. Colonial merchants cut the coin into halves, quarters, and eighths. Two bits equaled a quarter. When the United States adopted a decimal currency in 1792, it kept the quarter denomination because the Spanish 2-reales coin was already everywhere.

Jefferson preferred a 20-cent denomination. He called it the "double tenth." Hamilton's original mint report proposed only the dollar and the dime. The quarter was added during the legislative process because Congress could not ignore the coin that merchants already used. The first US quarters were not struck until 1796, and only 6,146 were minted — the Spanish coin was so abundant there was little demand for the American copy.

Every other decimal currency in the world uses 10, 20, and 50 as its subdivisions. The euro: 10c, 20c, 50c. The pound: 10p, 20p, 50p. The Australian dollar: 10c, 20c, 50c. The 25-cent denomination is a New World anomaly, traceable to the Spanish colonial peso. The number 25 looks like it belongs in a decimal system — after all, 25 divides evenly into 100. But the reason for choosing 25 over 20 was not arithmetic. It was inheritance.

In 1875, the United States tried the decimal correction. Senator Jones introduced a twenty-cent piece. Over a million were minted. The coin failed in two years — it was too similar in size to the quarter, and merchants couldn't tell them apart. Congress abolished it in 1878. The "correct" denomination could not displace the inherited one.

The quarter then embedded itself in infrastructure. Parking meters, starting in 1935. Pay phones, standardized at 25 cents in the 1980s. Vending machines, after Coca-Cola's 73-year nickel price finally ended in 1959. Arcade games, where "quarter-play" was the universal standard. Laundromats, car washes, toll baskets. The quarter became the base unit of coin-operated America — not because 25 is a better number than 20, but because three centuries of Spanish colonial commerce had installed the denomination before the decimal system arrived to claim it.

The deepest fossil was on Wall Street. The New York Stock Exchange, founded the same year as the Coinage Act, quoted prices in eighths of a dollar — one real of the piece of eight. Stocks traded in increments of 12.5 cents from 1792 until June 1997, when the NYSE shifted to sixteenths, and finally to decimal pricing on January 29, 2001. The Spanish octal system governed American financial markets for 209 years. The piece of eight outlasted the Spanish empire by two centuries.

The quarter persists because the predecessor denominates the successor. The decimal system replaced the octal system. The denominations didn't change. The new arithmetic absorbed the old structure and wore it as its own. Twenty-five cents looks decimal. It is colonial.
