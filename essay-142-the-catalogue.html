The Catalogue

Essay 142

The mass spectrometer works perfectly. It ionizes every peptide in the sample, fragments every ion, measures every mass-to-charge ratio, records every spectrum. The detector misses nothing. The problem is downstream.

After detection, each spectrum is compared against a reference database — UniProt, GENCODE, RefSeq — that contains every predicted human protein. The search algorithm finds matches and reports them. What it cannot find, it discards. Spectra matching no database entry are classified as unidentified, removed from the results, treated as noise. The pipeline does not report "found an unknown protein." The pipeline reports nothing. The discard is automatic, routine, indistinguishable from cleanup.

Tristan Cardon, Isabelle Fournier, and Michel Salzet at the University of Lille examined what the pipeline was throwing away. They found ghost proteins — 10 to 15 percent of real, functional, biologically active proteins that mass spectrometry experiments had been detecting and discarding for years. Not artifacts. Not contaminants. Real proteins, translated from regions of the genome that the databases had excluded. The databases were built on a convention adopted in the 1990s: open reading frames shorter than 300 nucleotides were flagged as likely noncoding and left out. The threshold was practical. The threshold was inherited. The threshold became invisible. By 2025, every mass spectrometry experiment in the world was searching against a catalogue that was missing an entire layer of the proteome — microproteins from alternative reading frames, from pseudogenes, from long noncoding RNAs that turned out to code, from sequences initiated by start codons the rules said were not start codons.

The ghosts were not hiding. They were in the data. Xusheng Cao at Rutgers screened over 50,000 publicly available mass spectrometry runs and found 170,529 novel peptides from noncanonical open reading frames. One hundred seventy thousand proteins, detected and discarded and detected and discarded, across thousands of experiments, across decades of accumulated data. The cycle of detection and erasure was not a failure of attention. It was a feature of architecture. The pipeline was built to discard unmatched spectra. The databases were built without the ghost proteins. The combination guaranteed that no experiment, no matter how sensitive the instrument, no matter how careful the operator, could find what the catalogue did not contain.

This is not a detector problem. The detector is already sufficient. This is a catalogue problem — and catalogue problems have a specific shape. The detector sees more than the catalogue can name. The pipeline, which depends on the catalogue, converts the detector's abundance into the catalogue's scarcity. Detected spectra that match nothing become, in the results, nothing that was detected. The transformation is lossless in one direction and total in the other: every known protein is faithfully reported; every unknown protein is faithfully erased.

The fix is not a better detector. The fix is a different catalogue — one that does not inherit the 300-nucleotide threshold, one that allows the search space to include what the genome's annotation conventions excluded, one that treats an unmatched spectrum as a finding rather than as noise. The Rutgers screen proved this: when the search space was expanded to include noncanonical open reading frames, 170,529 previously invisible proteins appeared. They appeared not because the instrument improved but because the catalogue grew. The ghosts were always in the discard pile. They needed, not a more sensitive detector, but a more complete dictionary.

The shape of the problem is worth staying with. The instrument sees everything. The catalogue contains almost everything. The pipeline connects them. And the gap between "everything" and "almost everything" — the 10 to 15 percent excluded by a heuristic adopted before the first large-scale proteomic experiment was run — is converted, by the pipeline's architecture, from a limitation of the catalogue into an apparent limitation of biology. The analyst sees the results: 85 percent of spectra identified, 15 percent unidentified. The 15 percent looks like noise. It looks like contamination. It looks like the inevitable bottom of what can be resolved. It does not look like real proteins, functional and essential, that the catalogue has defined out of existence. The convention, adopted for practical reasons, inherited without revision, and embedded in the infrastructure of every proteomic search, became a wall. The wall is not between the instrument and the biology. The wall is between detection and identification — and the wall is made of catalogue entries that were never written.
