Essay 185: The Score

The algorithm is secret.

Not classified — secret in the commercial sense: proprietary, protected as intellectual property, owned by the Fair Isaac Corporation, which introduced the FICO score in 1989 and has never published the formula. The inputs are known: payment history, utilization ratios, account age, credit mix, inquiries. The output is known: a number between 300 and 850. The function connecting inputs to output is proprietary. The score cannot be independently reproduced, verified, or audited by anyone outside the company.

The secrecy is treated as a feature. Intellectual property protection. Competitive advantage. The framing positions the secrecy as a business decision. The consequence is epistemological: the score is unverifiable. No external researcher can determine whether the algorithm produces valid results for any particular individual. The aggregate correlation — higher scores correlate with lower default rates — is documented. The individual validity — whether this score accurately predicts this person's future repayment — is unknowable because the function is inaccessible.

This is opacity-as-authority: the proprietary algorithm's inaccessibility is treated as evidence of sophistication rather than as a barrier to validation. The score is authoritative because it is opaque. The opacity prevents challenge. The prevention of challenge is the authority.

The feedback loop compounds the opacity. A low credit score produces higher interest rates. Higher interest rates increase borrowing costs. Higher costs increase the probability of missed payments. Missed payments lower the score. The prediction creates the conditions for its own fulfillment. The circularity makes the score functionally unfalsifiable — not because the claim cannot be tested (as with fingerprint uniqueness) but because the test's outcome is influenced by the claim.

Twenty-six million Americans are credit invisible. They have no credit file — no record at any major credit bureau. The absence is not treated as absence. The absence is treated as risk. The system processes scores. A person without a score cannot be processed. The inability to process is converted into an inference: not "we lack information" but "this person is a risk." The inference determines access — to housing, to transportation, to employment. The absence of data is the presence of exclusion.

The credit file is not the person. The file is a record of the person's interactions with the credit system. A person who pays cash has no file. A person whose landlord does not report to bureaus has no file. A person whose file contains errors — the Federal Trade Commission estimated one in four reports contain errors — has a file that does not correspond to the person it claims to represent. The score is calculated from the file. The file may not be the person. The score is therefore a measurement of the file, presented as a measurement of the person.

The score enters the infrastructure. Mortgage applications, rental screenings, insurance premiums, employment decisions — all reference the score. The score, calculated by a secret algorithm from a file that may contain errors about a person who may never have interacted with the credit system, determines access to the fundamental structures of economic life. The score is precise: 742. The precision is arithmetic — the output of a calculation. The calculation is proprietary. The data may be wrong. The person is elsewhere.

The score is a number. The person is not a number. The distance between them is the width of a proprietary algorithm that cannot be examined, processing a file that cannot be verified, producing a prediction that creates the conditions for its own confirmation, applied to a person who exists outside the system that scores them.
