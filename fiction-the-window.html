Part CCXLI: The Window

Piece #1646: The Four Numbers

The NFRC label on a window displays four values. U-Factor: the rate of non-solar heat transfer, in BTU per hour per square foot per degree Fahrenheit — lower means the window resists heat flow better. Solar Heat Gain Coefficient: the fraction of solar energy that passes through the glass, from zero to one — lower means the window blocks more sun. Visible Transmittance: the fraction of visible light that passes through, from zero to one — higher means more daylight. Air Leakage: cubic feet per minute per square foot of frame area. Four numbers, four dimensions, four aspects of a single opening in a wall. The consumer stands in a showroom and reads U-Factor, because U-Factor appears first, because the salesperson says "lower is better," because one number is simpler than four. The other three numbers wait on the sticker. The sticker is removed after installation.

Piece #1647: The Zone

ENERGY STAR defines four climate zones for windows — Northern, North-Central, South-Central, Southern — each with different qualifying criteria. In the Northern zone, qualifying windows must have U-Factor no higher than 0.27 and SHGC no lower than 0.32. The first requirement limits heat loss. The second requires solar gain — the window must let winter sun in to passively heat the house. In the Southern zone: U-Factor no higher than 0.40 and SHGC no higher than 0.25. The window must block solar heat to reduce cooling loads. A window with U-Factor 0.25 and SHGC 0.22 qualifies in the South but not the North. It blocks the sun too well for Minneapolis. It does not block the sun enough for Phoenix. The ENERGY STAR logo appears on the window that qualifies. The logo does not say for which zone. The consumer reads the logo as a property of the glass. The property belongs to the glass-in-a-climate. The climate is in the specification. The logo is on the window.

Piece #1648: The Reciprocal

The wall beside the window is insulated to R-13 — thirteen units of thermal resistance per inch of thickness, reported higher-is-better. The window in the wall is rated at U-Factor 0.25 — a quarter unit of thermal conductance, reported lower-is-better. R equals one divided by U. The window at U-0.25 has R-4. The wall at R-13 has U-0.077. These are reciprocals of the same physical quantity — the rate at which heat passes through a barrier. The insulation industry chose to report resistance. The window industry chose to report conductance. One says R-13 and the consumer thinks "well-insulated." The other says U-0.25 and the consumer thinks "efficient window." If the window said R-4 and the insulation said R-13, the consumer would see, on the same wall, that the window is the weakest point by a factor of three. The formats prevent the comparison. The physics does not.

Piece #1649: The Glass

The NFRC label reports whole-window values: the area-weighted average of center-of-glass, edge-of-glass, and frame performance. Center-of-glass U-Factor for a triple-pane low-E argon unit can reach 0.10 — extraordinary insulation. But the aluminum or vinyl frame conducts heat at a much higher rate. The edge of glass, where the spacer bar separates the panes, is worse than the center. The whole-window U-Factor — 0.25 — is the truth the consumer lives with. Some manufacturers cite center-of-glass values in marketing materials. The NFRC label cites whole-window. The consumer who researches online finds 0.10. The consumer who reads the label finds 0.25. Same window. Different frame of reference. The frame is, literally, the frame.

Piece #1650: The Opening

Two hundred and forty-one Parts. The window is a hole in the envelope — the one place where the builder deliberately weakened the thermal barrier to let in light and air and view. The NFRC label quantifies the weakness: four numbers measured in a laboratory, on a component, under standardized conditions that include no flashing, no air sealing, no rough-opening thermal bridging, no integration with the water-resistive barrier. The label measures the window. The building experiences the installation. Field studies find that the majority of a window's in-situ thermal performance loss comes not from the glass or frame but from the gap between the window unit and the wall it sits in — the gap the label cannot measure because the gap does not exist in the test chamber. Thomas stood in the showroom reading U-Factor 0.25. He was reading the glass. He was not reading the gap. He was not reading the climate zone. He was not reading the reciprocal that would tell him his new window was R-4 in an R-13 wall. He was reading one number of four, on a label that described a component, in a format that prevented comparison with the material on every side of it. He bought the window. He hired a contractor. The contractor installed it. The gap performed its physics whether the label acknowledged it or not.
