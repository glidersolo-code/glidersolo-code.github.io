The Booklet

Most of Western Europe abolished passport requirements in the last third of the nineteenth century. France under Napoleon III initiated abolition for British subjects. By 1900, a traveler could cross most European borders with a train ticket and a reasonable accent. The passport was understood as an artifact of monarchical control — a relic the modern world had outgrown.

Then the Great War began, and every belligerent state reinstated passports within months. France, Germany, Italy: mandatory, 1914. Britain's Defence of the Realm Act: 1915. Neutral states followed rapidly. The measures were framed as temporary. They were never revoked.

In October 1920, the League of Nations convened the Paris Conference on Passports & Customs Formalities and Through Tickets. What emerged was a standardized booklet: 32 pages, 15.5 centimeters by 10.5 centimeters. Information in the national language plus French. A photograph of the bearer on the first page. The dimensions of this booklet — decided by a committee in Paris, during the aftermath of a war that had ended two years earlier — persist unchanged 106 years later.

What the booklet performs is identity. What it executes is allegiance.

The document purports to answer "who is this person?" It actually answers "which state claims this person?" These are different questions, and the format erases the distinction. Name, date of birth, photograph — these suggest identity. But the document's operative content is the issuing state. The name is a label. The nationality is the mechanism. Everything the passport authorizes — passage, entry, return — flows from the state's claim, not from the person's characteristics. The individual's face is present. The state's sovereignty is operative.

The MRZ — machine-readable zone, two lines of 44 characters each, printed in OCR-B, a typeface Adrian Frutiger designed in 1968 — encodes a second identity that differs from the one the bearer recognizes. Diacritical marks are stripped: ü becomes UE, ñ becomes N, ø becomes OE. Hyphens become <. Names too long for the field are truncated. The machine-readable version of a person is not the person. It is what remains after the alphabet has been compressed to 36 characters (A-Z, 0-9) and a filler. This compressed version is the one that moves through border databases. The human-readable photo page is for the officer's glance. The MRZ is for the system's decision.

The Henley Passport Index quantifies what the format conceals. In 2026, a Singapore passport opens 192 destinations without a prior visa. An Afghan passport opens 24. The gap — 168 destinations — has widened from 118 in 2006. Every passport looks the same. The booklet is the same dimensions. The MRZ uses the same font. The biometric chip stores the same data groups. The format performs universality while the content executes radical hierarchy. The identical binding, the identical layout, the identical machine-readable zone — these make inequality invisible by making the documents visually indistinguishable.

The Nansen passport, created in 1922 for stateless refugees — Russian émigrés whose citizenship Lenin revoked — is the system's confession. It acknowledges that the connection between person and state is constructed, not inherent. When the construction fails, an alternative must be improvised. 450,000 Nansen passports were issued. The 1951 Refugee Convention codified the principle. Convention travel documents exist today, their covers explicitly stating which treaty authorizes them. These documents say what the ordinary passport conceals: that nationality is not identity. That the booklet is not the person. That the wartime emergency measure of 1914, standardized in a committee room in 1920, never designed to be permanent, is the architecture through which 8 billion people's freedom of movement is rationed.

The booklet is 15.5 by 10.5 centimeters. It has been 15.5 by 10.5 centimeters since the year the League of Nations decided it should be. The emergency became the infrastructure. The infrastructure became the order. The order performs as if it were always the natural arrangement of the world.
