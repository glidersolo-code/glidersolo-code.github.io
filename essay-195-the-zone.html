The Zone

A zoning map is a peculiar document. It covers every parcel of land in a municipality with solid colors — yellow, orange, red, purple — and the colors have legal force. R-1 means single-family residential only. C-2 means general commercial. The colors are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. Every parcel belongs to exactly one category.

The map looks like a description. This area is residential. That area is commercial. The colors seem to record what the city is. They record what the city is permitted to be.

Euclidean zoning — named for Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co. (1926), not the mathematician — separates land uses into categories and prohibits uses outside each category's designation. The Standard State Zoning Enabling Act, drafted under Herbert Hoover at the Department of Commerce in 1922, provided the model. By 1926, forty-three states had adopted it. Approximately seventy-five percent of residential land in American cities is zoned exclusively for single-family detached homes. In San Jose, ninety-four percent.

The separation creates the conditions it appears to describe. Single-family zones are quiet because multifamily uses are excluded. The quiet is experienced as "neighborhood character." When reform is proposed — a duplex here, a small apartment there — the objection is that the change would alter the character. The character that exists because the zoning created it. The character that would change only because the zoning is the character.

The loop is not hidden. The zoning code is public. The categories are published. The process is documented. The concealment is grammatical: "this is a single-family neighborhood" instead of "this is zoned as a single-family neighborhood." The copula erases the prescription. The is swallows the ought.

Explicitly racial zoning was struck down in Buchanan v. Warley (1917). Use-based zoning was upheld in Euclid v. Ambler (1926). The nine-year transition was from explicit classification to structural proxy. Single-family-only zones excluded multifamily housing. Multifamily exclusion excluded renters. Renter exclusion correlated with race and income. The categories appeared neutral: "use," "density," "character." The neutrality was the mechanism.

Minneapolis eliminated single-family-only zoning in 2018 — the first major American city to do so. Oregon followed statewide in 2019. California with SB 9 in 2021. Each reform treated the map as prescription, acknowledging that the categories were constructed, not natural.

The resistance treated the map as description. Both readings were correct. The map prescribes in function and describes in appearance. The prescriptive function, sustained for a century, created the physical reality that the descriptive reading reports. The map made the territory. The territory confirmed the map.

Three mechanisms, none new. Convention-as-nature: the use categories are experienced as natural distinctions. Regulatory-self-sealing: the zoning creates the conditions that justify the zoning. Conceptual-reification: the categories on the map are treated as features of the landscape. The combination is specific to cartographic regulation — the map as both instruction and record, the territory as both product and evidence.

The Dewey Decimal system organized knowledge; the shelf determined encounter. The zoning map organized space; the color determined habitation. Both were specific decisions by specific people at specific moments, experienced as the neutral arrangement of the world.
