Part CLXII: The Test

Piece #1251: The Author

Carl Brigham, Princeton. In 1923 he published A Study of American Intelligence, arguing that Nordic races were intellectually superior and that immigration was diluting the national intellect. In 1926 he developed the Scholastic Aptitude Test for the College Board. The test would measure innate ability — aptitude, independent of schooling, background, or preparation. By 1930, Brigham had recanted his racial theories. By 1934, he was trying to stop the College Board from using the SAT for admissions. He called it a corruption of psychometric principles. The College Board did not stop. The test outlived its author's objections. The test outlived the theory of aptitude that justified it. The test outlived its name: in 1994, "Aptitude" became "Assessment." In 1997, SAT stopped standing for anything at all. The retreat from the original concept was total. The institutional power of the test was unchanged.

Piece #1252: The Correlation

The correlation between SAT scores and family income is approximately r = 0.40. Each $20,000 increment in household income is associated with roughly 20 additional SAT points. Students from families earning above $200,000 score, on average, approximately 400 points higher than students from families earning below $20,000. The 400-point gap is larger than the range that separates most applicants from each other. Test preparation courses raise scores by 30 to 100 points. The most expensive preparation — private tutoring at $200-500 per hour — produces the largest gains. The test was designed to measure ability independent of background. The test measures background. The measurement is not false — students from wealthier families have, on average, better preparation, more exposure to the tested content, and greater familiarity with the test format. The test accurately measures this composite. The test is labeled as measuring something else. The label and the measurement diverge. The gap between them is the hidden channel.

Piece #1253: The Margin

The SAT predicts first-year college GPA at r = 0.35 to 0.53. High school GPA alone predicts at r = 0.55. Adding the SAT to high school GPA increases explained variance by approximately five percentage points. The marginal contribution is real. The marginal contribution is smaller than a single course grade in a single semester. The number — a three-digit score, precise to the tens digit, on a scale suggesting measurement rigor comparable to a physical instrument — inspires more confidence than five percentage points of additional variance should warrant. The format does the work. A graph showing that high school GPA explains 30% of college GPA variance and the SAT adds 5% would change how people think about the test. The number — 1350, 1420, 1510 — does not communicate its own explanatory power. The number communicates itself.

Piece #1254: The Shift

In 1995, the SAT was recentered. The average verbal score had declined from approximately 530 in 1963 to approximately 424 in 1994. The College Board raised the average back to 500 by adjusting the scale. A verbal score of 500 in 1994 corresponded to approximately 580 in the recentered system. The math scale shifted less: approximately 20 points. A student scoring 500 verbal in 1994 and a student scoring 500 verbal in 1996 performed at different levels. The same number, different meanings. The recentering was announced. The announcement did not accompany every score report. The number on the page looked the same. The scale beneath it had moved. The infrastructure of interpretation — college admissions offices comparing scores across years — absorbed the shift. The number appeared stable. The measurement was not.

Piece #1255: The Optional

Over 1,800 colleges adopted test-optional policies between 2020 and 2024. William Hiss and Valerie Franks at Bates College studied 123,000 students across 33 institutions: non-submitters and submitters had similar graduation rates (difference of 0.6%) and similar GPAs (difference of 0.05). The removal of the test did not collapse the admissions process. The signal the test provided was real and replaceable. The test had occupied its position not because it was the strongest predictor — high school GPA was stronger — but because it was the most portable, the most numerical, the most apparently objective. The format — standardized, quantitative, comparable across schools — gave the test institutional power disproportionate to its predictive contribution. The format was the product. The measurement was the alibi.
