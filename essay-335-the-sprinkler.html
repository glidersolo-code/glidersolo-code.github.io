The Sprinkler

The oscillating sprinkler box says "covers up to 3,600 square feet." The consumer reads: one sprinkler, centered in the yard, waters 3,600 square feet. The number performs as the sprinkler's capability. The number is the sprinkler's capability at 80 PSI through a short hose — a condition most suburban lawns do not meet.

Most residential outdoor spigots deliver 30 to 60 PSI after the pressure drop through the house plumbing. A hundred feet of 5/8-inch garden hose adds friction loss — five to ten PSI, sometimes more, depending on fittings, kinks, and the coil in the grass. By the time the water reaches the sprinkler's nozzles, the pressure may be 30 PSI. The coverage area scales with pressure nonlinearly: throw distance varies with the square root of pressure, and area varies with the square of throw distance. At 30 PSI, the sprinkler delivers roughly 37 percent of the area it achieves at 80 PSI. Thirteen hundred square feet instead of thirty-six hundred. One-third of the box. The consumer who bought one sprinkler for 3,200 square feet discovers dry corners.

The "up to" qualifier is technically present and functionally invisible. In the aisle, two boxes: "covers up to 3,600 sq ft" and "covers up to 2,400 sq ft." The consumer compares the integers. The "up to" is identical on both boxes — a constant that cancels in the comparison. What remains are two numbers and the assumption that larger means more. The consumer never asks: "up to" at what pressure? The format has already framed the comparison as a contest between areas, not between conditions.

Hose diameter compounds the gap. A 5/8-inch hose at 60 PSI delivers approximately 10.5 gallons per minute. A 1/2-inch hose delivers 6.3. The sprinkler's nozzles require a minimum flow rate to achieve full oscillation and throw distance. Below that threshold, the oscillation mechanism slows, the throw shortens, and the rectangle becomes a narrow strip. The consumer who connects the sprinkler to the hose already coiled in the garage has introduced a variable the box did not specify. The box specified the sprinkler's capability. The hose determines the sprinkler's reality.

Daisy-chaining — connecting multiple sprinklers in series on successive hose sections — compounds the gap multiplicatively. The first sprinkler draws pressure. The second gets less. The third gets whatever remains. A consumer who buys three 3,600-square-foot sprinklers for a half-acre yard may get full coverage from the first, half from the second, and a wet circle from the third. The box's number described each sprinkler independently, at 80 PSI, through a short connection. The consumer installed them as a system the box never modeled.

The sprinkler is a nozzle at the end of a pipe at the end of a system. Every component between the water main and the nozzle subtracts from the number on the box. The box's number is the nozzle's potential in isolation. The yard is not isolation. The yard is pressure loss, hose friction, hose diameter, hose length, and the distance from the spigot to the lawn. The number was tested in a lab. The lawn is not a lab. The rectangle the consumer expects is the rectangle the lab achieved. The rectangle the lawn produces is smaller, browner at the edges, and purchased on the strength of a number that described a different installation entirely.
