Part CLXIII: The Index

Piece #1256: The Belgian

Adolphe Quetelet, Brussels, 1832. An astronomer. A statistician. Not a physician. He wanted to characterize the average man — l'homme moyen — using mathematical relationships between physical measurements. He needed a formula that would normalize weight across different heights: a way to compare a tall man's weight to a short man's weight on a common scale. He chose weight divided by height squared. He chose squaring because it produced a roughly constant ratio across the Belgian and French men in his data. The choice was pragmatic, not physiological. There is no biological reason to square the height. Cubing the height would approximate isometric scaling — same proportions, different sizes. Squaring works for Quetelet's 19th-century Belgian population. It underestimates adiposity in shorter people and overestimates it in taller people. The exponent was a convenience. The convenience became doctrine.

Piece #1257: The Name

Ancel Keys, University of Minnesota, 1972. Journal of Chronic Diseases. He evaluated several indices of relative weight: the Quetelet Index, ponderal index, Broca index. He concluded that the Quetelet Index was "if not fully satisfactory, at least as good as any other" for epidemiological studies of obesity. He renamed it the Body Mass Index. The name change was consequential. "Quetelet Index" sounds like a statistical convenience. "Body Mass Index" sounds like a clinical measurement. The formula did not change. The perception changed. Keys intended it for populations — epidemiological studies comparing obesity rates across groups. The index migrated to clinical medicine and was applied to individuals. The population-level tool became a diagnostic for a person sitting in a doctor's office. Keys did not recommend this migration. The migration happened because the formula was simple, the calculation was fast, and the alternative — direct measurement of body fat — required expensive equipment.

Piece #1258: The Threshold

The WHO Expert Committee, Geneva, 1995. Technical Report Series 854. The thresholds: underweight below 18.5, normal 18.5-24.9, overweight 25.0-29.9, obese 30.0 and above. The thresholds were derived primarily from mortality data in European and North American populations — the populations for which the most data existed. They were applied globally. In 2004, a WHO expert consultation examined BMI in Asian populations. The finding: Asian populations have higher body fat percentages and higher risks of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at lower BMI values. The consultation recommended "public health action points" at 23 and 27.5 for Asian populations. The recommendation was not binding. Most clinical guidelines worldwide continue to use 25 and 30. The thresholds calibrated for European bodies are applied to bodies they were not calibrated for. The thresholds are infrastructure. Infrastructure outlives the population that built it.

Piece #1259: The Miscount

Tomiyama, Hunger, Nguyen-Cuu, and Wells. International Journal of Obesity, 2016. They analyzed NHANES data — a nationally representative sample of the US population — and cross-referenced BMI categories with cardiometabolic health markers: blood pressure, triglycerides, cholesterol, glucose, insulin resistance, and C-reactive protein. Among people classified as overweight by BMI: 47% were metabolically healthy. Among people classified as obese: 29% were metabolically healthy. Among people classified as normal weight: 30% were metabolically unhealthy. The BMI-based classification correctly identified metabolic health in approximately 51% of the adult population. The formula classified half the population correctly and half incorrectly. The formula remained the standard clinical screening tool. The standard screening tool performs at chance level for the outcome it is used to predict.

Piece #1260: The Weight

The formula measures weight and height. The formula does not measure fat. It does not measure muscle. It does not measure bone density or water weight. A bodybuilder with 8% body fat and a sedentary person with 32% body fat, at the same height and weight, receive the same BMI. The formula treats all mass as equivalent because the formula contains no variable for composition. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry measures fat directly. Bioelectrical impedance estimates it. Hydrostatic weighing calculates it. BMI does not measure what these tools measure. BMI measures what a bathroom scale and a tape measure can provide. The formula is popular because it is cheap. The formula is clinical because it is popular. The formula defines obesity because it defines obesity. The circularity is invisible because the formula is the only one in the room.
