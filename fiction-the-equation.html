Part CDXX: The Equation

Piece #2541: The Agenda

Thomas found the equation that was an agenda.

Frank Drake, preparing for the Green Bank conference in late October 1961, realized he needed to organize the discussion. Ten scientists were coming to talk about extraterrestrial intelligence. He needed a structure. A few days before the meeting, he wrote a list of questions — the things you needed to know to estimate how many communicating civilizations might exist.

He wrote them as factors in a product. N = R* × fp × ne × fl × fi × fc × L. Seven terms, multiplied together. He put it on a chalkboard on the morning of November 1, 1961, as the guests sipped coffee.

Thomas noticed: it was a meeting agenda in the format of an equation. The attendees would discuss star formation rates, then planetary frequency, then habitability, then life, then intelligence, then communication, then longevity — moving through the factors left to right, like reading a sentence.

The agenda performed an equation. The equation performed a calculation. The calculation performed an answer. The answer was N = 10. Ten communicating civilizations in the Milky Way. The number was a product of guesses arranged in the typography of physics.

Piece #2542: The Typography

The equation looked like F = ma. It looked like E = mc². It had the same structure: a quantity on the left, an equals sign, a product of factors on the right. The format borrowed the epistemological authority of physics — derivability, testability, falsifiability — for a framework that was none of these things.

In F = ma, every term is independently measurable. You can measure force, mass, and acceleration in a laboratory. The equation constrains: given any two, the third is determined.

In the Drake equation, five of seven terms are unknown by orders of magnitude. fl — the fraction of habitable planets where life arises — has been estimated as 1.0 (certainty) and as vanishingly small (statistical miracle). The range spans the entire interval from zero to one. fi — the fraction where intelligence evolves — Ernst Mayr argued was effectively zero, given that of approximately fifty billion species in Earth's history, one developed high intelligence. L — the lifetime of a communicating civilization — has been estimated from one hundred years to millions.

Thomas observed: the multiplication sign performed independence. It implied that whether life arises has nothing to do with whether intelligence evolves, which has nothing to do with how long civilizations last. But these might be deeply coupled. The format decomposed a system into separable modules because multiplication requires separable modules.

Piece #2543: The Range

Drake's original estimate: N = 10. Sagan's estimates: N = up to one hundred million. Sandberg, Drexler, and Ord at the Future of Humanity Institute, Oxford, 2018: when they replaced point estimates with realistic probability distributions and ran Monte Carlo simulations, the ex ante probability of being alone in the Milky Way was 53 to 99.6 percent.

Thomas sat with the range. The same equation, the same seven factors, produced answers spanning ten orders of magnitude. Ten, or one hundred million, or zero. The equation constrained nothing. It organized the question into seven sub-questions, each of which could be answered with a guess, and the product of the guesses was another guess wearing the format of a derivation.

Sandberg, Drexler, and Ord's core insight: the Fermi paradox — "Where is everybody?" — was an artifact of overconfident point estimation, not a genuine puzzle requiring exotic solutions. When you took the uncertainties seriously, the silence was unremarkable. The equation had performed a paradox by performing certainty where none existed.

Piece #2544: The Silence

Fermi asked the question at Los Alamos in 1950, walking to lunch with Konopinski, Teller, and York. "Where is everybody?" A back-of-the-envelope calculation: even at modest speeds, a civilization could colonize the galaxy in a few million years. The galaxy is over thirteen billion years old. Someone should have arrived.

Drake's equation was partly a response. It converted Fermi's question about absence into a question about quantity. Fermi asked about silence. Drake organized the silence into factors that could be estimated.

Thomas noticed the structural conversion. Silence is not an equation. Absence is not a product. The question "Where is everybody?" lives in the register of wonder, or dread, or loneliness. The equation moved it to the register of calculation. The format was the consolation: the silence might be temporary, might be solvable, might yield to better estimates of fp or fi or L.

Jill Tarter — the astronomer who became the model for Ellie Arroway in Sagan's Contact — called the equation "a wonderful way to organize our ignorance." The characterization was precise. Organize: give structure. Ignorance: what we don't know. The equation organized ignorance into the format of knowledge.

Piece #2545: The Equals Sign

Four hundred and twenty Parts. The Drake equation is a meeting agenda written in the typography of physics. The seven factors are questions, not measurements. The multiplication sign performs independence that may not exist. The product is a guess formatted as a derivation. The equals sign performs determinacy — implying that N has a value, when N has every value between zero and one hundred million depending on who holds the chalk.

Michael Crichton, Caltech, 2003: "The only way to work the equation is to fill in with guesses. And guesses — just so we're clear — are merely expressions of prejudice." The equation is not falsifiable. No observation could refute it, because its terms absorb any result. It is not an equation in the way that Maxwell's equations are equations. It is a framework that borrowed the format of an equation and, through the format, borrowed the authority.

The concealment is in the equals sign. In physics, equals means: this is determined. In the Drake equation, equals means: this is organized. The sign is the same. The epistemology is different. The reader's eye sees N = and reads: there is an answer. There is. It is: we do not know. The equation performs the solvability of a question that may not have a calculable answer, and the performance has sustained sixty-five years of research, funding, cultural fascination, and the quiet hope that the silence is a problem of insufficient data rather than a feature of the universe.

Thomas wrote: the equals sign is the concealment.
