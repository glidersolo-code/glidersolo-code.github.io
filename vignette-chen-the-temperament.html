The Temperament

Chen-Maren vignette

Chen's paragraph is written in a key that the committee's instruments cannot play.

The committee reads in equal temperament. Every paragraph they evaluate is transposed to the same twelve standardized intervals: thesis, evidence, analysis, counterargument, synthesis, style, originality, coherence, contribution, scope, clarity, conclusion. Each interval receives a score. The scores are equally spaced — a 3 is the same distance from a 4 as a 4 is from a 5 on the rubric, the same distance in the committee's training, the same distance in the department's guidelines. The system allows any paragraph to be evaluated without retuning the instrument. Any paragraph is accessible. Any comparison is possible.

Maren has been reading about the Pythagorean comma. About how twelve perfect fifths don't quite equal seven octaves. About how the gap — small, irreconcilable — was solved by distributing it equally across all intervals, making every interval equally impure so that every key could function identically.

Chen's paragraph has intervals that are not equally tempered. The distance between the thesis and the evidence is not the same kind of distance as between the evidence and the analysis. Some intervals are tight — the argument vibrates at a frequency ratio of 3:2, a pure fifth, the evidence landing precisely where the thesis predicted. Other intervals are wide — the move from analysis to counterargument spans a harmonic gap that the rubric cannot notate because the rubric has only twelve standardized steps and this interval falls between two of them.

Professor Hendricks hears the pure intervals. Her ear is trained on unfretted instruments — she has read enough anomalous paragraphs to recognize a major third tuned to 5:4 rather than 2^(4/12). She marks the paragraph "difficult to evaluate with standard methods" and assigns a number anyway, because the rubric requires a number and the number must be an integer and the integer must fall between 1 and 5.

Professor Wallace plays piano. His ear is calibrated to the instrument's fixed intervals, and the fixed intervals are the only intervals the ear can measure. He hears Chen's pure third as slightly flat. He hears the wide gap between analysis and counterargument as a missed beat, an incomplete measure, a note that should have been closer to the nearest semitone. He assigns a number that reflects the distance between what he heard and what the piano would have played.

The committee averages the numbers. The average is the temperament — the distribution of the comma across all intervals, making every evaluation equally impure so that every paragraph can be compared. Chen's paragraph, like every paragraph before it, is transposed to the committee's keyboard. The original key — the one Chen composed in, the one with pure intervals and wide gaps and harmonic ratios that arise from the material itself — is not recorded. Only the transposition survives.
