The Speaker

A box says 1000W. The speaker inside produces approximately 40 watts of continuous audio power.

The discrepancy is PMPO — Peak Music Power Output. The number represents a theoretical instantaneous maximum: peak voltage squared divided by minimum impedance, measured for a fraction of a millisecond, under conditions the speaker cannot sustain. PMPO has no standard definition. No test procedure governs it. Different manufacturers calculate it differently. The number that dominates the box — the number the consumer compares while shopping — is the one with the least relationship to the listening experience.

The FTC Amplifier Rule (16 CFR Part 432, effective 1974) exists specifically to prevent this. It requires power claims for amplifiers to be stated in continuous average power (RMS), measured across the full audio bandwidth (20 Hz to 20,000 Hz), at no more than the rated total harmonic distortion, into the rated impedance. The rule was designed to make audio power claims comparable and honest. It applies to amplifiers. It does not clearly apply to integrated speaker systems, Bluetooth speakers, or imported consumer electronics sold direct-to-consumer through online marketplaces. The rule exists; the enforcement gap is the box.

Three wattage numbers describe the same amplifier. A receiver might be 100 watts RMS per channel, 200 watts peak, and 400 watts "dynamic power." The hardware is identical. The number changes with the measurement method. Peak power measures the instantaneous maximum of the waveform — roughly twice RMS for a sine wave. Dynamic power measures a brief transient burst at reduced distortion requirements. PMPO abandons measurement altogether and calculates a theoretical ceiling. The consumer who compares "100W RMS" to "1000W PMPO" is comparing an honest measurement to a marketing abstraction and reasonably concluding that the latter is ten times more powerful.

Impedance compounds the ambiguity. The same amplifier rated at 50 watts into 8 ohms produces roughly 80 watts into 4 ohms. Lower impedance draws more current, yielding a higher number. Manufacturers can rate into 4, 6, or 8 ohms; the impedance appears in the specifications, not on the box. The wattage appears on the box. The consumer compares the visible number while the invisible number determines what it means.

Behind all of this lies a logarithmic mismatch. Perceived loudness doubles with approximately every 10 dB increase. Ten dB requires ten times the power. Doubling from 50 to 100 watts — a 3 dB increase — is barely perceptible. The consumer who upgrades from a 50-watt to a 100-watt speaker has doubled the power to achieve the smallest volume increment the ear reliably detects. The relationship between watts and loudness is logarithmic. The consumer compares linearly. Marketing exploits neither the physics nor the perception but the gap between them — presenting linear numbers to logarithmic ears, inflated by methods the consumer cannot evaluate, at impedances the consumer cannot see.
