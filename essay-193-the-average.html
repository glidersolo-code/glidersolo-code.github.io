Essay 193: The Average

Thirty stocks represent the market.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average, published since 1896, measures thirty stocks. The United States has approximately 4,000 publicly traded companies. The thirty are selected by a committee at S&P Dow Jones Indices, using qualitative criteria: "leading company," "excellent reputation," "sustained growth," "of interest to a large number of investors." The selection is subjective. The number is reported as "the market."

The index is price-weighted. Each stock's influence is proportional to its share price, not its market capitalization, not its revenue, not its economic importance. A stock at $400 per share moves the index four times more than a stock at $100, regardless of company size. The weighting is an artifact of Charles Dow's original arithmetic — sum the prices, divide by the number of stocks. The arithmetic was simple. The consequence is that the index is disproportionately sensitive to high-priced stocks, which are high-priced for reasons (stock split history, share count decisions) unrelated to economic significance.

The divisor maintains continuity. When Dow created the index, the divisor was 12. After 128 years of stock splits, additions, and removals, the divisor is approximately 0.152. The sum of thirty stock prices divided by 0.152 produces a number in the tens of thousands. The number is not an average of anything — it is a continuity-adjusted sum, a mathematical artifact. The divisor is invisible in reporting: "the Dow is at 38,000" does not explain that this number is thirty prices divided by 0.152.

The committee curates. Apple was added in 2015. General Electric — original from 1896 — was removed in 2018. Amazon replaced Walgreens in 2024. Each change alters what the index measures. The divisor adjusts to make the transition seamless. The seamlessness conceals the change: the index appears continuous across compositional shifts. Survivorship bias operates — declining companies are removed, growing companies are added. The index tracks the committee's curation as much as it tracks the market.

The number is reflexive. Futures contracts, options, ETFs, and retirement portfolios track the Dow. When the Dow moves, money moves. The movement of money affects the stocks. The stocks affect the Dow. The measurement influences the measured. The reflexivity is the infrastructure: a number so widely watched that it becomes a force, a measurement so influential that it shapes the thing it claims to observe.

The concealment is selection-as-representation. A committee-curated, price-weighted index of thirty stocks is treated as a measurement of the market — 4,000 companies, millions of transactions, the entire publicly traded economy. The number does not claim to represent the market. The headlines do. "The Dow rose 200 points" is reported as "the market rose." The translation is the concealment: a specific, curated, arbitrarily weighted number becomes a statement about the whole. The number is real — it moves money, retirement decisions, economic sentiment. The number is not the market. The market is elsewhere.
