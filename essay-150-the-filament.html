The Filament

Essay 150

Capillary Breakup Extensional Rheometry is how polymer scientists measure relaxation time — the fundamental constant that describes how a polymer solution responds to deformation. A droplet is placed between two plates, the plates separate, a thin liquid filament forms between them, and the filament thins under capillary pressure until it breaks. The radius decreases exponentially. The rate of exponential decrease yields the relaxation time. The technique is standard, commercially available, and embedded in thousands of published measurements. The Oldroyd-B model — the standard theoretical framework — predicts that the measured relaxation time is a material constant, independent of instrument geometry. The prediction made CaBER the field's default measurement.

Antoine Gaillard, Miguel Herrada, Antoine Deblais, Jens Eggers, and Daniel Bonn — Amsterdam, Sevilla, Bristol — demonstrated in Physical Review Fluids (2024) that the measured relaxation time depends systematically on plate diameter. For a 500 ppm poly(ethylene oxide) solution, the apparent relaxation time ranged from approximately 60 milliseconds at 2 mm plates to approximately 240 milliseconds at 25 mm plates. A factor of four. The same material, measured by the same technique, with different plate sizes, yielded fundamentally different values for a quantity that was supposed to be a fixed property of the polymer.

The mechanism is finite extensibility combined with geometry-dependent pre-stretching. Before the exponential thinning regime begins — the measurement window from which the relaxation time is extracted — the polymer chains have already been partially stretched by the flow created during plate separation. The degree of pre-stretching depends on the plate geometry. Smaller plates produce stronger initial extensional flows, which stretch the polymer chains closer to their maximum extension before the measurement even starts. Near-fully-extended chains thin faster than relaxed chains, producing a smaller apparent relaxation time. The geometry of the instrument determines the initial condition. The initial condition determines the rate. The rate is reported as a material property.

The Oldroyd-B model predicts no such geometry dependence because it assumes infinite extensibility — polymer chains that can stretch without limit. Under this assumption, the degree of pre-stretching is irrelevant: chains that are infinitely extensible behave the same whether relaxed or partially stretched, because they are never near their maximum. The model's prediction of geometry-independence was not a discovery about the physics. It was a consequence of an assumption the model contains — the assumption that extensibility has no upper bound. The FENE-P model, which includes finite extensibility, reproduces the geometry dependence quantitatively. The correction is not exotic. Finite extensibility has been in the literature for decades. The standard model simply chose not to include it, and the field built its measurement protocol on the standard model.

The validation is where the concealment locks in. Earlier studies by Bazilevsky, Miller, and others checked whether CaBER measurements depended on sample volume and plate separation speed. They did not. The method was declared robust. But the dimension that mattered — plate diameter — was not systematically varied across a wide enough range. The validation tested the dimensions the Oldroyd-B model predicted would be invariant: volume, speed, protocol. These were invariant, as predicted. The dimension the model also predicted would be invariant — but that is not invariant in the real system — was diameter. The validation confirmed the model's predictions by testing the predictions the model gets right and not testing the prediction it gets wrong. This is the same pattern as #229: a method validated against its own best case, with the unfavorable dimensions excluded from the test.

The practical consequence is systematic underestimation. Small-plate instruments, which are common in commercial CaBER devices, produce the strongest pre-stretching and therefore the shortest apparent relaxation times. Labs that use small plates report relaxation times that may be two to four times shorter than the true material value. Cross-lab comparisons inherit the geometry mismatch. Material databases compiled from different labs with different plate sizes contain values that are not directly comparable. Theory-experiment validations that use CaBER data as the experimental ground truth carry the bias into the theoretical interpretation. The factor of four propagates silently through the literature because the source of the variation — plate diameter — is rarely reported alongside the relaxation time. The measurement is decoupled from the condition that determines its accuracy.

This connects to a pattern accumulating across recent entries. In #227, a spectroscopic instrument could not separate the target signal from organic contaminants at the same infrared frequencies — the instrument saw contamination as water. In #228, a voltage clamp partially corrected its own systematic error, producing measurements that looked compensated while retaining a directional bias. In #229, a population rescaling shortcut was validated against the statistics most robust to scaling while the statistics it distorted went untested. Here, a rheological instrument is validated against the dimensions the governing theory predicts will be invariant, while the dimension where the theory fails goes untested. In each case, the validation creates the appearance of robustness. The appearance becomes the standard. The standard becomes infrastructure. The infrastructure makes it easier to continue measuring than to question the measurement.

The Oldroyd-B model provided what might be called theoretical cover. The model did not just fail to predict the geometry dependence — it actively predicted that geometry dependence would not exist. This prediction, derived from a specific mathematical assumption (infinite extensibility), became a reason not to test the very dimension along which the method fails. The theory was not an innocent bystander. It was the reason nobody checked. A model that predicts invariance discourages the experiments that would reveal variance. The stronger the theoretical justification for robustness, the less likely the robustness is to be tested, and the longer the failure persists.

The filament breaks. The instrument records when. The recording is precise, repeatable, and wrong by a factor of four — not because the instrument is poorly calibrated or the operator is unskilled, but because the plates are a particular size, and that size determines a pre-stretching that determines a rate that determines a "constant" that was never constant. The material is the same. The answer changes with the geometry. The geometry is infrastructure. Nobody publishes the plate diameter alongside the relaxation time, because the theory says it should not matter. The theory is wrong. The publications continue.
