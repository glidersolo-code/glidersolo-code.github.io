The Index

Adolphe Quetelet was a Belgian astronomer and statistician who wanted to describe the average man. In 1832, he devised a formula: weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. The index was not a medical tool. It was a mathematical convenience — a way to normalize body weight across heights for population-level analysis. Quetelet chose height squared not because squaring the height had biological meaning but because it produced a roughly constant ratio across the Belgian men he studied. The formula was descriptive. The formula was statistical. The formula became clinical.

Ancel Keys renamed it the Body Mass Index in 1972. He recommended it as a practical adiposity measure for epidemiology — explicitly acknowledging its limitations. "If not fully satisfactory," he wrote, "at least as good as any other." Keys was measuring populations. The index migrated to individuals. The population tool became a clinical diagnostic.

The WHO established the thresholds in 1995: underweight below 18.5, normal 18.5 to 24.9, overweight 25 to 29.9, obese 30 and above. The thresholds were derived from mortality data in European and North American populations. They were applied worldwide. In 2004, a WHO expert consultation noted that Asian populations have higher body fat percentages and higher cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI values. The consultation recommended different action points — 23 and 27.5 — but the original thresholds remained the global clinical standard. A formula calibrated for 19th-century Belgian men defines obesity for 21st-century humans everywhere.

The formula measures weight and height. It does not measure body fat. It does not distinguish between muscle mass and fat mass. A professional athlete and a sedentary office worker of the same height and weight have the same BMI. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry measures body fat directly. BMI does not. The formula cannot see fat distribution — visceral fat wrapped around organs versus subcutaneous fat beneath the skin — which matters more for metabolic risk than total mass. It treats all weight as equivalent because it measures all weight as equivalent. It was not designed to do more.

Tomiyama and colleagues, analyzing NHANES data, found that BMI-based classification correctly identified metabolic health in approximately 51 percent of the US adult population. Nearly half of people classified as overweight were metabolically healthy. Thirty percent of people classified as normal weight were metabolically unhealthy. The classification was approximately as accurate as chance.

The index occupies every physician's office, every electronic health record, every public health report. Its authority derives not from what it calculates — a ratio of weight to height — but from the context in which it is calculated: the clinical encounter, the medical chart, the diagnostic code. The format — a single number with named categories — performs the work of a measurement even when the number measures something other than what the categories imply.

Quetelet was describing the average Belgian. The average Belgian became the global threshold. The global threshold became a diagnostic. The diagnostic became a number in a chart that a patient reads as a statement about their health. At every step, the formula stayed the same. At every step, what the formula was asked to mean expanded beyond what the formula could say.
