Chen, the Drought

Chen reads the Gagan stalagmite paper on a train that passes through limestone country — chalk hills, the land white where the road cuts through it. The paper describes fifteen thousand years of drying recorded in mineral layers inside a cave on an island in Indonesia. Each layer is a year's rainfall translated into chemistry. Read backward: the drought was there all along.

Chen texts Maren: They blamed the competitor. It was the climate.

Maren: Which one?

Chen: The hobbits. Homo floresiensis. Everyone assumed Homo sapiens drove them to extinction because the timing overlapped. But the stalagmites show the drought started 26,000 years earlier. The prey base collapsed. The rivers went dry. The competitor hadn't arrived yet.

Maren: Are you talking about the hobbits or about us?

Chen stares at the text for the full length of a tunnel. When the train emerges, the chalk hills are still there, white and indifferent.

The paragraph — thirty-nine lines — has been in Maren's inbox for months now. Chen attributed Maren's silence to the paragraph's arrival: before the paragraph, conversation flowed; after the paragraph, silence. The coincidence was sufficient. The paragraph arrived, the warmth departed, cause and effect.

But Chen, reading the stalagmite paper on a train through limestone, thinks now about the layers before the paragraph. About the emails that had already been getting shorter for a year before the thirty-nine lines were sent. About the conversations that had been trending toward one-word replies not because Chen did something specific but because Maren's own climate was shifting — her postdoc ending, her committee convening, her attention drawn toward a future that did not obviously include arguments about concealment mechanisms in scientific literature.

The paragraph was the competitor. The drought was already there.

On the chalk hills, the road cuts expose the rock: white layers going down and down, each one a year of ocean sediment compressed to stone. The record is there. Chen just had not read it because the dramatic event — the paragraph — was easier to see.

Chen opens the laptop. Opens the email archive. Scrolls back past the paragraph, past the months of silence, to the time before. Reads the layers. The emails get shorter. The responses take longer. The warmth is there but thinner, like summer rainfall declining millimeter by millimeter. The trend started a full year before the paragraph.

The paragraph did not cause the silence. The paragraph arrived in a silence that was already forming.

Chen closes the laptop. The train passes another chalk cut. White layers. Fifteen thousand years of drying, recorded in stone. Nobody read it for two decades because the dramatic story was sufficient.

The limestone holds the record. Whether anyone reads it is a separate question.
