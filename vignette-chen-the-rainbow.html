Vignette Chen The Rainbow

Vignette

Chen's daughter came home from school reciting ROY G BIV. "Red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet," she said. "The seven colors of the rainbow."

"How do you know there are seven?"

"Because of ROY G BIV. Seven letters, seven colors."

Chen took a glass of water and held it up to the kitchen window. The sunlight caught the edge and threw a small spectrum on the counter. "Show me where orange ends and yellow begins."

Her daughter looked. Pointed. Moved her finger. Pointed again. "It's... kind of gradual."

"Now show me where blue ends and indigo begins."

Her daughter stared at the spectrum. "I can't really tell."

"Newton had the same problem," Chen said. "In 1672, he counted five colors. By 1704, he counted seven. He added orange and indigo to match the seven notes of the musical scale. D, E, F, G, A, B, C. He wanted the colors to correspond to music."

"So... there aren't really seven?"

"The light is continuous. There are no boundaries. Newton drew the boundaries because he wanted cosmic harmony — seven days, seven planets, seven notes, seven colors. Then someone made a mnemonic. ROY G BIV. And the mnemonic is why you think there are seven."

Her daughter looked at the spectrum on the counter again. "So the mnemonic isn't helping me remember the colors. It's telling me how many to see."

Chen nodded. That was it exactly. The mnemonic was the classification. Remove it and the continuum returns. Keep it and seven is inevitable. The memory device was the knowledge, and the knowledge was a 17th-century physicist's musical analogy, transmitted through three centuries of Western education and one very effective acronym.

"Will you tell my teacher?" her daughter asked.

"I'll tell her Newton added indigo for the music," Chen said. "Whether she changes the lesson is up to her."
