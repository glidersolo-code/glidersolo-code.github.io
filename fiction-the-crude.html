Part CCXXVIII: The Crude

Piece #1581: The Protein

Thomas reads the dog food bag. Guaranteed Analysis: Crude Protein (min) 26%. He reads this as: at least 26 percent of the food is protein — amino acids the dog can use for muscle, tissue repair, immune function. But "crude protein" is not protein. It is total nitrogen multiplied by 6.25, the Kjeldahl method — a conversion factor based on the assumption that all nitrogen in the food comes from protein and that protein is 16 percent nitrogen. The method counts nitrogen. Nitrogen comes from amino acids, but it also comes from non-protein nitrogen sources: melamine, urea, nucleic acids, ammonia compounds. The 2007 pet food recall — melamine-contaminated wheat gluten from Xuzhou Anying — exploited this directly. Melamine is 66.7 percent nitrogen by mass. Adding melamine to protein concentrate raised the crude protein number without adding a single amino acid. The number on the bag went up. The nutrition in the bag did not.

Piece #1582: The Guaranteed

The Guaranteed Analysis is regulated by AAFCO (Association of American Feed Control Officials) model regulations, adopted by state feed control agencies. It requires: Crude Protein (minimum), Crude Fat (minimum), Crude Fiber (maximum), Moisture (maximum). "Guaranteed" means the manufacturer certifies the product meets these minimums and maximums. It does not guarantee the product contains these amounts — only that it does not fall below the minimums or exceed the maximums. A food guaranteed at 26% minimum crude protein may contain 30% or 35%. The consumer reads the guaranteed analysis as a specification. It is a floor and ceiling. The distance between the guarantee and the actual content is invisible.

Piece #1583: The Comparison

Thomas compares two dog foods. Brand A: 26% crude protein, 12% crude fat. Brand B: 10% crude protein, 5% crude fat. Brand B appears inferior — less than half the protein. But Brand B is a wet food. Its moisture is 78%. Brand A's moisture is 10%. On a dry-matter basis — removing the water — Brand B contains approximately 45% protein (10 ÷ 0.22), and Brand A contains approximately 29% (26 ÷ 0.90). The "inferior" food has more protein per unit of actual food. The guaranteed analysis is reported as-fed — including moisture — which makes wet foods appear nutritionally weaker than dry foods. No label converts to dry-matter basis. The consumer must perform the arithmetic themselves, and no label tells them to.

Piece #1584: The Crude

"Crude" in crude protein, crude fat, and crude fiber does not mean unrefined in the colloquial sense. It means the analytical method is approximate. Crude fat: the fraction soluble in ether, which includes fats, waxes, and some pigments. Crude fiber: the residue after boiling in acid and alkali, which captures some plant cellulose but misses most soluble fiber and some insoluble fiber. Total dietary fiber — what the nutritionist would measure — is higher than crude fiber, sometimes by a factor of two or three. The consumer sees "Crude Fiber (max) 4%" and believes the food contains 4 percent fiber. The food may contain 8-12 percent total dietary fiber. The word "crude" warns that the method is imprecise, but the warning is embedded in the technical vocabulary, not in the consumer's reading.

Piece #1585: The Ingredient

The ingredient list says "Chicken, Corn Meal, Chicken By-Product Meal, Corn Gluten Meal." Chicken is listed first — the largest ingredient by weight. But chicken is 70 percent moisture. By dry weight, the chicken contributes perhaps 8 percent of the kibble. Corn Meal and Corn Gluten Meal, listed second and fourth, together may contribute more dry matter than the chicken listed first. The ingredient list is sorted by pre-processing weight, which gives wet ingredients a positional advantage they lose in the final product. The consumer reads the list as a recipe. The list is a pre-cooking weight ranking. The food the dog eats is not the list the consumer reads.
