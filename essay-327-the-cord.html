The Cord

The extension cord package prints two numbers: 15 amps and 100 feet. The consumer reads them as independent claims. This cord handles 15 amps. This cord reaches 100 feet. The consumer plugs a 1,500-watt space heater into the far end.

The numbers are not independent. They are two axes of a three-variable equation. The third variable — wire gauge — determines where the ampacity and the length intersect safely. A 16-gauge cord at 25 feet handles 13 amps. The same 16-gauge cord at 100 feet handles 7 amps before the voltage drop becomes significant and the heat generation becomes dangerous.

The physics: every foot of copper has resistance. At 12.5 amps (the draw of a standard 1,500-watt heater), 200 feet of 16-gauge copper (100 feet out, 100 feet back) drops approximately 20 percent of the voltage. The heater receives 96 volts instead of 120. Power scales with the square of voltage: 96 divided by 120, squared, equals 0.64. The heater delivers 960 watts — 64 percent of its rating. The missing 540 watts become heat in the cord.

The cord is warm. Under a rug, the warmth accumulates because the rug traps it. Coiled behind furniture, each loop heats the adjacent loop. The insulation rating assumed free air circulation. The consumer who runs the cord under a carpet has changed the thermal equation without changing the electrical load. Extension cords cause 3,300 home fires annually, 50 deaths, 270 injuries.

The breaker does not intervene. The breaker protects the 14-gauge house wiring at 15 amps. The 16-gauge extension cord drawing 12.5 amps is within the breaker's threshold. The breaker allows it. The cord has less capacity than the house wiring it extends, but the protection system guards the house wiring, not the cord. The cord occupies the gap between the breaker's jurisdiction and the appliance's demand.

The consumer who checks the amp rating against the heater's draw has performed the recommended safety calculation. The number fits. The result is correct and incomplete — because the calculation does not include length. The gauge-length-ampacity relationship is a curve, not a number. The package prints a point on the curve — the amp rating at some unspecified short length — and the consumer supplies the missing variable by unspooling the cord across the house. The third variable is the one the consumer controls and the label does not print.
