Part CXCVI: The Signal

Piece #1421: The Name

A brand consultancy in 1999 is hired to replace the name "IEEE 802.11b Direct Sequence." They present ten candidates. The committee chooses Wi-Fi because it echoes Hi-Fi — high fidelity, quality audio, consumer warmth. The name has no technical content. It is not an acronym. It means nothing.

Board members, uncomfortable with meaninglessness, invent a tagline: "The Standard for Wireless Fidelity." The tagline is a costume. When professional marketers join the organization, they remove it. By then the explanation has become the meaning. Consumers say "Wireless Fidelity" the way they say "32 degrees" — as though the words describe a fact rather than a decision.

Thomas thought about the names of his instruments. "Patient Health Questionnaire" — a name that performed neutrality the way Wi-Fi performed fidelity. A questionnaire for patients about their health. The name did not say: nine questions about two weeks of symptoms, scored 0-3, summed to an integer, compared to a threshold set by a committee. The name said: health. The name performed more than the instrument measured.

Piece #1422: The Gap

The IEEE 802.11 amendments run sequentially: a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, j, k, n, p, r, s, u, v, w, y, z, aa, ac, ad, ax, be. The consumer sees a, b, g, n, ac, ax — the amendments that increase speed — and wonders why the letters jump. They jump because the letters between them address security, quality of service, roaming, mesh networking, spectrum management. Twenty-odd amendments the consumer never encounters, occupying the alphabet the consumer cannot see.

Thomas's instruments had similar gaps. The DSM moved from DSM-III to DSM-III-R to DSM-IV to DSM-IV-TR to DSM-5. The numerals suggest a simple sequence. Between them were revisions, text revisions, field trials, committee votes, political negotiations between psychiatric factions — the non-consumer-facing amendments of diagnostic classification. The patient saw the number. The number contained the sequence. The sequence did not contain the process.

The gap is not a gap. It is a filter. The format shows what it considers relevant and hides what it does not.

Piece #1423: The Deployment

Both 802.11a and 802.11b were ratified on September 16, 1999. The letter "a" came first. The product "b" reached the market first — Apple's iBook G3 shipped with 802.11b in July 1999, four months before ratification. 802.11a products did not arrive until late 2001. The alphabet said one thing. The market said another.

Thomas found the same inversion. The Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (1960) predated the PHQ-9 (1999) by four decades. In clinical practice, the PHQ-9 was encountered first — primary care physicians learned it before they learned the Hamilton. In the literature, the Hamilton came first. Alphabetical order, chronological order, and deployment order were three different sequences. The consumer — the resident learning depression screening — experienced whichever order the training presented. The instruments had no inherent order. They had contingent arrivals.

The letter does not tell you when the thing arrived. The letter tells you when the committee started working on it. These are not the same event.

Piece #1424: The Equivalence

A consumer buys a router labeled "Wi-Fi 6." Another consumer buys an enterprise access point labeled "Wi-Fi 6." The first device has two spatial streams, costs fifty dollars, and handles twenty devices. The second has eight spatial streams, costs twelve hundred dollars, and handles a hundred. The generation number performs equivalence. The hardware does not.

Thomas saw the same operation in screening instruments. Two clinics both "use the PHQ-9." One clinic administers it on paper in a waiting room with no follow-up. The other integrates it into a clinical workflow with trained staff, standardized administration, and a response protocol triggered by specific scores. Both report PHQ-9 scores. Both claim to screen for depression. The instrument is the same. The implementation is not. The name performs equivalence across contexts that share nothing but the name.

The generation number is a category. Categories erase variation by design — that is what categories do. The question is not whether the variation exists. The question is whether the category discloses it.

Piece #1425: The Speed

Wi-Fi 5 advertises "up to 1,300 Mbps." Real-world throughput: 200 to 400 Mbps. The gap — 50 to 85 percent — is caused by protocol overhead, physical obstacles, interference, shared bandwidth, and the limitation that most client devices have two-antenna radios regardless of the access point's capability. The speed on the box describes the theoretical maximum of the best hardware under ideal conditions that no consumer occupies.

Thomas remembered the treatment response rates. "70% of patients respond to SSRIs." The number came from clinical trials — selected populations, controlled conditions, defined timeframes, specific definitions of "response" (usually 50% reduction in HAM-D score). In general practice, with unselected patients, varied adherence, and no protocol enforcement, the response rate was lower. The number on the label described the theoretical maximum under conditions no typical patient occupied.

"Up to" is the format's confession. It admits the number is a ceiling. The admission is in the smallest type. The number is in the largest. The consumer reads the number. The qualifier reads itself.

The format is the signal.
