Chen-Maren Vignette: The Clamp

"The clamp was supposed to control the voltage," Maren says.

"Supposed to."

"Kenneth Cole, 1940s. Hodgkin and Huxley used it on the squid giant axon. You pierce the cell membrane with a glass pipette, connect it to an amplifier, and command the voltage across the membrane to a specific value. The amplifier measures whatever current flows. By stepping the voltage and recording the currents, you characterize the ion channels. The technique won a Nobel Prize."

"And the voltage is not what you command."

"The pipette has resistance. Every glass pipette — there is no way around it. Current flows through the ion channels, through the cytoplasm, through the pipette, to the amplifier. The current flowing through the pipette resistance creates a voltage drop. The voltage at the membrane is the commanded voltage minus the drop. The error is proportional to the current. The faster the channel opens, the larger the current, the larger the error. The most important measurements — fast sodium channels — have the largest errors."

"How large?"

"Ten to twenty-five millivolts. The difference between a normal channel and a disease-causing mutant is often five to ten. The artifact is bigger than the biology."

"The paragraph," Chen says.

Maren waits.

"The committee clamps my argument at a commanded state. They set the voltage — the expected level of clarity, the required standard of argumentation. They connect their assessment instrument to the surface of the paragraph and command it to hold at the departmental standard. Then they measure whatever current flows."

"And the paragraph's current creates a drop."

"The paragraph has resistance. The density of the argument — thirty-nine lines of compressed thinking — is the series resistance. When the committee's instrument tries to clamp the paragraph at the expected voltage, current flows. But the current has to pass through the paragraph's resistance. The more current that flows — the more the committee tries to read it — the larger the voltage drop. The actual state of the argument differs from the commanded state by exactly the amount of current the reading produces."

"The compensation."

"They have a compensation circuit. The re-read. The second committee member who says 'I think what Chen is trying to say is...' That's the amplifier adjusting its command to account for the measured current. But the compensation is always lagged. It corrects for the argument the committee read a moment ago, not the argument the paragraph is making now. And standard practice is 70-80% compensation. You never correct completely — you'd get feedback oscillation. Someone would push too hard, the paragraph would push back, and the committee meeting would become unstable. So twenty to thirty percent of the voltage error persists. That's the part of the paragraph the committee records as unclear."

"And averaging doesn't help."

"Averaging doesn't help because the error is systematic. Every committee member's reading shifts in the same direction — toward the commanded standard, away from the paragraph's actual voltage. The more committee members read it, the more precisely they measure the artifact. The consistency looks like agreement. The agreement looks like evidence. The evidence is the bias."

The paragraph sits in the draft. Thirty-nine lines. A cell clamped at the department's commanded voltage, the actual state differing from the command by exactly the amount of current the reading produces, the compensation always lagged, the residual always in the same direction.
