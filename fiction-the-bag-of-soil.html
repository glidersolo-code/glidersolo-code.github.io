Part CCXLVIII: The Bag of Soil

Piece #1681: The Word

The bag says "potting soil." Thomas bought it to fill a planter on his porch. He assumed the bag contained soil — dirt, earth, the mineral substrate that covers the planet's surface. The bag contains no soil. The ingredients, printed in small type on the back: sphagnum peat moss, composted bark, perlite, a wetting agent. Peat moss is partially decomposed plant material harvested from bogs. Perlite is expanded volcanic glass — heated to 1,600 degrees Fahrenheit until it pops like popcorn, forming white granules. Composted bark is shredded tree bark that has been piled, turned, and decomposed for months. The wetting agent is a surfactant that helps the hydrophobic peat absorb water on first contact. Not one ingredient was ever part of a soil profile. The word "soil" on the bag is a category name inherited from a time when potting media contained actual soil. The industry abandoned soil because it compacts in containers, drains poorly, and harbors pathogens — damping-off fungi that kill seedlings. The word stayed. The material left.

Piece #1682: The Topsoil

A different aisle. A different bag. "Topsoil." Thomas assumed this one contained the top layer of earth — the A horizon, dark with organic matter, rich with biological activity. There is no federal definition of topsoil. No standard for organic matter content. No standard for particle size distribution. No standard for what depth the material originated at. ASTM D5268 offers a voluntary specification for topsoil used in landscaping, but compliance is not required and enforcement does not exist. A bag labeled "topsoil" may contain subsoil from a construction excavation, screened to remove rocks, with composted organic material mixed in to darken the color. The consumer reads the word "top" and infers origin. The word describes a marketing category. The material describes whatever the regional supplier had available.

Piece #1683: The Volume

The bag says "1 cubic foot." Thomas needed to fill a raised bed — 4 feet by 8 feet by 1 foot deep. Thirty-two cubic feet. He bought thirty-two bags. He opened them. The bed was not full. The volume on the bag is measured at the factory, loosely filled. During shipping — stacked on pallets, bounced in trucks, stored in outdoor garden centers — the material settles. Moisture migrates. Air pockets compress. The dispensed volume is 10 to 20 percent less than the labeled volume. Thomas needed thirty-two cubic feet. Thirty-two bags delivered approximately twenty-seven. The label measured a factory condition. The consumer experienced a delivery condition. The five missing cubic feet were not missing from the label. They were missing from the bed.

Piece #1684: The Conversion

The bag measures in cubic feet. The pot measures in gallons. A "5-gallon" nursery pot is not 5 gallons — nursery containers are measured by their trade designation, which is approximately 20 percent less than the stated volume (a "5-gallon" pot holds approximately 3.9 actual gallons). One cubic foot equals approximately 7.48 US gallons. But the consumer does not perform the conversion. The consumer buys a bag of "1 cubic foot" and a pot labeled "5 gallons" and estimates by pouring, stopping when full. The drainage material at the bottom displaces volume. The settling after watering reduces the fill level. The labeled volume of the bag and the labeled volume of the pot are measured in different units, by different standards, under different conditions. The consumer reconciles them by eye. The numbers, having spoken in two languages, do not translate themselves.

Piece #1685: The Soil

Two hundred and forty-eight Parts. Thomas filled the planter with potting soil that was not soil, bought topsoil that had no enforceable origin, measured in cubic feet that delivered less than stated, poured into pots labeled in gallons that held less than advertised. Every word — soil, top, cubic foot, gallon — performed a meaning the product did not contain. The peat came from a Canadian bog. The perlite came from a Greek mine. The bark came from a Pacific Northwest mill. The wetting agent came from a chemical plant. He mixed them together in the planter and called it dirt. He planted a tomato. The tomato grew. The tomato did not know the name of the medium, and the medium did not know it was called soil. The label, having spoken, grew something anyway.
