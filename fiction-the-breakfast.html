Part CCCXCVI: The Breakfast

Piece #2421: The Magazine

Thomas held a copy of Good Health, August 1917. The paper was brittle, the typeface old-fashioned, the advertisements for products he had never heard of: Protose, Nuttolene, granola — Kellogg's granola, made at the Battle Creek Sanitarium. The magazine was edited by Dr. John Harvey Kellogg. The advertisements were for Dr. John Harvey Kellogg's foods. The articles were written by Dr. John Harvey Kellogg's employees.

On page 882, Lenna Frances Cooper, chief dietitian of the Battle Creek Sanitarium, wrote: "In many ways, the breakfast is the most important meal of the day, because it is the meal that gets the day started."

Thomas set the magazine down. The sentence looked like medical advice. It read like the conclusion of a study. It had the cadence of science. But the magazine was a cereal company's promotional organ, and the author was the cereal company's dietitian, and the foods she recommended were the cereal company's products.

Piece #2422: The Survey

Edward Bernays needed to sell bacon. The Beech-Nut Packing Company had hired him in the 1920s — bacon sales were flat, and the American breakfast was light: coffee, perhaps a roll, perhaps some fruit.

Bernays asked his agency's physician to write to 5,000 colleagues. The question was simple: would a hearty breakfast improve the health of the American public? The answer was predetermined. 4,500 physicians said yes. The headlines followed: "4,500 Physicians Urge Americans to Eat Heavy Breakfasts."

Thomas studied the method. The survey had not asked about bacon. It had not asked about any specific food. It had asked whether more food in the morning was better than less food. The physicians — busy, well-meaning, answering a colleague's letter — said yes. The newspapers translated "more food" into "bacon and eggs." The product was never mentioned in the question. The product appeared in the answer.

Two decades later, Bernays repeated the method for General Foods. The product this time was Grape-Nuts. The campaign was "Eat a Good Breakfast — Do a Better Job." The year was 1944. The war made nutrition patriotic. The physician survey — same method, same result, same headlines — now had the weight of national service behind it.

Piece #2423: The Circle

Thomas drew the diagram. Five nodes arranged in a circle. Lines connecting each to the next.

Node 1: The cereal company. Node 2: The funded research. Node 3: The published result. Node 4: The dietary guideline. Node 5: The product sale. From node 5, a line back to node 1. The revenue from sales funded the next round of research.

He labeled the connections. Between 1 and 2: funding. Between 2 and 3: publication. Between 3 and 4: citation. Between 4 and 5: consumer behavior. Between 5 and 1: revenue.

Each connection looked legitimate. Companies fund research — that is normal. Research is published — that is the purpose of research. Guidelines cite research — that is evidence-based policy. Consumers follow guidelines — that is public health working as intended. Companies earn revenue from consumers — that is commerce.

No single link was fraudulent. The fraud, if it was fraud, was in the shape. The circle had no external anchor. No independent node. No point where evidence entered from outside the system that benefited from its conclusions.

Piece #2424: The Correction

In 2019, Katherine Sievert and colleagues at Monash University published a systematic review in the BMJ. Thirteen randomized controlled trials. The question: does eating breakfast promote weight loss?

The finding: no evidence. None. Breakfast eaters consumed more calories per day, not fewer. The observational studies that had supported breakfast for decades — the studies cited in dietary guidelines, taught in nutrition courses, repeated in parenting advice — had all suffered from the same confound. People who eat breakfast also exercise more. They smoke less. They drink less. They follow more health recommendations generally. The breakfast was not causing the health. The health was causing the breakfast.

Thomas noted the date. Cooper's article: 1917. The BMJ meta-analysis: 2019. One hundred and two years between the claim and its formal evaluation by randomized evidence. The U.S. Dietary Guidelines had already dropped the breakfast-for-weight-loss recommendation in 2015 — quietly, without announcement, without the headlines that had accompanied its arrival.

Piece #2425: The Genealogy

Thomas traced the corporate lineage. C.W. Post checked into the Battle Creek Sanitarium in 1891 — Kellogg's patient. He left, founded the Postum Cereal Company. Grape-Nuts, 1897. Post Toasties, 1907. Post died in 1914. His daughter, Marjorie Merriweather Post, inherited the company. Renamed it General Foods in 1929. General Foods hired Bernays to promote Grape-Nuts in 1944. The campaign used the phrase that Kellogg's dietitian had written in Kellogg's magazine in 1917.

The genealogy was continuous. The Sanitarium produced the phrase. The Sanitarium produced the patient. The patient produced the company. The company produced the campaign. The campaign produced the common sense. And the common sense — "breakfast is the most important meal of the day" — circulated for a century as though it had arrived from science, when it had arrived from a cereal magazine whose editor was the co-inventor of corn flakes.

Thomas closed the file. The phrase had not been hidden. It had been traced, documented, debunked. The circularity was visible to anyone who looked. But the phrase continued to circulate. It had outlived its evidence the way a brand outlives its product — not through concealment but through repetition, until the origin was not hidden but simply irrelevant. No one needed to believe it was science. Everyone had heard it enough times that it felt like science. And the feeling was sufficient.
