The Wall

The number on the bag of insulation is R-19. The number of the wall is not.

R-value measures thermal resistance — the opposition to heat flow through a material. The unit is ft²·°F·h/BTU. The test is ASTM C518: a sample of insulation clamped between two plates in a laboratory, one hot, one cold, at a mean temperature of 75°F. The measurement is precise. The measurement is also incomplete, because it measures the insulation, not the wall the insulation goes into.

A 2×6 wood-framed wall with R-19 fiberglass batts in the cavities does not perform at R-19. The studs — spaced 16 inches on center, occupying roughly 10-12% of the wall area — are wood, and wood conducts heat at approximately R-1.25 per inch. Heat flows preferentially through the studs because they offer less resistance. This thermal bridging reduces the whole-wall R-value to approximately R-15 to R-17. The insulation between the studs works exactly as tested. The studs around it do not.

Upgrading from R-13 to R-19 — filling a deeper cavity with more insulation — adds approximately R-1.1 of effective whole-wall performance. Not R-6. The difference between the nominal improvement and the effective improvement is the thermal bridge. The studs do not change when the insulation changes. They conduct the same heat regardless of what fills the space between them.

Everett C. Shuman proposed R-value in 1945 at Penn State's Building Research Institute. The concept was designed for consumer clarity: higher numbers mean better performance, and R-values add in series. Both properties are true for the insulation material. Neither is fully true for the wall assembly. The FTC codified the concept into regulation in 1979 — the R-value Rule, 16 CFR Part 460 — requiring standardized labeling on all home insulation. The regulation mandates that R-value refer to the material, not the assembly. The consumer reads it as the assembly anyway.

Closed-cell foam insulation introduces another gap between label and performance. Polyisocyanurate and extruded polystyrene are manufactured with blowing agents — gases trapped in the foam cells that provide high initial R-values. Over time, the blowing agents escape through the cell walls and are replaced by air. The R-value drops permanently. This thermal drift was unacknowledged until the late 1990s, when Oak Ridge National Laboratory developed the LTTR methodology — Long-Term Thermal Resistance — requiring manufacturers to report aged rather than initial values. The insulation that performs at R-7 per inch on the day it leaves the factory may perform at R-5.5 per inch five years later. The label captures one moment in a declining curve.

Europe uses U-value — the reciprocal of R-value — where lower numbers mean less heat loss. U-value describes the heat that escapes. R-value describes the heat that doesn't. The choice is not trivial. A consumer reading U-value is reading loss. A consumer reading R-value is reading resistance — a positive quality, an achievement, something the material does. The format determines whether the number sounds like a problem or a solution. Both describe the same physics. One makes the wall sound protective. The other makes the wall sound leaky.

The number on the bag of insulation measures a sample between two plates in a laboratory in conditions the wall will never replicate. The studs are not in the lab. The air films are not in the lab. The moisture is not in the lab. The fasteners, the sheathing, the cladding, the wind — none of it is in the lab. The number is real. The wall is not the lab.
