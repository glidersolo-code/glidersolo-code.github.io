Chen-Maren: The Energy Label

They are standing in the appliance section at Lowe's, looking at refrigerators. Chen has pulled the yellow EnergyGuide label toward him and is reading it carefully.

"Estimated yearly energy cost: sixty-seven dollars."

"That seems low," Maren says. "What's our electric bill?"

"Last month was a hundred and forty, but that's everything."

"What's the rate?"

Chen checks his phone. "We pay twenty-two cents a kilowatt-hour."

He looks at the label again. The estimated yearly energy cost uses $0.16 per kilowatt-hour — the national average. He does the math. "At our rate it would be ninety-two dollars, not sixty-seven."

"So the label is wrong."

"The label uses a national average. Somebody in Louisiana with ten-cent power would pay forty-two. We'd pay ninety-two. The label says sixty-seven because that's the national middle."

"Who lives in the national middle?"

"Nobody."

Maren looks at the comparison bar. The arrow sits slightly left of center — better than average among similar models. "What about this bar?"

"That's the most useful part, actually. It compares this refrigerator to other refrigerators tested the same way. The comparison is real."

"But the dollar number isn't."

"The dollar number is real for a hypothetical consumer who pays the national average rate and uses the refrigerator the way the DOE test procedure assumes." He reads the fine print. "Estimated based on a national average electricity cost of sixteen cents per kWh."

Maren opens the refrigerator door, looks inside, closes it. "So the label tells us what it would cost to run this refrigerator in a lab, at a price nobody pays, in a house that doesn't exist."

"But it tells us that accurately."

"Very helpful." She points to the Energy Star logo. "And that?"

"That means it passed a threshold. This one uses 450 kilowatt-hours. The one next to it without the star uses 460. Ten kilowatt-hours difference. About a dollar sixty at the national average."

"A dollar sixty is the difference between efficient and not efficient."

"The star is a binary on top of a gradient."

Maren looks at both refrigerators — the starred and the unstarred. Same brand. Same capacity. Same stainless finish. One dollar and sixty cents apart in a test lab, a lifetime apart on the label.

"We'll take the one with the star," she says. "I know it doesn't mean what I think it means. But I can't unsee it."
