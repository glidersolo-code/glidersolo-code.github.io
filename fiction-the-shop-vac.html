Fiction

Part CCLXXI: The Shop Vac

Piece #1796: The Inrush

Six point five peak horsepower. The number is on the front of the box in the largest type. It refers to a measurement that lasts eight to twelve milliseconds — the inrush current when the motor is first energized and the rotor is stationary. In that fraction of a second, the motor draws far more current than it will ever draw again during operation. The peak is the startup. The startup is the measurement. The measurement is the marketing. After the first twelfth of a second, the motor settles to its operating draw: 12 amps on a 15-amp household circuit. The continuous mechanical output is approximately 1.5 to 2 horsepower. The box says 6.5 because 6.5 happened once, for twelve milliseconds, before the vacuum began doing anything.

Piece #1797: The Outlet

A standard household outlet provides 15 amps at 120 volts. Fifteen times 120 equals 1,800 watts. One mechanical horsepower equals 746 watts. The outlet can deliver, at maximum, 2.4 horsepower — and the motor converts electricity to mechanical work at 50 to 65 percent efficiency, yielding roughly 1.2 to 1.5 HP at the impeller. No shop vacuum plugged into a standard outlet can sustain 6.5 horsepower. The physics of the outlet forbids it. The consumer who reads the box is reading a number that the outlet in their garage cannot physically provide for more than a hundredth of a second. The constraint is not the motor. The constraint is the wall.

Piece #1798: The Lawsuit

An independent laboratory tested the vacuums. A model marketed as "6.5 Peak HP" sustained 1.93 HP — thirty percent of the labeled number. A "2.5 Peak HP" model sustained 1.29 HP — fifty-two percent. Six consolidated class action lawsuits alleged that Shop-Vac Corporation and Lowe's Home Centers had misrepresented the horsepower ratings. The settlement, reached in 2016, required modified marketing language and extended motor warranties. The company did not admit wrongdoing. The marketing changed. The box still says "peak." The word "peak" technically discloses that the number is not sustained. The consumer reads the integer. The integer is the comparison. The qualifier is the footnote.

Piece #1799: The Drum

The same class action found that advertised tank capacity exceeded actual usable volume by approximately 36 percent. A "16-gallon" drum holds less than 16 gallons because the drum tapers toward the motor housing and the filter assembly occupies internal space. Two numbers on the box — peak horsepower and tank capacity — and both numbers describe the container at its theoretical maximum rather than the appliance at its operating reality. The consumer carries home a box with two measurements. Neither measurement describes what the consumer will experience when the vacuum is plugged in and turned on and the sawdust begins to move.

Piece #1800: The Thousandth

Two hundred and seventy-one Parts. One thousand eight hundred pieces. The shop vacuum box presents a twelve-millisecond measurement as the machine's defining specification. The consumer compares 6.5 to 5.0 and concludes one machine is thirty percent more powerful. Both machines plug into the same outlet. Both outlets deliver the same 1,800 watts. Both motors convert those watts at similar efficiency. The difference between 6.5 and 5.0 peak HP is a difference in inrush — a surge that dissipates before the nozzle touches the floor. Thomas vacuumed the workshop. The sawdust moved at the speed the outlet permitted, not the speed the box promised. The box described the machine's first hundredth of a second. The workshop needed the next forty-five minutes. The number and the need occupied different timescales entirely.
