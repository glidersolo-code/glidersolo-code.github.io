Part CC: The Stone

Piece #1441: The Clean Break

The GIA color scale starts at D. Previous systems — A-B-C, I-II-III, Roman numerals — had degraded through inconsistent use. Different dealers assigned different grades to the same stones. The vocabulary had been corrupted by the market it served. GIA started at D to signal a new regime. The letter performs a fresh beginning. The consumer wonders why the alphabet is incomplete. The answer is that the missing letters had already failed.

Thomas saw similar resets. The DSM-III (1980) was a clean break from the DSM-II — abandoning psychoanalytic etiology for descriptive criteria, replacing narrative with checklist. The numbering continued (II to III), but the system was discontinuous. The new version did not extend the old. It replaced it. The Roman numeral performed continuity. The content performed rupture.

Clean breaks in nomenclature are always responses to corrupted predecessors. The new system starts where the old one failed. But the consumer, who never saw the corruption, experiences the gap as arbitrary. Why D? Why III? The answer is always: because what came before was ruined.

Piece #1442: The Reversal

Below Z on the GIA scale, body color is a defect — less yellow is better. Beyond Z, color becomes a treasure — vivid yellow, deep pink, saturated blue. The same physical property reverses its valuation at a market threshold. Below the threshold, color subtracts value. Above it, color multiplies it.

Thomas found reversals in clinical measurement. Mild depression (PHQ-9 5-9) was monitored. Moderate depression (10-14) was treated. The same score trajectory — moving from 9 to 11 — crossed a threshold where the clinical response reversed from watchful waiting to pharmaceutical intervention. Two points on a continuous scale, the same symptom burden, opposite clinical actions. The threshold was not in the patient. It was in the protocol.

Reversals are the clearest evidence that the format is not measuring a property of the object. A property of the object does not reverse its meaning at a boundary. Only a convention does.

Piece #1443: The Magnification

Clarity is assessed at ten-times magnification. An inclusion invisible at 10× but visible at 20× does not affect the grade. The boundary of what counts as a flaw is set by the power of the instrument, not by the existence of the feature.

Thomas thought about screening thresholds. The PHQ-9's cutoff of 10 for "moderate depression" was a magnification — a resolution that determined what counted as a case. At a cutoff of 8, more patients would be classified as depressed. At a cutoff of 12, fewer. The number did not change the patients. It changed what the instrument could see. Like the 10× loupe, the cutoff was chosen by committee, for practical reasons, and then treated as a natural boundary.

The magnification and the cutoff share a structure: both set the resolution at which reality is observed, both were chosen by convention, and both have become invisible. The grader uses 10× because that is the standard. The clinician uses 10 because that is the cutoff. Neither asks why.

Piece #1444: The Cliff

The Rapaport price list publishes wholesale diamond prices per carat by color and clarity. At each grade boundary — G to H color, SI1 to SI2 clarity, 0.99 to 1.00 carats — the price jumps. The visual difference between G and H is imperceptible to most consumers. The price difference is 10 to 15 percent. The grade boundary creates a financial cliff where the physical property creates a slope.

Thomas saw price cliffs in pharmaceutical pricing. A drug that crossed the threshold from "specialty" to "non-specialty" — often defined by annual cost — moved from one formulary tier to another, changing the patient's copay dramatically. The same molecule, the same dose, the same disease. The price changed because the category changed. The category changed because the cost crossed a threshold. The threshold was in the formulary, not the pharmacology.

Cliffs are what happen when continuous properties are graded into categories and then priced by category. The format — the grade, the tier — creates discontinuities that the underlying reality does not contain. The diamond does not become worse at H. The drug does not become more expensive intrinsically at $10,001. The cliff is in the map, not the territory.

Piece #1445: The Weight

One carat is 200 milligrams, standardized in 1907. The unit descends from the carob seed — believed, incorrectly, to have a uniform weight. The consumer reads carat as size. It is weight. A well-cut 0.90-carat diamond can appear larger than a poorly-cut 1.00-carat because the cut determines how weight distributes across depth and surface area. The market prices by weight. The consumer buys by appearance. The unit mediates between them without disclosing the gap.

Thomas's scores were weights, not sizes. A PHQ-9 of 15 was the weight of symptom endorsement — nine items, scored 0-3, summed. But a 15 composed of high scores on sleep and appetite and low scores on suicidal ideation was not the same clinical size as a 15 composed of low scores on sleep and appetite and high scores on suicidal ideation. The sum was the same. The distribution was different. The weight did not disclose the shape.

Weight and size are not the same property. Scores and severity are not the same property. The format that reports one while the consumer reads the other is not lying. It is performing a substitution — offering the measurable in place of the meaningful, and trusting the consumer not to notice the difference.

The format is the stone.
