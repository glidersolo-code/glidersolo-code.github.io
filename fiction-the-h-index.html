Part CDLV: The Index

Piece #2716: The Integer

Thomas opens a scientist's Google Scholar profile. The number is there, beside the name: h-index 45. He knows what this means. Forty-five papers have each been cited at least forty-five times. The formula is simple — simpler than almost any formula in science. And the formula produces an integer. Not a ratio, not a percentage, not a decimal. An integer. Thomas notices that integers feel different from other numbers. An integer suggests that something was counted. Counted things feel factual. "She has an h-index of 45" sounds like a measurement. "She published 45 papers with at least 45 citations" sounds like a description. The integer compresses the description into a measurement. The compression is the format. The format is the authority.

Piece #2717: The Ratchet

Thomas checks the profile of a scientist who retired in 2014. The h-index reads 52. He checks the same profile six months later. The h-index reads 53. The scientist has not published in twelve years. The number rose anyway, because other scientists continued to cite the old papers. Thomas considers: the h-index cannot decrease. It is a ratchet — it clicks forward and never clicks back. A paper once above the threshold stays counted. A career once measured stays measured at its peak or higher. The number performs a present-tense assessment. The number contains only the past. The direction of the career — ascending, plateauing, ended — is invisible in the integer, because the integer can only grow. Thomas wonders what it means to carry an assessment that cannot decline. It means the assessment has no tense. It looks like now. It is always then.

Piece #2718: The Extremes the Index Cannot See

Author A has 8 publications cited a total of 338 times. Author B has 8 publications cited a total of 28 times. Both have h=5. Thomas stares at this. A twelve-to-one difference in total impact, invisible. Above the h-threshold, additional citations do not register — a paper cited 5,000 times contributes identically to one cited 6 times, so long as both exceed h. Below the threshold, papers do not exist. Thomas calculates Albert Einstein's h-index: approximately 4. A prolific author of a hundred competent reviews, each cited a hundred times, scores h=100. The index ranks the competent reviewer twenty-five times higher than the physicist who reshaped the universe. The formula is too simple to see its own blindness. The blindness is at both extremes — the bottom and the top — and the integer sits in the middle, seeing neither.

Piece #2719: The Field the Integer Dissolves

A hiring committee reviews candidates from three departments. The physicist has h=38. The mathematician has h=12. The literary scholar has h=4. The committee compares the numbers. The numbers are identical in format. The fields are not identical in anything. In physics, the top 1% threshold for citations is over 2,000. In mathematics, it might be 150. A mathematician with h=12 may be among the finest in the generation. A physicist with h=38 may be solidly mid-career. The integer performs commensurability — it makes the numbers comparable because they share a format. But sharing a format is not sharing a scale. Thomas watches the committee discuss the numbers as though they are discussing the careers. They are discussing the numbers. The careers are in the papers. The papers are in the library. The committee is in a room with a spreadsheet.

Piece #2720: The Reform That Proves the Persistence

In 2013, the San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment urged institutions to stop using citation metrics in hiring. In 2015, the Leiden Manifesto articulated ten principles for responsible metrics use. In 2022, Utrecht University formally dropped impact factors from all hiring and promotion decisions. Thomas reads about China's 2020 reform — the "breaking the Five Onlys" — dismantling an entire evaluation infrastructure built on SCI metrics. The reforms keep coming. The h-index remains on every Google Scholar profile, computed automatically, consultable in seconds. Thomas understands. The reforms address the institution. They do not address the format. The format is a single integer next to a name. The integer is easier to read than the papers. As long as reading the integer is easier than reading the papers, the integer will be read instead of the papers. The reform proves the persistence because the reform targets the wrong thing. It targets the policy. The persistence lives in the format.
