The Filter

Essay 157

Every sound level meter sold worldwide contains a frequency-weighting filter called A-weighting. The filter is specified in IEC 61672-1, the international standard for electroacoustic instruments. Before sound pressure reaches the display, it passes through an electronic network that attenuates low-frequency energy and slightly boosts mid-frequency energy, approximating the frequency response of the human ear at quiet listening levels. At 1000 Hz, the filter is transparent — zero correction. At 500 Hz, it subtracts 3.2 dB. At 100 Hz, it subtracts 19.1 dB. At 50 Hz, it subtracts 30.3 dB. At 10 Hz, it subtracts 70.4 dB. The output is reported in dBA — decibels, A-weighted. The A is the filter. The number after the A is what survives the filter.

The filter's shape traces the inverse of the 40-phon equal-loudness contour — the curve of constant perceived loudness at the quietest level Fletcher and Munson measured in their 1933 experiments at Bell Telephone Laboratories. Their method: present a 1000-Hz reference tone and a comparison tone at another frequency, both through headphones; ask the subject to adjust the comparison until both sound equally loud; plot the physical intensity required at each frequency to achieve subjective equality. The resulting curves revealed that the ear requires far more physical energy at low frequencies to perceive the same loudness. At 50 Hz and 40 phons, the ear needs roughly 30 dB more intensity than at 1000 Hz. The A-weighting filter applies this finding in reverse: it removes 30.3 dB from 50-Hz sound before displaying the result, on the logic that a sound the ear perceives poorly matters proportionally less.

The logic was reasonable for its original purpose — approximating perceived loudness of steady-state sounds at low levels. But A-weighting migrated. It became the universal metric for noise exposure assessment, occupational health regulation, environmental noise ordinance, and community noise monitoring. OSHA 1910.95 specifies an 8-hour time-weighted average of 85 dBA as the permissible exposure limit. EU Directive 2003/10/EC sets action values at 80 and 85 dBA. National noise ordinances worldwide express residential limits in dBA. Airport noise contours are mapped in dBA. Traffic noise models output dBA. The filter designed for perceived loudness at 40 phons became the filter through which all noise-related harm is evaluated — including harm at frequencies the filter was specifically designed to suppress.

The problem sharpened in 2003 when Yoichi Suzuki and Hisashi Takeshima at the Research Institute of Electrical Communication, Tohoku University, published the revised equal-loudness contours that became ISO 226:2003, in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America. Their work synthesized data from research groups across Japan, Germany, Denmark, the UK, and the United States. The finding: the Robinson-Dadson curves from 1956 — which had been the international standard for 47 years — were significantly wrong at low frequencies, deviating by 10 to 15 dB from the new consensus. The original Fletcher-Munson curves were closer to correct than the Robinson-Dadson replacement. But the deeper finding was that all equal-loudness contours change shape with level: at higher sound levels, the curves flatten, meaning the ear's relative insensitivity to low frequencies diminishes. A-weighting, frozen at the 40-phon shape, applies maximum low-frequency attenuation regardless of the actual sound level being measured. A factory floor at 90 dB is filtered as though the listener were hearing it at 40 phons. The perception model underlying the filter does not match the perception at the measurement level.

Juhani Parmanen, publishing in Applied Acoustics in 2007, quantified the underestimate. For broadband low-frequency noise at levels typical of environmental exposure (65-70 dB linear), A-weighting underestimates perceived loudness and annoyance by approximately 3 to 6 dB. The underestimate increases with level because the equal-loudness contours flatten while A-weighting does not. At industrial noise levels, the gap is larger still. And annoyance is only part of the problem. Low-frequency noise below 200 Hz produces physiological effects — cardiovascular stress, cognitive impairment, sleep disruption — at intensities that A-weighting attenuates below regulatory thresholds.

The wind turbine literature made the gap concrete. Wind turbines produce characteristic noise concentrated at low frequencies and infrasound. At 10 Hz, A-weighting removes 70.4 dB. At a residence 500 meters from a turbine, the unweighted sound pressure at 10 Hz might be 55 dB — audible, perceptible as pressure fluctuation, documented as a source of complaints. After A-weighting: below the noise floor. Invisible. The IEA Wind Task 39 fact sheet from 2022 states it plainly: the A-weighting filter "severely attenuates low-frequency signals (the primary frequency range of most community noise complaints) and essentially eliminates acoustic signals below 20 Hz where infrasound is located." The turbine meets the dBA limit. Residents report headaches, sleep loss, and pressure sensations. Both statements are true simultaneously because the regulatory standard and the physiological response are measured through different filters — one electronic, one biological.

Iryna Myshchenko, Vitalii Nazarenko, and colleagues at Wroclaw University of Science and Technology and the Kundiev Institute for Occupational Health, publishing in the Ukrainian Journal of Occupational Health in 2021, documented a compounding mechanism. Building structures attenuate sound differently at different frequencies: 8 dB at 31.5 Hz versus 14-16 dB at 250 Hz. Workers inside a building near a low-frequency source experience proportionally more low-frequency energy than the outdoor measurement suggests. The A-weighted indoor reading further attenuates the low-frequency component that was already over-represented relative to outdoor measurement. The result is double attenuation — architectural and electronic — applied selectively to the frequency range that A-weighting was already suppressing. A 2023 NIOSH case study confirmed the mechanism: when measured in one-third-octave bands, a building's low-frequency environment exceeded German, Dutch, and Polish noise guidelines at 20, 25, and 50-250 Hz. The A-weighted summary reading: compliant.

The Scandinavian exceptions prove the structural nature of the problem. Denmark requires separate assessment of noise in the 10-160 Hz band. Germany uses a C-minus-A criterion: when the difference between C-weighted and A-weighted measurements exceeds 20 dB, low-frequency noise investigation is triggered. The United Kingdom developed the Moorhouse-Waddington criteria for one-third-octave assessment at 10-160 Hz. These alternatives exist because the standard approach failed for low-frequency sources — the complaints persisted at levels below the dBA limits, and the alternative frameworks were developed specifically to capture what A-weighting misses. But these are exceptions. The global default is dBA.

The concealment mechanism here connects to but extends the pattern of the previous three entries. In #234 (soil pH), the protocol determines the number — different electrolytes produce different pH values for the same soil. In #235 (salivary cortisol), the cross-reactivity becomes dominant when the matrix inverts. In #236 (breath alcohol), the proxy is converted to blood units by a fixed ratio that varies two-fold across the population. Here, the filter is calibrated to a perceptual model — what the average ear reportedly hears at quiet levels — and applied as though perception and harm are the same thing. The conflation is precise and structural: the filter was designed to answer the question "how loud does this sound?" and is deployed to answer the question "how harmful is this noise?" The two questions have different answers below 200 Hz, where the ear's insensitivity to loudness does not predict the body's insensitivity to exposure. Low-frequency sound that the ear barely perceives still vibrates the chest, disrupts sleep, and triggers cardiovascular stress responses. The filter removes it from the measurement. The body does not remove it from the experience.

The filter is infrastructure. It is in every compliant sound level meter, every noise ordinance, every environmental impact assessment, every occupational health standard. The perception model was revised in 2003. The filter was not. The cost of updating the filter — recalibrating instruments, revising regulations, reopening noise permits, re-measuring every site that was declared compliant — exceeds the distributed cost of the error. The error is carried by millions of workers and residents whose low-frequency exposure exceeds physiological thresholds while satisfying regulatory ones. The meter displays a number. The number has passed through a filter shaped by a 1930s perception experiment applied at the wrong level in the wrong context for the wrong question. The number is precise. The number is what survived the filter. What the filter removed is not on the display.
